<p class="lead">A finding of unfair dismissal doesn't automatically produce a payout. The Commission must first ask whether the person should get their job back — and only if that's inappropriate does it move to compensation, which is calculated, capped and can be reduced to nothing.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain why reinstatement, not compensation, is the Act's starting point — and when that primacy actually gives way.
- Work through the s.392(2) compensation factors in the order the Commission actually uses them, including the cap and the mandatory misconduct reduction.
- Recognise the practical ways a remedy can end up smaller than expected — or at nil — even where the dismissal was unfair.

## The gateway: s.390

Before any remedy is available at all, the Commission must be satisfied of two things: that the person was protected from unfair dismissal, and that they were unfairly dismissed <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[113]" phrase="the FWC is satisfied that the person was protected from unfair dismissal" />. Even then, making an order is discretionary, not automatic — the Full Bench has described the question of whether to order *any* remedy once unfairness is found as "remain[ing] a discretionary one" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2050" at="[47]" phrase="remains a discretionary one" />, and whether the applicant has actually suffered financial loss can bear on that discretion <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2050" at="[47]" phrase="whether an applicant has suffered financial loss may be a relevant" />.

Critically, s.390(3) builds in a sequence: the Commission **must not** order compensation unless it is first satisfied that reinstatement is inappropriate <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[113]" phrase="the FWC is satisfied that reinstatement of the person is inappropriate" />. Compensation is never a free first choice.

## Reinstatement comes first — s.391

The Act's object provisions give reinstatement primacy among remedies. As one decision put it, reading the objects clause, "it is clear the legislature intended to give primacy to reinstatement in circumstances where an employee has been unfairly dismissed" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[122]" phrase="it is clear the legislature intended to give primacy to reinstatement" />. That primacy has a procedural bite: a Full Bench has held that the requirement to find reinstatement inappropriate before considering compensation "does not provide licence to search for a reason to order an employee's reinstatement when that is not appropriate" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[123]" phrase="does not provide licence to search for a reason to order an employee's reinstatement when that is not appropriate" /> — in other words, primacy means *consider it properly first*, not *find a way to grant it regardless*.

An order for reinstatement can come with two further orders under s.391(2)-(3): one to maintain continuity of employment and service, and one to restore remuneration lost because of the dismissal. Neither is automatic — both are expressed as orders the Commission "may" make if it "considers it appropriate to do so" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[129]" phrase="the continuity of the person's employment" />, and an order to restore lost pay in particular is a genuinely discretionary exercise: the Commission may decline it, or make only a partial order, where the employee engaged in misconduct <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[133]" phrase="an order to restore lost pay does not necessarily follow an order for reinstatement" />.

### Is reinstatement appropriate? The loss of trust and confidence test

The most common argument against reinstatement is that trust and confidence between the parties has broken down to the point the relationship can't be restored. The test, quoted and applied across decisions, comes originally from *Perkins v Grace Worldwide (Aust) Pty Ltd* (1997) 72 IR 186 — not read in the original for this guide, but quoted at length in the decisions below. The core proposition: "trust and confidence is a necessary ingredient in any employment relationship" and a loss of it is relevant to reinstatement's practicability only "provided that such loss of trust and confidence is soundly and rationally based" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[124]" phrase="trust and confidence is a necessary ingredient in any employment relationship" />.

That test isn't a rubber stamp for the employer's say-so. A Full Bench has distilled several guardrails:

<div class="key">
<h3>Loss of trust and confidence — the guardrails</h3>
<ol class="principles">
<li>It's a relevant consideration, but not the sole or necessary one, in deciding whether reinstatement is appropriate.</li>
<li>Each case turns on its own facts; most employment relationships can withstand some friction.</li>
<li>The claim must be soundly and rationally based, and the onus is on the party asserting it.</li>
<li>An employer's reluctance to shift its view of the employee's guilt, despite the Commission's contrary findings, is not itself a sound basis for the claim.</li>
<li>Difficulty or embarrassment in re-employing someone the employer believed guilty of wrongdoing doesn't, on its own, show a loss of trust and confidence.</li>
</ol>
</div>

Those five points are drawn from a Full Bench decision discussing *Perkins*, quoted in <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[125]" phrase="the onus of establishing a loss of trust and confidence rests on the party making the assertion" /> — itself not read in the original for this guide and flagged below. A related Full Bench authority on the same question (also flagged) makes the point sharply: dismissing someone for misconduct nearly always produces *some* loss of trust on the employer's part, but "that question cannot be answered solely by reference to the views of management witnesses. All of the circumstances should be taken into account" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[126]" phrase="that question cannot be answered solely by reference to the views of management witnesses" />.

**Applied — reinstatement ordered.** A university lecturer dismissed for performance was reinstated. The Commission found little evidence the relationship was "irretrievably broken, or so untenable as to serve as an impediment to her reinstatement" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[127]" phrase="irretrievably broken, or so untenable as to serve as an impediment to her reinstatement" />, and concluded reinstatement was appropriate <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[128]" phrase="it is appropriate that she be reinstated, and I intend to do so" />. The Commission went on to order reinstatement, an unbroken continuity of service, and lost remuneration <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[137]" phrase="Dr Lucy Zhao's continuity of service shall not be taken to have been broken by her dismissal" />. Permission to appeal that outcome was later refused by a Full Bench majority <Cite id="[2020] FWCFB 3571" at="[66]" phrase="Permission to appeal is refused" />, so it stood.

**Applied — reinstatement refused on genuine loss of trust.** Contrast that with a two-person medical imaging clinic where both the employee and the practice owner agreed their relationship had broken down. The Commission found "the degree of bitterness between the parties and the plethora of accusatory statements made, rendered reinstatement inappropriate" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4840" at="[3]" phrase="the degree of bitterness between the parties and the plethora of accusatory statements made, rendered reinstatement inappropriate" />, noting both sides' "level of tolerance for the other is zero" before concluding reinstatement was "clearly not appropriate" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4840" at="[8]" phrase="Reinstatement is clearly not appropriate" />. Even so, the decision was careful to record that reinstatement is only a "presumptive" remedy, not an entitlement: "there is no right to reinstatement consequent upon a finding that a person has been unfairly dismissed" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4840" at="[7]" phrase="there is no right to reinstatement consequent upon a finding that a person has been unfairly dismissed" />.

**Reinstatement can also fail for reasons that have nothing to do with "trust and confidence."** In one matter the applicant had already found other work and the Commission simply wasn't satisfied the employer would treat him appropriately if he returned, concluding it was "inappropriate to order reinstatement" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[118]" phrase="I am satisfied that it is inappropriate to order reinstatement" /> without needing the full loss-of-trust framework. In another, reinstatement failed because the applicant hadn't sought it, had already started a new job, and the valid reason for dismissal was threats of violence against people who still worked there <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2050" at="[46]" phrase="reinstatement would not be appropriate" />.

## Compensation — s.392

Once reinstatement is ruled out, compensation becomes available — but only if the Commission also considers it appropriate in all the circumstances, and only calculated through the factors in s.392(2):

<div class="key">
<h3>s.392(2) — what the Commission must take into account</h3>
<ol class="principles">
<li>The effect of the order on the viability of the employer's enterprise.</li>
<li>The length of the person's service.</li>
<li>The remuneration the person would have received, or would likely have received, had they not been dismissed.</li>
<li>The person's efforts (if any) to mitigate their loss.</li>
<li>Remuneration earned since the dismissal, up to the date of the compensation order.</li>
<li>Income reasonably likely to be earned between the order and the actual payment.</li>
<li>Any other matter the Commission considers relevant.</li>
</ol>
</div>

This is usually applied through what's known as the Sprigg formula, named for a 1998 AIRC Full Bench decision that has not been read in the original for this guide. A Full Bench has described it as "the well-established approach to the assessment of compensation under s.392", with its first step being to work out what the applicant would have received had they not been dismissed — the "Anticipated Period of Employment" <Cite id="[2021] FWCFB 280" at="[32]" phrase="the well-established approach to the assessment of compensation under s.392 is to apply the 'Sprigg Formula'" />. That first step is unavoidably speculative, as the Full Bench that first articulated it acknowledged: "there is a speculative element involved in all such assessments… assessment of relative likelihoods is integral to most assessments of compensation or damages in courts of law" <Cite id="[2021] FWCFB 280" at="[33]" phrase="there is a speculative element involved in all such assessments" />.

### A full worked example

One decision walks through every s.392(2) factor against a dismissed 18-year-old casual worker, and is worth following step by step:

- **Viability.** No evidence an award would affect the employer's viability <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[123]" phrase="an award of compensation would affect the viability of the Respondent's enterprise" />.
- **Length of service.** "Just over six months' service. This is not a long period of time." <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[124]" phrase="This is not a long period of time" />
- **Anticipated remuneration.** The Commission found he would likely have continued for six weeks, calculated as 38 hours × $28.01 × 6 weeks = $6,386.28 <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[127]" phrase="I am satisfied that Mr Lee would have been likely to continue in his employment for a period of six weeks" />.
- **Mitigation.** Not satisfied he'd made "strident efforts" to find other work, so one week was deducted <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[128]" phrase="I am not satisfied that Mr Lee made strident efforts to mitigate the loss suffered" />.
- **Earned since dismissal / likely to earn.** Nothing earned in the relevant period, and the forward-looking factor wasn't relevant on these facts <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[130]" phrase="Mr Lee did not earn any remuneration within the six weeks" />.
- **Misconduct (s.392(3)).** None found, so no reduction <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[135]" phrase="I do not find that Mr Lee engaged in any misconduct that would reduce the amount to be awarded to him" />.
- **Shock, distress or humiliation (s.392(4)).** Expressly excluded from the amount <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[136]" phrase="does not include a component by way of compensation for shock, distress or humiliation" />.
- **Compensation cap (s.392(5)-(6)).** The high income threshold was $162,000 (half of that is $81,000) — comfortably above the amount assessed, so the cap didn't bite <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[138]" phrase="The high income threshold immediately prior to the dismissal was $162,000" />.
- **Order.** $6,386.28 less the one-week mitigation deduction of $1,064.38, leaving $5,321.90 plus superannuation, payable within 14 days <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[141]" phrase="The Respondent is to pay to Mr Lee the following amounts within 14 days" />.

### The cap and the statutory language

The cap itself is set directly by the statute: compensation must not exceed the lesser of a specified 26-week remuneration figure and half the high income threshold immediately before dismissal, and the amount ordered must not include anything for "shock, distress or humiliation, or other analogous hurt" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2900" at="[120]" phrase="must not include a component by way of compensation for shock, distress or humiliation" />. A separate decision frames the underlying purpose plainly: compensation exists "to provide a person who has been unfairly dismissed with reparation for losses reasonably attributable to the unfair dismissal. It is not intended to be punitive." <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4840" at="[9]" phrase="to provide a person who has been unfairly dismissed with reparation for losses reasonably attributable to the unfair dismissal" />

## How remedy claims fall short

Three different mechanisms can take an otherwise-calculable compensation amount down to little or nothing. They are not interchangeable, and advisers should be precise about which one is actually in play.

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Point of principle</span>
<h3>Won the case, got nothing</h3>
<p>A dismissal was found unfair for lack of process and personal circumstances, but the applicant's own conduct — threatening a director — had contributed to it. The Commission found compensation "appropriate... but that the compensation amount is nil", expressly because that conduct "contributed to his dismissal". <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2050" at="[48]" phrase="it is appropriate to order compensation, but that the compensation amount is nil" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A finding of unfair dismissal and a finding that the employee's own conduct contributed to it can coexist. s.392(3) is mandatory once that contribution is found — the Commission <em>must</em> reduce the amount, and that reduction can go all the way to zero.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Point of principle</span>
<h3>Nil at first instance, corrected to $7,029.90 on appeal</h3>
<p>A decision found reinstatement inappropriate for genuine loss of trust and confidence, assessed a 6-week anticipated employment period, and then <em>deducted the applicant's entire post-dismissal earnings</em> from that period — producing nil. On appeal, the Full Bench found this misapplied the Sprigg formula: she'd found new work (at a lower rate) only partway through the 6 weeks, so the proper approach was to offset only the <em>shortfall</em> between what she would have earned and what she actually earned, not her full new earnings against the full anticipated period. <Cite id="[2021] FWCFB 280" at="[34]" phrase="misapplied the Sprigg Formula by deducting all of the amount of monies earned since termination from the amount she had determined as the Anticipated Period of Employment" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Mitigation only offsets the actual shortfall in income during the overlapping period, not the applicant's gross alternative earnings against the full assessed period. Getting the arithmetic right is not a formality — it decided the difference between nil and over $7,000 here.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Point of principle</span>
<h3>Reinstatement isn't only about trust and confidence</h3>
<p>Reinstatement was refused not because of any breakdown of trust, but because the applicant hadn't asked for it, had already started new employment, and the underlying valid reason was threats of violence against people still working there. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2050" at="[46]" phrase="reinstatement would not be appropriate" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Don't assume a reinstatement argument always has to run through the Perkins "loss of trust and confidence" framework. Practical facts — a new job already started, safety to remaining staff — can settle the question without it.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the warehouse supervisor

<Scenario questions={["Is reinstatement likely to be appropriate here?", "If not, what would the compensation calculation look like?", "What's the biggest risk to the employer's position on remedy?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Fictional scenario. Elena has worked as a shift supervisor at a regional logistics warehouse for four years. She was dismissed last month after a client complained about a delivery error; the Commission has just found the dismissal unfair because Elena was never told about the complaint and was given no chance to respond before being let go — the error itself was, on the evidence, largely the client's own data mistake.</p>
    <p>Elena says she wants her job back and has not sought other work. The warehouse's general manager says the team has "moved on" and would find it awkward to have her back, but accepts under questioning that nobody has said anything hostile about her personally, and no one disputes she was a reliable and well-regarded supervisor before this incident. Elena was on $72,000 a year plus a vehicle allowance. Since her dismissal (six weeks ago), she has done some reading about applying for roles but has not yet submitted any applications.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Reinstatement:</strong> On these facts it looks likely to be appropriate. There's no soundly or rationally based loss of trust and confidence — "awkwardness" and the team having "moved on" are the kind of discomfort the authorities say isn't enough on its own. No safety issue, no new job already started, and the applicant wants reinstatement. The employer would need something more concrete — a genuine, evidenced breakdown in the working relationship — to displace the Act's starting preference for giving her the job back.</li>
      <li><strong>If reinstatement were nonetheless found inappropriate:</strong> The anticipated employment period would likely run well beyond six weeks given four years' clean service and no performance issues — this isn't a short-tenure case like the casual worker example above. Her very limited mitigation efforts (six weeks without a single application) would likely support some deduction, though probably modest given how recently the dismissal occurred. No misconduct on her part to trigger s.392(3). No shock/distress component. The cap (half the high income threshold) is unlikely to bind at her salary level over a realistic anticipated period.</li>
      <li><strong>Biggest risk to the employer:</strong> Treating "the team would find it awkward" as if it were a loss-of-trust-and-confidence case. Awkwardness isn't the test — a soundly and rationally based breakdown is. Without more, this risks reinstatement being ordered regardless of the employer's preference, with continuity of service and lost pay orders likely to follow.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what further evidence shows:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Nothing more than managerial discomfort: reinstatement ordered, with continuity of service and lost remuneration.</div>
      <div class="lo">Genuine evidence of a specific, serious breakdown (not yet on the facts given): reinstatement refused, compensation calculated over a lengthy anticipated period given her service and clean record.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
