<p class="lead">Dismissing without notice is the exception, not the default — and the bar for it is set by a regulation, not by how angry the employer is.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain when an employer must give notice (or pay in lieu) of termination, and when it doesn't have to.
- Apply the Regulation 1.07 definition of "serious misconduct", and tell it apart from the s.387(a) valid reason test.
- Spot the pattern where a summary dismissal survives as a valid reason but still costs the employer a notice payment.

## The notice obligation

The default position is that an employer must not end someone's employment without giving notice, or paying them in lieu of it. The obligation to pay notice is outlined in s.117 of the Act <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1273" at="[46]" phrase="The obligation to pay notice is outlined in s.117 of the Act" />. That default applies whatever the employer thinks of the employee's conduct — it only falls away if a specific exception applies.

## The summary dismissal exception

Serious misconduct is one of those exceptions. Section 123(1)(b) of the Act provides that an employer is not required to pay notice to an employee whose employment is terminated because of serious misconduct <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1273" at="[46]" phrase="Section 123(1)(b) of the Act provides that an employer is not required to pay notice to an employee whose employment is terminated because of serious misconduct" />.

"Serious misconduct" isn't whatever the employer chooses to call it. It's defined by **Fair Work Regulation 1.07**, and it includes:

- wilful or deliberate behaviour that is inconsistent with the continuation of the contract of employment, and
- conduct that causes serious and imminent risk to the health or safety of a person, or to the reputation, viability or profitability of the employer's business

<Cite id="[2024] FWC 2513" at="[51]" phrase="wilful or deliberate behaviour that is inconsistent with the continuation of the contract of employment" /> <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2513" at="[51]" phrase="serious and imminent risk to: (i) the health or safety of a person; or (ii) the reputation, viability or profitability of the employer's business" />.

The regulation also lists specific conduct — theft, fraud, assault, sexual harassment, intoxication at work, refusing a lawful and reasonable instruction — as examples that will meet the standard on their own.

## Don't conflate this with the valid reason test

This is the trap Module 5 (Valid reason) already flagged. The s.387(a) valid reason test and the reg 1.07 serious misconduct test are **not the same test**, and meeting one doesn't require meeting the other. As the Full Bench principles put it: the criterion for a valid reason is not whether serious misconduct as defined in reg 1.07 has occurred, since reg 1.07 has no application to s 387(a) <Cite id="[2023] FWC 65" at="[36]" phrase="reg 1.07 has no application to s 387(a)" />.

In practice this cuts both ways:

- Conduct can be a **valid reason** for dismissal under s.387(a) without being serious enough to justify summary dismissal under reg 1.07 — in which case the employee can still lose the unfair dismissal case on valid reason, while the employer separately owes notice.
- Conduct that meets the reg 1.07 bar will almost always also be a valid reason, but that's a consequence of how serious reg 1.07's own threshold is — not because the two tests ask the same question.

## What the Commission looks for in applying reg 1.07

- **Deliberateness, not just seriousness.** A considered, repeated refusal to follow a lawful and reasonable direction can be conduct that is inconsistent with the continuation of the contract of employment under reg 1.07(2)(a), even without dishonesty or violence <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1273" at="[47]" phrase="inconsistent with the continuation of his contract of employment" />.
- **Gravity and context.** Whether conduct poses a serious and imminent risk to reputation or safety is weighed against factors like the seriousness of what was alleged and the seniority of the people involved <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2513" at="[51]" phrase="the seniority of the persons against whom those allegations were made" />.
- **Deliberate is not automatically enough.** Conduct can be deliberate and still fall short of reg 1.07 if it doesn't breach a clear policy and doesn't show the kind of calculated disregard the regulation is aimed at <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6693" at="[48]" phrase="did not constitute serious misconduct" />.

## The practical risk of getting it wrong

Summarily dismissing someone for conduct that turns out not to meet the reg 1.07 bar doesn't just risk losing on valid reason — it can turn an otherwise defensible dismissal into an unfair one, for no reason other than the missing notice.

In one matter, the Commission found the employee's conduct was serious enough to be a valid reason for dismissal, but not serious enough to meet reg 1.07 — so Mr Chebbo should have been dismissed on notice, and the decision to dismiss him summarily was harsh <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6693" at="[48]" phrase="Mr Chebbo should have been dismissed on notice" />. Because that was the only unfairness in the case, compensation was confined to the decision not to terminate on notice <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6693" at="[49]" phrase="confined to the decision not to terminate on notice" /> — in that matter, four weeks' pay.

**The adviser's takeaway:** if the reg 1.07 bar is genuinely in doubt, paying notice and dismissing for conduct anyway is usually the safer default. The employer still loses the employee. It just doesn't compound a conduct dispute with an avoidable notice claim.

## How summary dismissals fail

<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>Deliberate, but not serious misconduct</h3>
<p>Dismissed on the spot for attending a protest in breach of a stay-at-home direction. The conduct was deliberate and put the employer's reputation at risk — but didn't meet reg 1.07. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6693" at="[48]" phrase="Mr Chebbo's conduct did not constitute serious misconduct" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Deliberate isn't the same as "serious misconduct". If it's close, pay the notice.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>No valid reason, and no notice</h3>
<p>Dismissed with immediate effect over a disputed "resignation" that the Commission found never happened — with no notice period given on top of no valid reason at all. <Cite id="[2024] FWC 913" at="[95]" phrase="dismissed with immediate effect and was not provided any notice period" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A weak conduct case and a missing notice period compound each other — don't let both happen at once.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The label was too strong for the facts</h3>
<p>A seven-year employee with performance difficulties was summarily dismissed for "serious misconduct" where the facts, at most, warranted a review, a caution and some coaching. <Cite id="[2024] FWC 3291" at="[72]" phrase="was a disproportionate response to the matters that had arisen in the Respondent's workplace" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Reach for "serious misconduct" only when the facts actually clear the reg 1.07 bar — not because it's the stronger-sounding label.</p>
</div>

## Adviser checklist

Work through this before recommending a dismissal without notice. If the reg 1.07 limb can't be named and evidenced, pay notice instead.

## Practice scenario: the loading dock

<Scenario questions={["Does this meet the reg 1.07 bar for summary dismissal?", "What should happen to notice while that's being worked out?", "What's missing before deciding?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>This is a fictional scenario. Priya is a senior chef at a mid-sized restaurant group, four years' service, no prior warnings. During a delivery, she had a loud, heated argument with an external supplier's driver over a late and partly spoiled order, in front of two junior kitchen staff. No physical contact occurred, but Priya swore at the driver and told him to "get out before I throw you out." The driver complained to the restaurant's head office the same afternoon.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>7:40am</span><b>Delivery arrives late, partially spoiled</b>
      <span>7:52am</span><b>Argument in loading dock, overheard by two kitchen hands</b>
      <span>9:15am</span><b>Supplier's formal complaint received by head office</b>
    </div>
    <p>The General Manager wants Priya dismissed that afternoon for "serious misconduct", without notice, citing the risk to the supplier relationship. Priya hasn't been asked for her account yet.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Not on the facts available yet.</strong> The outburst was deliberate in the sense that Priya chose her words, but deliberate rudeness toward one supplier's driver is a long way from the examples reg 1.07 gives (theft, fraud, assault, intoxication) and isn't obviously "serious and imminent risk" to the business — one complaint about one driver isn't yet evidence the supplier relationship itself is at risk, let alone imminently.</li>
      <li><strong>Notice:</strong> unless further facts emerge that clearly meet the reg 1.07 bar, the safer course is to stand Priya down (on pay) while the complaint is investigated, then decide between a disciplinary outcome short of dismissal, or dismissal on notice — not a same-day summary dismissal.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> Priya's account of what was said and why; whether the driver's conduct (a late, spoiled delivery) provoked the exchange; whether the supplier has actually threatened to end the relationship, or whether that's the GM's assumption; any history of similar conduct from Priya; and what the restaurant's own conduct policy says about dealing with external contractors.</li>
    </ol>
    <p style="margin-bottom:6px"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">A documented pattern of threats or abuse toward contractors, after a clear policy and prior warning: closer to reg 1.07, summary dismissal more defensible.</div>
      <div class="lo">A one-off, provoked outburst with no real commercial fallout: at most a warning, or dismissal on notice if the relationship with Priya has broken down — not summary dismissal.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
