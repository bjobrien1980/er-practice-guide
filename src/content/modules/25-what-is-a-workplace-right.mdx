<p class="lead">A workplace-rights claim under s.340 starts with a workplace right. If there isn't one, there is nothing for the reverse onus to attach to. This module is about what counts: the three kinds of workplace right in s.341, what makes something a complaint or inquiry, and where the edges are.</p>

<div class="notice">
<strong>Current-source gate:</strong> the current text of ss.340 and 341 of the Fair Work Act and the definition of "workplace law" in s.12 were checked on 14 November 2026. The meaning of "able to make a complaint or inquiry" has divided the Federal Court, and this module records the position in the decisions cited. The High Court's 2023 decision in *Qantas v TWU* has now been read directly and is cited in the future-exercise discussion. The Federal Court decisions were read from the judgment files held in the Briefer corpus, not from the Court's website — see the note under Decisions cited.

## What you'll be able to do

- Sort an employee's claimed workplace right into the correct limb of s.341(1), and say what each limb requires.
- Tell a complaint or inquiry apart from a request for help.
- Explain what "able to make" a complaint means, where the courts disagree, and why the disagreement rarely changes the advice.
- Spot the people the complaint limb does not cover, and the rights that are protected before they exist.

## Three kinds of workplace right

[Module 2](/modules/general-protections) introduces s.340. Section 341(1) defines what it protects. A person has a workplace right if the person:

- **(a)** is entitled to the benefit of, or has a role or responsibility under, a workplace law, a workplace instrument or an order of an industrial body;
- **(b)** is able to initiate, or participate in, a process or proceedings under a workplace law or workplace instrument; or
- **(c)** is able to make a complaint or inquiry — either **(i)** to a person or body that can seek compliance with a workplace law or instrument, or **(ii)** if the person is an employee, in relation to their employment.

The first three of these — (a), (b) and (c)(i) — are tied to workplace laws and workplace instruments. The last is not. As a Full Court majority put it, the rights in (a), (b) and (c)(i) are "confined to rights, roles and responsibilities under, or arising from, workplace laws and workplace instruments", and (c)(ii) "is not so confined" <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 15" at="[16]" phrase="is not so confined" />. That is why most disputes about whether a workplace right exists are disputes about (c)(ii).

The protection is wide, but it is not unlimited: "Although the protection is broad in its scope, it has its limits" <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 15" at="[10]" phrase="Although the protection is broad in its scope, it has its limits" />.

## Benefits, roles and responsibilities: s.341(1)(a)

**Benefits.** An award rate, a leave entitlement under the National Employment Standards and a classification under an enterprise agreement are all benefits under a workplace law or instrument. The practical requirement is that the employee can say which law or instrument, and which entitlement. In one Commission case an employee alleged pay irregularities and missed breaks but "has not pointed to the workplace law, workplace instrument or order made by an industrial body" behind them <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3124" at="[57]" phrase="has not pointed to the workplace law, workplace instrument or order made by an industrial body" />. No workplace right was established under any limb.

"Workplace law" reaches beyond the Fair Work Act. The same decision accepted that lodging a workers compensation claim "is likely to be regarded as having or exercising a workplace right" <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3124" at="[58]" phrase="is likely to be regarded as having or exercising a workplace right" />. Check s.12 for the definition; it includes laws dealing with work health and safety.

**Roles and responsibilities.** This part of the limb protects people because of what they do under a workplace law, not what they are owed. The standard example is a health and safety representative <Cite id="[2018] FCAFC 191" at="[72]" phrase="The Explanatory Memorandum gives the illustration of the workplace health and safety representative" />. But the words are not limited to representatives. In *Auimatagi*, a Full Court held that a head contractor enforcing a site safety rule had a "role or responsibility" under work health and safety law: "Its responsibility is not just a legal duty" <Cite id="[2018] FCAFC 191" at="[72]" phrase="Its responsibility is not just a legal duty" />.

Two things follow for advisers. First, a supervisor or manager carrying out a statutory safety duty may themselves hold a workplace right. Second, an employer can hold one too, which matters when a union or another business takes action against the employer for enforcing a safety requirement.

## Processes and proceedings: s.341(1)(b)

An employee who is able to start or take part in a process under a workplace law or instrument has a workplace right. Section 341(2) identifies examples of processes and proceedings, including Fair Work Commission and court processes, protected industrial action and processes connected with making or varying workplace instruments. The examples do not remove the need to ask whether this person was entitled or able to initiate or participate in the particular process. The ones advisers meet most often are Commission applications, an enterprise agreement's dispute procedure, bargaining and protected industrial action: identify the workplace law or instrument and the source of the person's ability in each case.

The usual trap is timing. An employee who has told the employer they intend to file a Commission application, or who has just used the dispute procedure, holds a workplace right under this limb whether or not they have also "complained".

## Complaints and inquiries: s.341(1)(c)

This is the limb most claims rely on. It has four questions.

### Who made it, and to whom?

Limb (c)(ii) — a complaint or inquiry "in relation to his or her employment" — is available only to employees. In *Azad*, a doctor engaged as an independent contractor could not use it, because the word "employee" has its ordinary meaning and "does not include an independent contractor" <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1650" at="[66]" phrase="does not include an independent contractor" />.

A contractor can still rely on limb (c)(i), but only for a complaint or inquiry made to a person or body able to seek compliance with a workplace law. The Fair Work Ombudsman and a union are the examples given in the explanatory material, and the judge in *Azad* found "no indication in the Explanatory Memorandum that employers are a relevant person or body" <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1650" at="[118]" phrase="there is no indication in the Explanatory Memorandum that employers are a relevant person or body" />. On the facts and reasoning in *Azad*, a contractor's complaint made only to the principal was unlikely to qualify under limb (c). Do not treat that as a universal rule: identify the relevant law, instrument and recipient, and analyse limbs (a) and (b) separately. The application in that case was dismissed <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1650" at="[265]" phrase="the application is dismissed" />.

For employees, a complaint to the employer itself is enough. The Full Court in *Alam* recorded as accepted that limb (c) is not confined to external authorities, but includes "complaints or inquiries made to the employer itself" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 178" at="[65]" phrase="include complaints or inquiries made to the employer itself" />.

Under limb (c)(i), the body complained to does not have to be able to fix that particular complaint. In a Commission case, an employee threatened to go to the Commission or the Ombudsman about something neither could have acted on. That "does not remove the making of a complaint or inquiry from the protection" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 245" at="[161]" phrase="does not remove the making of a complaint or inquiry from the protection" />.

### Was it a complaint or inquiry at all?

A complaint is "an expression of discontent which seeks consideration, redress or relief" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 178" at="[59]" phrase="an expression of discontent which seeks consideration, redress or relief" />. No particular words are needed, and the employee does not have to call it a complaint. The question "is to be determined as a matter of substance, and not of form" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 178" at="[59]" phrase="is to be determined as a matter of substance, and not of form" />.

What falls short is a plain request. In *Alam*, a new employee asking her manager to help set up a system account, to arrange a letter of authority and to schedule a meeting was making requests, and each "should be characterised as a request and not as a" complaint or inquiry <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 178" at="[61]" phrase="should be characterised as a request and not as a" />. The Commission reached the same view of an email that was "a mere request for assistance by the Applicant" in doing her job <Cite id="[2023] FWC 245" at="[156]" phrase="a mere request for assistance by the Applicant" />.

"I'm not making a complaint, but…" is harder. In *Alam*, a communication in which the employee told her manager she did not need to make a complaint could not "reasonably be characterised as a complaint or inquiry of the requisite kind" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 178" at="[62]" phrase="cannot reasonably be characterised as a complaint or inquiry of the requisite kind" />. But a passage from *Shea v TRUenergy*, quoted in the Commission, says a grievance can still be a complaint "despite an express disavowal of any intention to complain" if a reasonable observer would conclude the employee wanted it considered or acted on <Cite id="[2024] FWC 840" at="[23]" phrase="despite an express disavowal of any intention to complain" />. Do not treat a disclaimer as settling the question either way. Look at what the employee wanted to happen.

An inquiry is protected in its own right. An employee who asked to use his study-leave entitlement, and about payment for it, exercised a workplace right; the request was "an expression of grievance and inquiry that the Respondent should take notice of and rectify" <Cite id="[2024] FWC 840" at="[24]" phrase="an expression of grievance and inquiry that the Respondent" />.

### Was the employee "able to" make it?

This is the contested question. The words "is able to make a complaint or inquiry" limit the right, and the Full Court has said the meaning "has been the subject of divergent views in this Court" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 178" at="[65]" phrase="has been the subject of divergent views in this Court" />.

<div class="key">
<h3>Three positions on "able to"</h3>
<ol class="principles">
<li><strong><em>Whelan</em> (Full Court, 2019).</strong> An employee whose contract contemplated a discretionary bonus and a bonus plan complained that neither had been dealt with. "He was entitled to make complaint or inquiry on these subjects" <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 16" at="[28]" phrase="He was entitled to make complaint or inquiry on these subjects" />. The Court approved the proposition that a complaint must be founded on a source of entitlement, whether in an instrument or otherwise.</li>
<li><strong><em>PIA Mortgage v King</em> (Full Court majority, 2020).</strong> There must be a right to make the complaint, and "there must be an identifiable source of that entitlement or right" <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 15" at="[14]" phrase="there must be an identifiable source of that entitlement or right" />. But the sources are wide: "legislative provisions that are not workplace laws, contractual terms providing a right to make complaints and the general law" <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 15" at="[16]" phrase="legislative provisions that are not workplace laws, contractual terms providing a right to make complaints and the general law" />.</li>
<li><strong><em>Cummins v Keenan</em> (Bromberg J, Mortimer J agreeing, 2020).</strong> No such requirement exists at all: "there is no textual or contextual basis for construing s 341(1)(c)(ii) as requiring a complaint or inquiry to be underpinned by a right or entitlement" <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 204" at="[64]" phrase="there is no textual or contextual basis for construing s 341(1)(c)(ii) as requiring a complaint or inquiry to be underpinned by a right or entitlement" />. This view was not needed to decide that appeal <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 204" at="[67]" phrase="it is not necessary to do so for the disposition of the appeal" />.</li>
</ol>
</div>

The *PIA Mortgage* majority shows how little the requirement excludes in practice. Because an employee can sue for breach of contract, and "A suit may be regarded as the ultimate form of complaint" <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 15" at="[19]" phrase="A suit may be regarded as the ultimate form of complaint" />, an employee is able to complain to the employer about an alleged breach of the employment contract. An employee who alleges that the employer has breached a statute relating to the employment is also able to complain about it, "whether or not the statute directly provides a right to sue or make a complaint" <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 15" at="[20]" phrase="whether or not the statute directly provides a right to sue or make a complaint" />. In that case, an executive's email and his solicitors' letter of demand about a threatened early termination were the exercise of a workplace right <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 15" at="[32]" phrase="the trial judge was correct to hold that Mr King had exercised a" />.

In *Alam* (2021), the Full Court was asked to choose between these approaches. It declined to adopt Bromberg J's view, held that "this Court should proceed on the understanding of s 341(1)(c) indicated by the unanimous decision of the Full Court in Whelan" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 178" at="[97]" phrase="this Court should proceed on the understanding of s 341(1)(c) indicated by the unanimous decision of the Full Court in Whelan" />, and doubted that the *PIA Mortgage* approach "will produce different outcomes in practice" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 178" at="[98]" phrase="will produce different outcomes in practice" />.

### After *Alam*

Two later Full Court decisions were read for this guide on 14 November 2026.

**_Jess v Cooloola Milk_ (2022).** A casual driver asked his employer whether he would be paid extra for additional work, and was dismissed soon after. The trial judge held that this was not a complaint or inquiry he was "able to make". The Full Court disagreed. McElwaine J, with whom the other judges generally agreed, said that in *Alam* <Cite id="[2022] FCAFC 75" at="[75]" phrase="this Court undertook a comprehensive analysis of the competing views" /> and concluded that *Whelan* is to be followed. On the facts the answer was simple: <Cite id="[2022] FCAFC 75" at="[74]" phrase="Objectively and reasonably understood, it was an inquiry in relation to the payment of overtime" />, and overtime was an award entitlement. The appeal was allowed and the case sent back.

**_CFMMEU v Quirk_ (2023).** This decision came after the High Court's judgment in *Qantas v TWU*, and two of the judges described what the High Court said about s.341(1)(c). The High Court decision is not held in the corpus, so what follows is the Full Court's account of it.

- Rares ACJ recorded that the High Court referred to *Alam*, *PIA Mortgage* and the other Federal Court decisions, but <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[42]" phrase="their Honours did not, or need to, resolve the differences between them" />.
- He recorded the plurality as holding that the words "is able to" are not words of limitation. They operate when <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[43]" phrase="circumstances have come into existence in which the person has a present capacity to exercise a relevant power or freedom" />.
- Katzmann J said the same of the plurality, and added that if the trial judge had treated the words as a limitation, <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[337]" phrase="it may be accepted that his Honour erred" />.
- Colvin J, who had sat in *Alam*, gave his own view that <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[548]" phrase="it is not necessary for there to be a demonstrated ability to make a complaint or inquiry to a particular body conferred by legal norms" />.

The employees in *Quirk* still lost on this ground, for a different reason. They were union officials who had criticised the union's management in the media. Katzmann J held that, however broadly "able to make" is read, those criticisms were not shown to be complaints "in relation to" their employment: <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[338]" phrase="The mere fact that the Federal Union was also their employer was not enough" />.

### Single judges since *Alam*

Single judges have kept applying the *Whelan* approach, including after the High Court's decision. The decisions below were read on this point on 14 November 2026.

- **The requirement is treated as settled.** In *Pigozzo v Mineral Resources* (2022), Feutrill J said <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1166" at="[44]" phrase="it is now settled that the unanimous decision in" /> *Whelan* is to be followed, citing *Alam* and *Jess*. In *Messenger v Commonwealth* (2022), Snaden J put it firmly: <Cite id="[2022] FCA 677" at="[150]" phrase="an employee must first demonstrate that it was made in the exercise of, or otherwise to protect or vindicate, some right or entitlement conferred upon them" />. A sense of grievance is not enough.
- **The High Court's statement has been noted, not treated as ending the requirement.** In *Dorsch v HEAD Oceania* (2024), Raper J summarised the plurality as saying that the words, while not words of limitation, <Cite id="[2024] FCA 162" at="[217]" phrase="necessarily indicate that circumstances have come into existence in which the person has a present capacity to exercise a relevant power or freedom" />.
- **The source is usually found.** In *Nuttall v Hatch* (2024), decided after the High Court's judgment, Hespe J still asked for a source: <Cite id="[2024] FCA 339" at="[180]" phrase="the right or entitlement to make the complaint or inquiry must be founded on a source of entitlement" />. For complaints that a senior executive was interfering with the employee's ability to do his job, the source <Cite id="[2024] FCA 339" at="[185]" phrase="is in the general law related to contracts" />, and also in the employer's grievance procedure.
- **Occasionally it is not.** In the same case, the employee's request that his manager support him against the executive was not an inquiry he was able to make, because the grievance procedure <Cite id="[2024] FCA 339" at="[188]" phrase="did not confer on Mr Nuttall an entitlement to request an advocate to assist him" />.
- **The argument is still being run.** In *AXR54 v Piper Alderman* (2025), the employer relied on *Messenger* to say that an employee's disputes with her supervisors' directions could not be complaints of the protected kind. Perram J, on a preliminary application, would not decide it: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1509" at="[24]" phrase="This may or may not well turn out to be a good point for the respondents at trial" />.

Where that leaves the law, on the decisions read: the Full Court in *Alam* and *Jess* requires a source of entitlement, in the wide sense that includes the contract, a policy, a statute and the general law. The High Court, as later judges describe it, has said "is able to" are not words of limitation but refer to a present capacity. Single judges have continued to look for a source and have nearly always found one. The limits that have actually defeated claims are narrower: a request that is not a complaint or inquiry at all, a complaint that is not about the person's own employment, and a request for something no instrument or law gives.

**What this means for advice.** Assume that a complaint or inquiry about pay, hours, leave, safety, bullying, the terms of the contract, or an alleged breach of any law that bears on the job is one the employee is able to make. An employer defence built on "there was no right to complain about that" remains available in principle, but it rarely succeeds and the law around it is not fully settled. It should not be the reason a decision goes ahead. If it is the only answer the employer has, escalate. More useful questions are whether the communication was a complaint or inquiry at all, and whether it was about the person's own employment.

### Does it have to be right, or reasonable?

No. A complaint can be wrong and still be protected. In the Commission case about leave accruals, the employee's complaint compared her entitlements with a colleague's in a way that made no legal sense: "Although such a complaint or inquiry is misconceived, it is a complaint or inquiry, nonetheless" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 245" at="[162]" phrase="Although such a complaint or inquiry is misconceived, it is a complaint or inquiry, nonetheless" />.

What is required is that it be genuine. The *PIA Mortgage* majority adopted the proposition that "the complaint must be made genuinely, in good faith and for a proper purpose" <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 15" at="[26]" phrase="the complaint must be made genuinely, in good faith and for a proper purpose" />. Treat an argument that a complaint was not genuine as a hard one to win. An employer that thinks a complaint is tactical should still deal with it on its merits and keep it apart from any decision about the employee.

## Rights that do not exist yet

Section 340(1)(b) prohibits adverse action taken to *prevent* the exercise of a workplace right. In *Qantas v TWU*, the airline outsourced its ground handling work at a time when the affected employees could not yet take protected industrial action. It argued that s.341 is written in the present tense, so the right had to exist when the action was taken. The Full Court rejected that. The provision protects "the future possible exercise of a particular workplace right" <Cite id="[2022] FCAFC 71" at="[99]" phrase="the future possible exercise of a particular workplace right" />, and its wording "does not support an implication that the workplace right must necessarily be present and existing" <Cite id="[2022] FCAFC 71" at="[100]" phrase="does not support an implication that the workplace right must necessarily be present and existing" />. Qantas's appeal was dismissed <Cite id="[2022] FCAFC 71" at="[431]" phrase="Both the Qantas appeal and TWU appeal must be dismissed" />.

Qantas took the point further, to the High Court. The High Court confirmed that s.340(1)(b) reaches adverse action taken for a substantial and operative reason of preventing an anticipated future exercise of a workplace right, not only a right already being exercised: the affected employees did not have the relevant rights at the time of the outsourcing decision, but were expected to be able to exercise them later <Cite id="[2023] HCA 27" at="[43]" phrase="would be able to exercise and would in fact exercise those workplace rights" /> <Cite id="[2023] HCA 27" at="[60]" phrase="regardless of whether that other person has the relevant workplace right at the time the adverse action is taken" />. This future-exercise principle does not answer the separate question whether a workplace right presently exists under s.341. A later Federal Court judgment also records that the appeal was dismissed <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[104]" phrase="An appeal was dismissed" />.

The practical lesson is about timing a restructure, an outsourcing or a roster change. "They can't do that yet" is not a safe answer if one reason for acting now is to stop employees doing it later.

## Prospective employees

Section 341(3) treats a prospective employee as having the workplace rights they would have if employed. Read the subsection and the exceptions that follow it before advising on a recruitment decision.

It does not turn every recruitment discussion into a workplace right. In a Commission case, a worker asked for changes to the employment contract he had been offered. The Deputy President held "there is no workplace right to have discussions or obtain clarification upon the terms of an employment contract at the point of recruitment" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1941" at="[33]" phrase="there is no workplace right to have discussions or obtain clarification upon the terms" />. That is a single-member decision on its own facts, not a general licence for recruitment conduct. Check for discrimination, coercion, contractual obligations, instrument-based rights and other statutory protections before treating a take-it-or-leave-it offer as safe. The rest of the decision was not read for this guide.
