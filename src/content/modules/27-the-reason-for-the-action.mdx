<p class="lead">Most general protections cases aren't decided by what the employee did or what the employer did. They're decided by why. This module is about how a court works out the reason for an adverse action, whose reasons count, and what an employer has to put in front of the court to prove them.</p>

<div class="notice">
<strong>Before you rely on this:</strong> the Act (ss.340, 360, 361 and 793) and the High Court decisions in <em>Barclay</em> and <em>BHP Coal</em> were checked on 14 November 2026. <a href="/about#checking">How this guide is checked</a>.
</div>

## What you'll be able to do

- State the question a court asks about the reason for adverse action, and what the employer has to prove to answer it.
- Identify every person whose reasons will be examined — not only the person who signed the letter.
- Judge whether the employer's evidence of its reasons is likely to be accepted, and what happens if part of it's missing.
- Separate a protected complaint from things that merely happened around it.

## One question: why was the action taken?

[Module 2](/modules/general-protections) introduces the reverse onus. This module assumes the employee has already shown a workplace right (or other protected matter) and adverse action. What is left is the reason.

Section 361 exists because the reason for a decision is something only the decision-maker really knows. Its purpose is to put on the employer the onus of proving what lies "peculiarly within his own knowledge" <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[99]" phrase="peculiarly within his own knowledge" />. So the court starts by presuming the action was taken for the alleged reason, and it falls to the employer to show the action "was not actuated, in any way" by the protected matter <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[109]" phrase="was not actuated, in any way" />.

Strictly, the employer must prove a negative: what did *not* drive the decision. In practice that is "most often discharged by proving what did" <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[110]" phrase="requires evidence as to what did not actuate the dismissal" />.

<div class="key">
<h3>The settled points</h3>
<p>A Full Court summary, set out by O'Callaghan J in <em>Buckeridge v Littlepay</em>, collects the propositions that matter most to an adviser:</p>
<ol class="principles">
<li><strong>The employee still has work to do.</strong> The employee should say precisely what the adverse action was and what the alleged reason was <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="allege with sufficient particularity" />, and must prove the protected matter exists as an objective fact <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="must establish the existence of that circumstance as an objective fact" />.</li>
<li><strong>One prohibited reason among several is enough.</strong> Section 360 provides that a person takes action for a particular reason if the reasons for the action include that reason. The prohibited reason must still be an operative reason in the statutory causal inquiry; it need not be the sole or dominant reason. The High Court describes the relevant formulation as a "substantial and operative" reason, or reasons including that reason <Cite id="[2023] HCA 27" at="[43]" phrase="substantial and operative" /> <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="or if the reasons for the action include the proscribed reason" />.</li>
<li><strong>Decision-makers usually have to give evidence.</strong> Discharging the onus "usually requires decision-makers to give direct evidence of their reasons" <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="usually requires decision-makers to give direct evidence of their reasons" />.</li>
<li><strong>Knowing about the complaint isn't the same as acting on it.</strong> A decision-maker's knowledge of the protected matter, even their consideration of it, doesn't require a finding that it was the reason <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="does not require a finding that the action was taken because of that circumstance" />.</li>
<li><strong>Proving the real reasons may not be enough on its own.</strong> Even where the employer's stated reasons are accepted, a lack of evidence that there were no <em>additional</em> reasons can leave the presumption standing <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="the absence of evidence that there were no additional reasons" />.</li>
</ol>
</div>

## The decision-maker's evidence

The High Court's statement in *Barclay*, as quoted in *Buckeridge*, is that it will generally be "extremely difficult to displace the statutory presumption" if the decision-maker gives no direct evidence <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[111]" phrase="extremely difficult to displace the statutory presumption" />. The other side of that statement matters just as much: direct evidence from a decision-maker that is accepted as reliable can discharge the onus by itself <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[112]" phrase="accepted as reliable" />.

So the practical question is whether the decision-maker will be believed. In *Buckeridge*, a general counsel made two complaints, one about a colleague and one about the chief executive, and was later made redundant by that chief executive. The timing was poor for the employer. It still won, because the chief executive's answers under cross-examination were consistent with his affidavit and with the documents written at the time <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[121]" phrase="consistent with his affidavit evidence, and the contemporaneous documents" />. The judge accepted that the reasons he gave were "the true and only reasons" <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[133]" phrase="the true and only reasons" />, and the Fair Work Act claims were dismissed <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[145]" phrase="must be dismissed" />.

If the decision-maker isn't believed, that will ordinarily be "a weighty consideration and often a determinative consideration" against the employer <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="a weighty consideration and often a determinative consideration" />. Decision-maker evidence isn't strictly a legal precondition <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="is not a necessary pre-condition" />, but an adviser shouldn't plan on winning without it. In the Commission, an employer whose decision-maker neither made a statement nor attended was found to have provided no notable evidence to discharge the onus <Cite id="[2025] FWC 777" at="[26]" phrase="failed to provide any notable evidence to discharge the reverse onus of proof" />.

## Whose reasons count

An employer is usually a company, and a company decides through people. Often more than one.

**Joint decisions.** In *SBP Employment Solutions v Smith*, a manager who had complained repeatedly about his workload and hours was dismissed by a decision of four directors. Only two gave evidence. On appeal, Rangiah J treated the decision as "made jointly by all the Directors" <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[108]" phrase="made jointly by all the Directors" />. What the two who gave evidence said about the reasons of the two who didn't was to be "given little weight" <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[108]" phrase="should be given little weight in the absence of direct evidence" />. Missing two of four decision-makers wasn't automatically fatal, but it was a substantial factor against the employer <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[108]" phrase="is not of itself fatal, but is a substantial factor to be weighed" />. The employer failed to prove that the complaints weren't a substantial and operative factor <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[114]" phrase="failed to prove that the complaints made by Mr Smith were not a substantial and operative factor" />.

**People who feed the decision.** The inquiry can reach past the person with authority to dismiss. In *Han v St Basil's Homes*, Rares J summarised Full Court authority this way: a person involved in the process may be someone whose state of mind counts "even though the ultimate act, such as termination, is taken or conveyed by another decision-maker" <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[20]" phrase="even though the ultimate act, such as termination, is taken or conveyed by another decision-maker" />. If a decision-maker relies on someone else's report or assessment, and that person was influenced by a prohibited reason, the prohibited reason becomes a reason for the action — "even if the decision-maker is not aware" of it <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[20]" phrase="even if the decision-maker is not aware that the individual acted because of that proscribed reason" />. The same passage describes a Full Court case where the decision-maker acted on information from another person "without investigating its reasonableness, truth or accuracy" <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[22]" phrase="without investigating its reasonableness, truth or accuracy" />, so that other person's motives had to be examined.

*Han* itself turned on this. A registered nurse who had complained about the care of residents was dismissed by a senior manager, who relied on another manager's oral reports of an investigation and "did no work to check any of what Ms Mota told her" <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[229]" phrase="did no work to check any of what Ms Mota told her" />. The judge found that the investigating manager's adverse reporting had shaped the decision-makers' attitudes and "created a different assessment process than should have obtained" <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[239]" phrase="created a different assessment process than should have obtained" />. The employer didn't discharge its onus <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[240]" phrase="discharged its onus of proof to displace the presumption" />, and the Court found the dismissal was because of the nurse's complaints and her race <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[241]" phrase="took adverse action against Ms Han by terminating her employment" />.

**But not everyone with a motive.** There's a limit. In the same case, the employee alleged that co-workers who had prompted a complaint against her were motivated by her race. Her claim about a warning failed, because there was no direct evidence that the co-workers' state of mind was shared by the three managers who decided to give the warning <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[77]" phrase="there was no direct evidence that the actual or perceived state of mind of those co-workers was shared" />. A person's motive matters only if that person actually contributed to the decision. The contrast inside the one case is the lesson: the co-workers' motives didn't reach the warning decision, but the investigating manager's reports did reach the termination decision.

**Advisers who don't decide.** In September 2026 the Full Court restated both sides of this in *Totino v Metro Trains*. An employer must identify the decision-makers for each adverse action, but that "does not mean that it is necessary for an employer to call evidence from every person involved in the decision-making processes" <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[30]" phrase="it is necessary for an employer to call evidence from every person involved in the decision-making processes" />. The people whose reasons matter are the decision-makers and anyone "whose contribution to that conduct rose above a threshold level" <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[29]" phrase="whose contribution to that conduct rose above a threshold level" />. In that case the employer's acting workplace relations director had reviewed the employee's situation and given written advice, but wasn't called. The two managers who decided gave evidence, and the Court accepted that they "did not rely on advice to determine what actions to take" <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[53]" phrase="did not rely on advice to determine what actions to take" />. Because the adviser hadn't made a significant contribution, "there was no need to call" him <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[58]" phrase="there was no need to call" />, and the appeal was dismissed <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[110]" phrase="the appeal is dismissed" />.

The contrast with *Han* is the working test. Where the decision-makers form their own view, an adviser's input may not need to be separately proved if it didn't materially contribute to the decision. Where they adopt someone else's report or recommendation, or materially rely on that person's information, that person's contribution and reasons become part of the case. The label "adviser" doesn't settle which it is.

The lesson for advisers: **map the decision before it's made.** List who decides, who recommends, and whose reports or allegations the decision rests on. Every name on that list is a potential witness, and every one of them needs reasons that can be explained.

### Corporate attribution: s.793

Section 793 matters where the respondent is a body corporate. In a proceeding under this Part, conduct engaged in by an officer, employee or agent on behalf of the body corporate is treated as conduct of the body corporate where the statutory conditions are met, including that the person acted within the actual or apparent scope of their authority or employment. That attribution question is separate from the evidentiary question addressed above: the court must still identify whose state of mind or contribution materially affected the action, and whether the employer has discharged the s.361 onus.

Don't treat s.793 as making every workplace conversation the reason for a later decision. Map the person's role, authority, conduct and connection with the decision, then analyse the relevant contributor and decision-maker evidence.

## The complaint, and what surrounds it

Two points from the Full Court summary help separate a protected complaint from its surroundings, and both need care.

First, knowledge isn't causation. An employer doesn't lose merely because the decision-maker knew of the complaint (see the settled points above).

Second, the summary records that adverse action taken because of *conduct resulting from* the exercise of a workplace right "may not offend" the prohibition <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[101]" phrase="conduct resulting from the exercise of workplace rights may not offend" />. That's the basis for the argument that an employee was disciplined for how they behaved while complaining, not for complaining. Treat this as a narrow argument. It rests on High Court and Full Court decisions this guide hasn't read in the original, the word used is "may", and a court will look hard at whether the conduct can really be separated from the complaint. Escalate before relying on it.

*SBP* shows how the line gets crossed without anyone intending it. The directors had real business reasons. But the evidence also showed two of them had become "frustrated with Mr Smith's frequent complaints" <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[112]" phrase="frequent complaints" />, and the judge found that frustration probably contributed substantially to the dismissal. Irritation at being complained about is a reason that includes the complaint.

## Witnesses who aren't called

Employers sometimes worry that leaving out any witness will be held against them. The rule is more limited. In *SBP*, Rangiah J explained that the rule in *Jones v Dunkel* allows an inference that the missing evidence wouldn't have helped the party, but it "does not permit an inference that the uncalled evidence would have been adverse" <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[86]" phrase="does not permit an inference that the uncalled evidence would have been adverse" />.

That's little comfort in a reverse-onus case. The employer carries the burden, so evidence that merely "would not have helped" leaves a gap the employer needed to fill. The uncalled directors in *SBP* cost the employer because of s.361, whatever *Jones v Dunkel* allowed.

## How the reasons case fails

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>Four decided, two explained</h3>
<p>Four directors jointly dismissed a manager who had complained about his hours and workload. Two gave evidence and spoke of what "we" decided; two stayed silent. <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[108]" phrase="is not of itself fatal, but is a substantial factor to be weighed" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Direct evidence from each decision-maker is preferred. One witness can't simply substitute their own account for another person's reasons, but evidence about another decision-maker's reasons may be admitted and assessed; its weight depends on the circumstances and supporting material.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>A believed witness and matching documents</h3>
<p>A senior lawyer complained about the chief executive and was then made redundant by him. His evidence held up under cross-examination and matched the documents written at the time. <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1036" at="[133]" phrase="the true and only reasons" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Bad timing can be survived. An unreliable decision-maker usually can't.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Mixed result</span>
<h3>Motive without a link to the decision</h3>
<p>An employee said co-workers who prompted a complaint against her were racially motivated. The claim about her warning failed: nothing showed the co-workers' state of mind was shared by the managers who decided. <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1010" at="[77]" phrase="there was no direct evidence that the actual or perceived state of mind of those co-workers was shared" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A third party's motive counts only if that person contributed to the decision. Check whether they did. The same employee won on her dismissal, where a manager's unchecked reports fed the decision.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The decision-maker stayed away</h3>
<p>An employee who had complained about a co-worker was told his role was redundant. The person who made that decision gave no statement and didn't attend the hearing. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 777" at="[26]" phrase="failed to provide any notable evidence to discharge the reverse onus of proof" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Without the decision-maker there's rarely anything to weigh against the presumption.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the Calloway Dental practice manager

<Scenario questions={["Whose reasons will a court examine?", "What is the weakest point in the employer's position?", "Does it help that the chief executive did not know about the pay query?", "What should happen before any decision is made?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Calloway Dental Group is a fictional company running nine dental clinics. Sarah has managed its largest clinic for four years. In March she emailed her regional manager, Dean, asking why clinic managers weren't being paid for the Saturday stocktakes, and saying she thought the award required it. Dean replied that he would "look into it" and didn't.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>March</span><b>Sarah emails Dean querying unpaid Saturday stocktake hours</b>
      <span>April</span><b>Sarah raises the query again at a managers' meeting; Dean tells a colleague she is "stirring the others up"</b>
      <span>May</span><b>Dean sends the chief executive a two-page report rating Sarah's clinic the worst performer and recommending her dismissal</b>
      <span>June</span><b>The chief executive and the finance director meet and agree to dismiss Sarah</b>
    </div>
    <p>The chief executive has never heard of the stocktake query. She accepted Dean's report as written. The clinic's revenue figures, which Dean's report doesn't attach, show Sarah's clinic ranked fourth of nine. The finance director's only note of the June meeting reads "agreed — action Dean's rec."</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>At least three people.</strong> The chief executive and the finance director decided jointly, so each has reasons of their own that must be proved. Dean didn't decide, but his report is what the decision rests on. On the authorities summarised in <em>Han</em>, his reasons for writing it will be examined too.</li>
      <li><strong>Dean's report.</strong> Sarah's pay query is very likely a workplace right, and dismissal is adverse action. Dean knew of the query, was irritated by it, and wrote a recommendation the figures don't obviously support. If his irritation influenced the report, that can be a reason for the dismissal even though he didn't make the decision.</li>
      <li><strong>Not much.</strong> Her ignorance means the query wasn't <em>her</em> reason. But she adopted Dean's report without testing it, so his state of mind may be attributed to the company regardless of what she knew.</li>
      <li><strong>Stop and rebuild the decision.</strong> Have the chief executive and finance director assess Sarah's performance from the source figures, not Dean's summary. Take Dean out of the process, or have his report independently checked. Record each decision-maker's own reasons at the time. Deal with the stocktake pay query on its merits and separately. If the figures don't support dismissal, there's no decision to defend.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Both decision-makers assess the source data themselves, reach their own documented view, and can each give evidence of it: defensible, though the timing remains a risk.</div>
      <div class="lo">The decision stands on Dean's untested report and a one-line note: the employer is unlikely to discharge the onus.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
