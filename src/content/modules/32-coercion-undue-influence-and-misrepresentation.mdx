<p class="lead">Three general protections do not need adverse action at all. An employer can contravene the Act by what it says: by a threat meant to stop people using a right, by pressure to sign something, or by a wrong statement about entitlements. This module covers coercion, undue influence or pressure, and misrepresentation, with attention to messages managers send to groups of staff.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Tell hard but lawful persuasion from coercion.
- Recognise a threat in a message to staff, including one framed as a joke.
- Check a statement about entitlements before it goes out, and know what "reckless" means here.
- Identify the agreements and decisions where pressure on an employee is prohibited.
- Explain who has to prove what.

## Three provisions

| Provision | What is prohibited | State of mind |
|---|---|---|
| s.343 Coercion | Taking or threatening any action against a person, intending to coerce them about a workplace right | Intent to coerce |
| s.344 Undue influence or pressure | An employer pressing an employee about certain agreements and decisions | None stated; the pressure must be undue |
| s.345 Misrepresentation | A false or misleading statement about workplace rights | Knowing or reckless |

None of these needs a dismissal, a demotion or any other adverse action. Each can be committed by a single email or post. Each carries the penalties and personal exposure described in Module 33.

A manager's state of mind is the employer's. In <em>Retail and Fast Food Workers Union v Tantex Holdings</em>, discussed below, the judge said of a restaurant manager that her <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[28]" phrase="state of mind is that of Tantex" />, applying s.793 of the Act.

## Coercion: s.343

### The rule

The Full Court summarised s.343(1) this way: <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 226" at="[26]" phrase="a person must not organise or take, or threaten to organise or take, any action against another person with intent to coerce the other person to exercise or not exercise" /> a workplace right, or to exercise it in a particular way.

"Intent to coerce" has a settled meaning. The words <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 226" at="[78]" phrase="require both an intention to negate choice, and the utilisation of means that are unlawful, illegitimate or unconscionable" />.

Both parts are needed. In <em>United Firefighters' Union v Minister for Emergency Services Victoria (No 2)</em>, the Full Court reviewed the history of the test and confirmed that <Cite id="[2025] FCAFC 179" at="[217]" phrase="mere action with intent to negate choice is not enough" />.

### Persuasion is not coercion

The second part of the test exists because ordinary workplace dealing involves pressure. As the Full Court put it when explaining where the test came from: <Cite id="[2025] FCAFC 179" at="[207]" phrase="It is in the very nature of bargaining that each party seeks to influence, persuade or induce the other" />.

An employer may argue strongly for its position, set out the consequences of a choice, and decline to offer more. In the <em>United Firefighters</em> case, a Minister intervened to stop a public agency signing a contract the union wanted. The union said that was coercion. It failed. The Minister's concerns about cost, oversight and accountability were <Cite id="[2025] FCAFC 179" at="[268]" phrase="accepted as legitimate interests that the Minister had sought to advance, protect or promote" />.

The line is crossed when the means are unlawful, illegitimate or unconscionable. The clearest case is a threat to do something the employer has no right to do.

### A threat in a staff Facebook group

<em>Tantex</em> is the case to remember. Tantex ran fast food restaurants under a franchise. The enterprise agreement gave employees a 10-minute paid drink break on shifts of a certain length. A union was publicising the entitlement while a new agreement was being negotiated. Managers used Facebook groups to communicate with staff.

A senior manager posted to one restaurant's group. In substance the post said that if employees insisted on the 10-minute break, they would not be allowed to get a drink or go to the toilet at any other time. Logan J found the manager <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[108]" phrase="made the 5 January 2019 Post very deliberately" />, meaning it to be read by the restaurant's employees as the true position.

The employer said the post was sarcasm or satire, not a threat. The judge accepted that it had those qualities, and that <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[144]" phrase="the mere presence of such qualities in a communication does not make that communication a threat" />. But in context it conveyed a warning of harm to employees who took the break, and it was meant to intimidate.

Three contraventions followed from the one post.

- **Adverse action by threat (s.340).** The threat was made because employees proposed to use an entitlement. The union <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[151]" phrase="proved a contravention of s 340 of the FWA" />. Module 26 covers threats.
- **Coercion (s.343).** The intent was to stop employees choosing to take the break. The means were unlawful: <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[155]" phrase="It was unlawful in that it constituted what I have found to be unlawful adverse action" />. The union <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[156]" phrase="proved a contravention of s 343 of the FWA as alleged" />.
- **Misrepresentation (s.345).** Discussed below.

Along the way the judge held that access to a toilet and drinking water during paid time was itself a workplace right, arising from work health and safety duties: <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[132]" phrase="The right to access the toilet or a drink of water was, in my view, a workplace right" />. An employer could regulate it, since <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[131]" phrase="it was permissible for it to place a reasonable restriction on that access" />, but could not deny it.

Lessons:

- A message in a staff group chat is a communication by the employer, and it is evidence.
- Tone does not rescue a threat. A joke about taking away something employees are entitled to, sent by a manager during a dispute about entitlements, will be read as the employees would read it.
- A threat to respond to a lawful claim by removing something else is a high-risk indicator requiring assessment under s.343. "If you want X, you'll lose Y" needs legal review before it is said; it is not automatically coercion without the required intent and unlawful, illegitimate or unconscionable means.

## Misrepresentation: s.345

### The rule

The Full Court's summary: <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 226" at="[27]" phrase="a person must not knowingly or recklessly make a false or misleading representation about the workplace rights of another person" />, or about the exercise or effect of the exercise of a workplace right.

Three questions must be addressed: whether the statement was false or misleading; whether the person who made it knew that or was reckless about it; and whether the person to whom it was made could reasonably be expected to rely on it. Section 345(2) supplies the statutory qualification: there is no contravention if that person could not reasonably be expected to rely on the representation. The reliance question is assessed objectively by reference to the recipient and the circumstances of the communication.

### Who proves it

The reverse onus does much less work here. In <em>Australian Federation of Air Pilots v Regional Express Holdings</em>, the union accepted that on the knowing-or-reckless element <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 226" at="[53]" phrase="it carried the burden in relation to this element" />. An employee or union alleging misrepresentation has to prove the employer's state of mind. For coercion and threats, by contrast, the presumption about reasons and intent can apply once the threat itself is proved (Module 27).

### What "reckless" means

In <em>Tantex</em>, the union argued that recklessness should be judged objectively, by what a reasonable employer would have known. Logan J rejected that. Recklessness is <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[51]" phrase="to be determined by reference to the author of the alleged representation" />: did that person know the statement was likely to be wrong or misleading and go ahead without caring?

That is a real protection for honest mistakes, but a limited one. The same manager's post in <em>Tantex</em> also said, wrongly, who was entitled to the 10-minute break. The judge found he was not deliberately dishonest. <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[117]" phrase="He was, however, reckless" />. The reason: <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[117]" phrase="This was a very calculated, generic representation by a Senior Manager" />, made to settle unrest among staff, by someone who had not checked what the clause said.

So recklessness was found where a senior person made a confident, general statement about entitlements, for a purpose, without reading the instrument.

### Statements that are safe

**True statements.** Another claim in <em>Tantex</em> concerned a manager who told an employee he had to come to work or find a replacement. In context she was speaking to an employee who was not genuinely sick. The statement <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1258" at="[52]" phrase="was true and known by her to be true" />, and the claim failed.

**Accurate statements of the employer's position.** In <em>AMACSU v Commissioner of Taxation</em>, a union said guidance about working from home during the pandemic misrepresented employees' rights under an enterprise agreement. Jagot J found the guidance was accurate: <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1225" at="[460]" phrase="It was not false or misleading" />. Her Honour added that even if the employer's view of the agreement had been wrong, a manager's statement of it would not have been knowingly or recklessly false, because it <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1225" at="[477]" phrase="would have accurately reflected the view of the ATO" />.

### Statements about industrial action

Telling employees that protected industrial action is unlawful is a statement about their workplace rights. In <em>Australian Nursing and Midwifery Federation v St Vincent's Private Hospitals</em>, nurses were taking action in the form of closing beds, which the union said was authorised by a protected action ballot. Managers told nurses the action was not permitted. The employer's evidence was that <Cite id="[2025] FCA 18" at="[56]" phrase="the statements were made with a genuinely held belief that they were true and correct" />.

Horan J still granted an urgent injunction. There was a serious question whether the statements were misleading in the sense that <Cite id="[2025] FCA 18" at="[54]" phrase="they have a tendency to lead the persons to whom representations are made into error" />, and a prima facie case on knowledge or recklessness. The judge concluded that <Cite id="[2025] FCA 18" at="[10]" phrase="orders should be made to restrain St Vincent's from making representations to its employees" /> that the action was unlawful or unprotected. This was an interim decision, not a final finding.

An employer that believes industrial action is unprotected has remedies of its own. Telling employees they are acting unlawfully, before a court or the Commission has said so, carries the risk shown here.

### Practical risk controls for statements about rights

1. Read the clause or section before describing it. Quote it where possible.
2. Mark a contested view as a view: "our reading of clause 29 is…", with the clause attached.
3. Have general communications about entitlements checked, especially during bargaining, a dispute or a union campaign.
4. If a statement turns out to be wrong, correct it in the same channel, promptly. Continued publication after the error is known may support recklessness in relation to a later or repeated representation, but does not retrospectively change the legal character of the original statement.

## Undue influence or pressure: s.344

Section 344 applies only to employers, and only to pressure on an employee about particular things. In summary, the current provision prohibits an employer from exerting undue influence or undue pressure on an employee in relation to a decision to:

- make, or not make, an agreement or arrangement under the National Employment Standards (for example, cashing out annual leave or averaging hours);
- make, or not make, an agreement or arrangement under a term of an award or enterprise agreement that the instrument permits (for example, time off instead of overtime);
- agree to, or terminate, an individual flexibility arrangement;
- accept a guarantee of annual earnings;
- agree, or not agree, to a deduction from wages.

Section 344 is distinct from the coercion test in s.343, but it is fact-sensitive. The statutory question is whether the employer exerted undue influence or undue pressure in relation to one of the specified decisions or agreements; it does not make every form of workplace persuasion unlawful. The cases read for this module do not support treating "undue" as a free-standing lower-bar formula divorced from the statutory context.

The one decision read for this module is an extreme example. In <em>Basi v Namitha Nakul (No 2)</em>, restaurant employees on temporary visas were made to pay part of their wages back to the employer. Halley J found the arrangement was <Cite id="[2023] FCA 671" at="[98]" phrase="enforced by coercive threats and the application of undue influence and pressure" /> and contravened ss.343 and 344 among other provisions. The employer's conduct <Cite id="[2023] FCA 671" at="[56]" phrase="took advantage of the inherent vulnerabilities of employees on temporary immigration visas" />.

Most s.344 risk is far more ordinary: a manager who wants a form signed today. As prudent risk-management controls for any agreement on the list:

- give the proposal in writing, with the relevant clause or section;
- give the employee time, and say they may get advice;
- do not link the decision to shifts, hours, promotion or continued employment;
- accept a no, and record that the employee's position is unaffected;
- take particular care with employees who are young, on visas, or new.

## Practice scenario: three messages at Halcyon Fitness

<Scenario questions={["Is the area manager's group message a problem?", "What about the FAQ on breaks?", "Is the leave cash-out request lawful?", "What should the adviser do today?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Halcyon is a fictional chain of 14 gyms covered by a modern award. A union has been leafleting staff about unpaid overtime for opening and closing duties. In one week:</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Monday</span><b>The area manager, Jono, posts in the staff group chat: "Heads up team — anyone putting in overtime claims for open/close can expect to find themselves off the weekend roster 😂 just saying"</b>
      <span>Tuesday</span><b>HR emails all staff an FAQ. One answer reads: "Casual employees are not entitled to paid or unpaid breaks." No one checked the award before sending</b>
      <span>Thursday</span><b>A club manager tells three part-time staff they need to sign annual leave cash-out forms "by tomorrow, or I can't guarantee your hours next month"</b>
    </div>
    <p>Jono says he was joking and everyone knows it. The HR manager says the FAQ was adapted from an old document and she believed it was right.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Yes.</strong> Claiming overtime under the award is a workplace right. The post tells employees that using it will cost them weekend shifts. That is a threat of adverse action and, because it is aimed at stopping claims, likely coercion: the intent is to negate a choice and the threatened action would itself be unlawful. The emoji and "just saying" do not change that. On <em>Tantex</em>, sarcasm does not prevent a message being a threat, and the context here is a live dispute about the same entitlement. Jono's state of mind is Halcyon's.</li>
      <li><strong>It needs checking now.</strong> If the award gives casuals breaks, the statement is false and is about workplace rights. Whether it was reckless depends on the author's state of mind. The HR manager believed it, which helps. But it was a general statement about entitlements, sent to all staff during a union campaign, without anyone reading the award. That is close to what was found reckless in <em>Tantex</em>. If it stays uncorrected after the error is noticed, the position gets worse.</li>
      <li><strong>No.</strong> Cashing out annual leave is an agreement the employee is free to make or refuse. A one-day deadline tied to future hours is pressure about that decision, and on any reading it is undue. If the hours are in fact reduced for someone who refuses, that is adverse action as well.</li>
      <li><strong>Three corrections and one briefing.</strong> Have Jono's post withdrawn by a message in the same chat, from someone senior, stating plainly that overtime claims will not affect rostering. Check the award and, if the FAQ is wrong, send a correction to the same list quoting the clause. Tell the three part-time staff in writing that the forms are optional, there is no deadline and their hours are unaffected. Brief managers on what they may say during the campaign. Then deal with the underlying overtime question on its merits.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">All three are corrected within the week, in the same channels, and the overtime issue is reviewed: the record shows prompt correction, and any claim is about a few days.</div>
      <div class="lo">Nothing is corrected and a claimant loses weekend shifts: a threat carried out, an uncorrected false statement, pressure to sign, and personal exposure for three managers.</div>
    </div>

## Decisions cited

Check every reference against the published judgment before relying on it. The Federal Court decisions below are direct source records: each quoted phrase and paragraph number was confirmed on 13 and 14 November 2026 in the judgment file held in the Briefer corpus, as collected from the Federal Court. They have not been re-checked against the Court's website, and no search was made for later appeals.

- ***Retail and Fast Food Workers Union v Tantex Holdings* [2020] FCA 1258** (Logan J, 31 August 2020) — contraventions of ss.340, 343 and 345 found on one post; other misrepresentation claims failed.
- ***Australian Federation of Air Pilots v Regional Express Holdings* [2021] FCAFC 226** (Bromberg, Kerr and Wheelahan JJ, 16 December 2021) — appeal allowed in part; the s.340 and s.343 claims were sent back for rehearing. Read here for the summaries of ss.343 and 345 and the parties' accepted positions on the tests; see also Module 26.
- ***AMACSU v Commissioner of Taxation* [2022] FCA 1225** (Jagot J, 14 October 2022) — proceeding dismissed. Read only for the misrepresentation claims.
- ***Basi v Namitha Nakul (No 2)* [2023] FCA 671** (Halley J, 22 June 2023) — a penalty decision. Read only for the findings on coercion and undue pressure.
- ***Australian Nursing and Midwifery Federation v St Vincent's Private Hospitals* [2025] FCA 18** (Horan J, 17 January 2025) — an interlocutory decision; injunction granted. The final result was not found.
- ***United Firefighters' Union of Australia v Minister for Emergency Services Victoria (No 2)* [2025] FCAFC 179** (Rangiah, Charlesworth and McElwaine JJ, 11 December 2025) — union's appeal dismissed.

The High Court's decision in *Esso Australia v Australian Workers' Union*, and the earlier Federal Court authorities on the meaning of "intent to coerce", are relied on only as described in the decisions above and have not been read in the original for this module.
