<p class="lead">Before the Commission looks at whether a dismissal was fair, it has to decide whether it can hear the case at all. Four gateway questions decide that — get one wrong and the merits never get considered.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Run the four jurisdictional gateways in the right order before anyone talks about fairness.
- Spot the two things that most often sink an application before it starts: minimum employment period and the 21-day time limit.
- Explain why "the employer had a good reason" doesn't help if the person was never protected from unfair dismissal in the first place.

## The four gateways

Before it considers the merits of any application, the Commission must decide four things, in this order: whether the application was lodged in time, whether the person was protected from unfair dismissal, whether the dismissal was consistent with the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code, and whether it was a case of genuine redundancy <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[6]" phrase="whether the dismissal was a case of genuine redundancy" />. A yes to the wrong one of these ends the case — no valid reason, no process, no proportionality analysis ever gets reached.

## Who is protected from unfair dismissal (s.382)

A person is protected from unfair dismissal only if they have completed <strong>at least the minimum employment period</strong>, and either a modern award covers them, an enterprise agreement applies to them, or their earnings are <strong>less than the high income threshold</strong> <Cite id="[2020] FWC 5506" at="[20]" phrase="at least the minimum employment period" />.

The two limbs are independent. Earning above the threshold isn't fatal on its own — a person can still be protected if a modern award covers their role, which is exactly the fight that played out in *Rogers v TMM Group*: both sides agreed Mr Rogers <Cite id="[2020] FWC 5506" at="[4]" phrase="satisfies the minimum employment period under s.382(a) and s.383" />, and agreed his income was above the threshold, but the whole case turned on whether an award covered his maintenance-supervisor role at a mine site.

## Minimum employment period (s.383)

The minimum employment period is **6 months**, or **one year** if the employer is a small business employer (fewer than 15 employees) <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2033" at="[17]" phrase="6 months ending at the earlier of the following times" />.

**Casual service mostly doesn't count.** As a general rule, a period of service as a casual employee is excluded from the calculation, <strong>except if</strong> the casual employee was a regular casual and had, during that service, a reasonable expectation of continuing employment on a regular and systematic basis <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2033" at="[19]" phrase="except if the casual employee was a regular and systematic employee" />.

This is where labour-hire and broken-service arrangements trip people up. In *Mulipola v Hays / DVA*, the applicant had worked a string of short assignments through a labour-hire agency; his most recent placement at the host agency lasted only 7 weeks and 1 day <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2033" at="[77]" phrase="not continuously employed by Hays for a period exceeding 6 months" />. None of his prior casual service counted towards continuity, because it wasn't shown to be regular-and-systematic engagement giving rise to a reasonable expectation of ongoing work with that particular placement.

**Adviser point:** before assuming someone clears this gate, map the actual employer against each engagement — a labour-hire worker's "service" with the host entity and with the agency are not automatically the same thing, and a break in engagements can reset the clock.

## The high income threshold

The threshold is an indexed dollar figure set under s.333 of the Act <Cite id="[2022] FWC 338" at="[18]" phrase="the amount prescribed by, or worked out in the matter prescribed by, the regulations" />, and it is measured against **earnings** as defined in s.332 — wages, amounts applied on the employee's behalf, and the **agreed money value of non-monetary benefits** <Cite id="[2022] FWC 338" at="[19]" phrase="the agreed money value of non-monetary benefits" />. Reimbursements and amounts that can't be determined in advance (like uncapped commission) are excluded.

Non-cash benefits catch people out. In *Johnson v Benex Civil*, the employer argued the applicant's income was <Cite id="[2022] FWC 338" at="[2]" phrase="in excess of the high income threshold of $153,600" />, once a company vehicle, private health cover and a mobile phone/data allowance were added to her base salary using the Fewings formula for valuing private vehicle use. The Commission agreed, on either of two competing mileage calculations, and dismissed the application <Cite id="[2022] FWC 338" at="[107]" phrase="exceeded the high income threshold of $153,600" />.

**Adviser point:** don't just compare base salary to the threshold. Add the agreed value of any car, phone, insurance or other non-cash benefit before telling anyone they're over or under the line — and remember the threshold itself moves each financial year, so always check the figure that applied on the dismissal date, not today's figure.

## The 21-day time limit (s.394(2))

An application must be made within 21 days after the dismissal took effect, or within a further period the Commission allows if satisfied there are **exceptional circumstances**, taking into account the reason for the delay, when the person became aware of the dismissal, any action taken to dispute it, prejudice to the employer, the merits of the application, and <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2405" at="[9]" phrase="fairness as between the person and other persons in a similar position" />.

Exceptional circumstances means circumstances that are <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2405" at="[10]" phrase="out of the ordinary course, unusual, special or uncommon" /> — they don't need to be unique or very rare, and a combination of otherwise-ordinary factors can add up to exceptional. In *Chait v Church of Ubuntu*, three different versions of the application were filed across a short window and the Commission ultimately found the application was made in time, and, in the alternative, that there were <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2405" at="[21]" phrase="exceptional circumstances justifying a grant of a further period" />.

But a sympathetic reason for delay is not, by itself, enough. In *Mamo v ICLED Australia*, the applicant's explanation for a short delay — a difficult mental state following her dismissal — was accepted as genuine, but a good explanation <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3903" at="[13]" phrase="might ultimately count for nought if the Commission is not satisfied that exceptional circumstances exist" />, and on the whole of the evidence the Deputy President was not satisfied, declining the extension <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3903" at="[54]" phrase="I decline to grant an extension of time under s.394(3)" />.

**Adviser point:** the clock starts when the dismissal took effect, not when the employee decides to act. Diarise it on day one of any matter, and don't assume a credible excuse will be enough — six factors are weighed, not one.

## The small business fair dismissal code (s.388)

A dismissal is consistent with the Code if, at the relevant time, the employer was a small business employer and <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2493" at="[19]" phrase="the Employer complied with the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code in relation to the dismissal" />. If the Code was complied with, the dismissal is taken to be fair and s.387 is never reached — this is a complete defence, not a factor to be weighed.

For summary dismissal, the Code only requires that the employer <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2493" at="[20]" phrase="believes on reasonable grounds that the Employee's conduct is sufficiently serious to justify immediate dismissal" />. The leading authority on what that means sets a two-step test: first, did the employer in fact hold the belief; and second, <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2493" at="[99]" phrase="necessary to consider whether that belief was based on reasonable grounds" />. It is not necessary that the belief turn out to be correct — but the second step requires a genuine investigation, not just an assertion.

That second step is where employers lose the point. In *Pewsukngem v Choc Dee Thai Restaurant*, the employer summarily dismissed a head chef over sexual harassment allegations surfaced by an internal staff survey. The Commission found the belief was not shown to rest on reasonable grounds — the survey had been run for an unrelated purpose, the termination letter was drafted before the Code checklist was even completed, and the checklist itself misrepresented what notice the employee had been given. The <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2493" at="[136]" phrase="jurisdictional objection that the Respondent terminated the Applicant's employment in accordance with the Code is not upheld" />.

**Adviser point:** "we believed it" is step one. Step two — can you show the inquiries that made the belief reasonable, done before the decision, not reconstructed after it — is where most small business Code defences actually fail.

## Genuine redundancy as a bar (s.389)

A dismissal is a case of genuine redundancy, and therefore cannot be found unfair, only if three things are all true: the employer no longer required the job to be performed by anyone because of changes in its operational requirements; any award or agreement consultation obligation was complied with; and redeployment within the employer's enterprise or an associated entity's enterprise would not have been reasonable <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[8]" phrase="no longer required the person's job to be performed by anyone because of changes in the operational requirements" />. Get any one of the three wrong and the objection fails — which is why, in practice, <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[22]" phrase="there are up to three limbs within s.389 to consider" />, and parties often blur them with the separate question of whether the dismissal was otherwise fair.

On the first limb, the focus is on the **job**, not the person or their duties individually — it is <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[57]" phrase="critical for the purpose of identifying a redundancy is whether the holder of the former position has" /> any duties left to discharge after a reorganisation, even if some of those duties are redistributed elsewhere. *Ulan Coal Mine Limited v Howarth* is a Full Bench authority that has not been read in the original for this guide — see the note below. On the third limb, redeployment reasonableness turns on matters like required qualifications, the employee's skills and experience, location and remuneration, and <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[58]" phrase="a number of relevant factors might need to be considered" /> beyond that sample list — from *Skinner & Pemberton v Asciano Services*, also not read in the original for this guide.

*McNichol v Shape Australia* shows all three limbs working together. A COVID-era downturn led the employer to restructure its project teams; it found it <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[228]" phrase="no longer required Ms McNichol's job to be performed by anyone because of changes in the operational requirements" />; there was <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[228]" phrase="no obligation to consult as Ms McNichol's employment was not covered by a modern award or enterprise agreement" />; and the Commission accepted that it <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[228]" phrase="would not have been reasonable in all the circumstances for Ms McNichol to be redeployed" /> given the downturn was national, not confined to her office. The dismissal was a genuine redundancy <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[229]" phrase="within the meaning of s.389 of the Act" />, and the application could not proceed to a harshness assessment.

[Module 18](/modules/redundancy-restructuring-and-consultation) covers redundancy, consultation and redeployment in full.

**Adviser point:** a redundancy label on a termination letter proves nothing by itself. Each of the three limbs needs its own evidence — and if any award or agreement applies, the consultation step has to actually happen before the decision, not just get explained afterwards.

## How the gateways fail

These four are drawn from real decisions. In three, the applicant's own claim didn't clear the gate; in the fourth, it was the employer's defence that failed.

<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Casual service that didn't add up</h3>
<p>A labour-hire worker's most recent placement lasted 7 weeks and 1 day — nowhere near the minimum employment period, and his earlier broken assignments didn't count towards it. <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2033" at="[79]" phrase="not satisfied Mr Mulipola has served the minimum employment period" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Map continuity against the actual engaging entity, assignment by assignment — don't assume total time in the labour-hire pool counts.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>A company car pushed earnings over the line</h3>
<p>Base salary alone was under the threshold; adding the agreed value of a company vehicle, health cover and phone plan tipped total earnings above it. <Cite id="[2022] FWC 338" at="[107]" phrase="exceeded the high income threshold of $153,600" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Always value the non-cash benefits before telling anyone they're protected on income grounds.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>A credible reason for delay wasn't enough</h3>
<p>A genuinely difficult mental state after dismissal explained a short delay, but the Commission still was not satisfied the circumstances were exceptional once all six factors were weighed. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3903" at="[54]" phrase="I decline to grant an extension of time under s.394(3)" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A sympathetic story is one factor among six — it is not, on its own, exceptional circumstances.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>A survey run for the wrong reason</h3>
<p>The employer's belief that misconduct justified summary dismissal wasn't shown to rest on reasonable grounds — the investigation had been run for an unrelated purpose and the termination letter pre-dated the Code checklist. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2493" at="[136]" phrase="jurisdictional objection that the Respondent terminated the Applicant's employment in accordance with the Code is not upheld" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A genuinely held belief still needs a genuine, documented investigation behind it — done before you decide, not reconstructed afterwards.</p>
</div>

## Adviser checklist

Work through these gateways in order before anyone discusses whether the dismissal was fair. A "no" on any one of them can end the matter regardless of how the dismissal was handled.

## Practice scenario: the warehouse restructure

  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>This is a fictional scenario for training purposes. Sarah works at a mid-sized logistics warehouse (generic facts; not based on any real employer). The business has 11 employees on its books. Sarah was engaged as a casual picker through a labour-hire agency from March to August last year, then taken on directly by the warehouse operator as a casual from September, working rostered shifts most weeks.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Mar-Aug</span><b>Casual via labour-hire agency (approx. 5 months)</b>
      <span>Sep onward</span><b>Casual, engaged directly by the warehouse operator</b>
      <span>14 months later</span><b>Told by text message her role is "no longer needed"</b>
      <span>+24 days</span><b>Sarah contacts a union delegate for advice</b>
    </div>
    <p>Sarah says she was never consulted about the decision, and that two new permanent pickers started the week after she was let go. The warehouse operator says it was "just a redundancy" and that Sarah was casual, so none of this applies to her anyway.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Likely protected.</strong> The business has 11 employees, so it's a small business employer and the minimum employment period is 12 months. Sarah's direct engagement from September has run well past that. The labour-hire period (a different legal employer) wouldn't normally count towards service with the warehouse operator — but it doesn't need to here, because her own direct service already clears 12 months. If she was a regular and systematic casual with a reasonable expectation of ongoing work (rostered most weeks for over a year), that casual service counts.</li>
      <li><strong>Probably out of time, but worth checking.</strong> 24 days after a text-message dismissal is outside the 21-day limit. The reason for the delay, when she actually found out it was final, and any steps she took in that window (the union contact) all go to whether an extension could be argued — but "just a few days" with no clear exceptional circumstance is a real risk, exactly like the mental-health case above where a good reason still wasn't enough.</li>
      <li><strong>Still to check:</strong> whether "no longer needed" was genuine — two new permanent pickers starting the next week cuts hard against the job no longer being required; whether any award applied and required consultation that didn't happen; and whether redeployment to one of the new roles would have been reasonable. A redundancy label doesn't settle any of this.</li>
    </ol>
  </Fragment>
