<p class="lead">A positive test, on its own, is rarely the whole case. The Commission wants to know what the policy actually required, whether the direction to test was lawful and reasonable, and whether the employer can tell the difference between a substance being in someone's system and that person being impaired by it.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Build a positive-test or refusal-to-test case on the right legal foundation, not just the lab result.
- Tell presence apart from impairment, and know which one your employer's policy and evidence actually support.
- Spot where disclosure obligations for prescription or medicinal cannabis use bite, even without any impairment at all.

## Positive test as a valid reason

A positive test is not self-executing proof of impairment, misconduct or a valid reason for dismissal. Check the authority for testing, the policy wording, the test method and confirmation process, chain of custody, the role’s safety context, prescription or medical explanations, privacy and adjustment issues, and what the employee understood they had to disclose.

A positive result for a prohibited substance, on its own, can be a valid reason for dismissal. In *Potter v Cement Australia Holdings*, a Silo Operator failed a random oral-swab test; the confirmatory laboratory test came back positive for methamphetamine at more than sixteen times the Australian Standard cut-off <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[12]" phrase="16 times the testing cut off" />. The Commission held that it is well established that an employee who attends work and returns a positive test for a prohibited substance may, of itself, have a valid reason for dismissal <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[35]" phrase="this may, of itself, constitute a valid reason for dismissal" />.

**Safety-critical work raises the stakes.** Cement Australia's policy set a Breath Alcohol Content limit of 0.00% and drug cut-offs at the Australian Standard levels <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[9]" phrase="Breath Alcohol Content limit is 0.00%" />, and Mr Potter's duties were performed in what the Commission called an isolated, safety critical operating environment <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[38]" phrase="isolated, safety critical operating environment" />. That context did real work in the outcome: the more dangerous the workplace, the less room there is to treat a breach as a technicality. Mr Potter's application was dismissed <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[52]" phrase="not harsh, unjust or unreasonable, the application is dismissed" />.

A zero-tolerance, presence-based policy is not automatically a licence to dismiss. Even the employer's own expert evidence in *Potter* didn't stop at the lab certificate — a toxicologist gave evidence about dose, timing and effect, concluding the applicant was "more likely than not" impaired at the relevant time <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[14]" phrase="the individual would have been impaired" />. That extra layer of evidence is what let the Commission treat the case as more than a paperwork breach.

## Presence versus impairment — the metabolites problem

This is where drug and alcohol cases get technically harder than most other conduct dismissals. Metabolites of a substance — especially cannabis — can sit in urine for days or weeks after any impairment has worn off. A positive result tells you the substance was used; it does not, by itself, tell you the person was impaired at work.

*Roach v Pacific National Services* shows how the Commission resolves that gap. Mr Roach, a train driver, smoked cannabis on a day off and returned a positive urine test for cannabinoids two days later <Cite id="[2022] FWC 103" at="[11]" phrase="returned a positive result for cannabinoids (cannabis) in a random drug test" />, in a role the Commission accepted involved safety critical work <Cite id="[2022] FWC 103" at="[15]" phrase="Mr Roach's role on 19 and 20 July 2021 involved safety critical work" />. The Commission accepted he genuinely did not feel impaired — but that didn't end the inquiry, and it didn't need to: he wasn't dismissed for being discernibly affected by drugs at work, he was dismissed because he attended work with a proscribed level of cannabinoids in his system <Cite id="[2022] FWC 103" at="[16]" phrase="not dismissed for being discernibly affected by drugs at work" />.

**The lesson for advisers runs both ways.** A "presence" policy (no prohibited substance in your system, full stop) can support a valid reason without proof of actual on-the-day impairment, provided the policy is framed that way and the workplace risk justifies it <Cite id="[2022] FWC 103" at="[29]" phrase="sound, defensible and well-founded reason to terminate his employment" />. But where the employer's own case turns on an impairment argument — as Cement Australia's partly did in *Potter* — a bare test result won't carry it; you need evidence that actually speaks to timing, dose and effect, not an assumption that a positive result equals impairment.

An employee also can't use their own uncertainty about how long a substance lingers as an excuse after the fact. Mr Roach accepted there was a "slim possibility" cannabis remained in his system before attending work and said as much when tested; the Commission treated his own hedge against this risk as working against him, not for him, when weighing harshness. Separately, drug and alcohol policies commonly impose an independent duty to self-report: Mr Roach was found to have been under an obligation to notify his line manager if he was aware his work performance could be affected by a drug, which he did not do <Cite id="[2022] FWC 103" at="[61]" phrase="under an obligation pursuant to PN's policies to notify his line manager" />. The dismissal was found not unfair <Cite id="[2022] FWC 103" at="[62]" phrase="not harsh, unjust or unreasonable" />.

## Refusal to test — was the direction lawful and reasonable?

Where the allegation is refusal rather than a positive result, the question shifts: was the direction to undergo testing lawful and reasonable, and did the employee have a reasonable basis for refusing it <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[11]" phrase="refused a lawful and reasonable direction that she undertake a drug test" />?

In *Desmond v Lyndons*, the applicant refused to provide a urine sample and asked to be tested instead by oral swab, citing a urinary tract infection. The Commission applied the common-law test for the lawfulness of a direction set out by Dixon J in *R v The Darling Island Stevedoring and Lighterage Co Ltd; Ex parte Halliday and Sullivan* — whether the direction was reasonable does not require an abstract assessment of its merit, and the employer need not show the direction was the preferable or "best practice" course <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[18]" phrase="The determination of whether an employer's direction was a reasonable one" />. On that footing, the Commission found there was nothing unlawful or unreasonable about the drug and alcohol policy itself <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[125]" phrase="nothing unlawful or unreasonable about the Policy per se" />, and that the specific direction to undertake a urine test was lawful <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[129]" phrase="I find that the direction was lawful" />.

**A policy breach doesn't automatically mean dismissal is fair.** As the Full Bench majority put it in *B, C and D v Australian Postal Corporation*, quoted in *Desmond*, a substantial and wilful breach of policy will often, if not usually, be a valid reason — but every case still turns on its own circumstances <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[12]" phrase="a substantial and wilful breach of a policy will often, if not usually" />. *B, C and D* is not independently located as a direct record in this corpus pass — check the quote against the original.

The applicant's refusal was ultimately found to have no reasonable basis, and the Commission held she had knowingly and wilfully defied a lawful direction <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[152]" phrase="refused to comply with a lawful and reasonable direction to undergo a urine test on 1 February 2021 and that she had no reasonable basis for the refusal" />; her dismissal was found not unfair <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[168]" phrase="knowingly and wilfully defied a lawful direction" />.

## Random, for-cause and targeted testing

Drug and alcohol policies typically distinguish several testing regimes: random testing, testing "for cause", targeted testing, and post-incident or near-miss testing. *Lyndons'* policy, for example, allowed the Respondent to "test for cause or undertake targeted, post incident or near miss, or random testing" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[21]" phrase="may test for cause or undertake targeted, post incident or near miss, or random testing" />, with random testing defined as selecting a worker or group without advance notice.

**Random testing regimes are generally accepted as legitimate**, even though they involve some interference with privacy — the Full Bench in *Briggs v AWH Pty Ltd*, as discussed in *Desmond*, held that random and targeted drug testing is a reasonable and legitimate employer response to the safety risk posed by employee drug use <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[15]" phrase="random and targeted drug testing is a reasonable and legitimate employer response to the risk to safety posed by employee drug use" />. *Briggs* is not independently located as a direct record in this corpus pass — check the quote against the original Full Bench decision.

That legitimacy doesn't evaporate just because an employee feels singled out. In *Desmond*, the applicant argued her selection wasn't really random because she was one of the last people at her branch still to be tested; the Commission rejected that, accepting that selection from a list generated without advance notice to site management was still random within the meaning of the policy <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[142]" phrase="I also accept that the Applicant was randomly selected for testing on 1 February 2021" />. For advisers: check what the policy itself defines as "random", "for cause" and "targeted" — don't assume these are interchangeable, because an employee's challenge to the category used can become a real argument if the policy's own definitions aren't met.

## Prescription medicine and medicinal cannabis

A positive result complicated by prescription medication or medicinal cannabis raises a different question: not "was the substance there", but "did the employee have an obligation to disclose it — and did they meet it?"

*Haigh v Platinum Blasting Services* is the clearest illustration in this corpus. Mr Haigh, who operated explosives-handling machinery at a mine, began using medicinal cannabis prescribed for anxiety and insomnia after a family member suggested it <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2465" at="[8]" phrase="informed by his brother-in-law that he had been prescribed medicinal cannabis" />. He disclosed the prescription once, went through his employer's medical management process, then stopped the alternative medication he'd been moved to and resumed the cannabis script without re-disclosing it. The lapse only came to light after a workplace safety incident triggered testing, during which he told the on-site paramedic that THC might show up <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2465" at="[19]" phrase="disclosed to BMA's onsite paramedic that Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) may be present in the test results" />.

**"I wasn't impaired" is not a defence to a disclosure obligation.** Mr Haigh argued he had no obligation to declare the prescription because it wouldn't impair him at work. The Commission rejected that reading of the policy: a clause requiring disclosure of medication that "could impair" or "may affect" judgement addresses the *possibility* of impairment, not a measured certainty of it <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2465" at="[47]" phrase="words that indicate the possibility of impairment rather than a measurable assessment of impairment" />. His assumption that silence was fine so long as he felt fine was found to be an incorrect reading of his own obligations <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2465" at="[46]" phrase="made an incorrect assumption that there was no obligation for him to disclose his prescription medication if it would not impair him at work" />. The valid reason for dismissal was the non-disclosure itself <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2465" at="[62]" phrase="There was a valid reason for dismissal as the Applicant did not disclose" />, not a failed drug test — he had, in fact, passed every test he'd ever taken. The dismissal was ultimately found not harsh, unjust or unreasonable <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2465" at="[89]" phrase="not harsh, unjust or unreasonable" />, though the Commission came close to the opposite conclusion given how clean his testing record was — see below.

For advisers, the practical point is to look at the exact words of the disclosure clause. "Could impair", "may affect" and "could affect your ability to work safely" are possibility-based triggers. They are not satisfied by an employee's own belief, however honestly held, that they personally weren't affected.

## How these dismissals fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The policy existed, but wasn't actually communicated</h3>
<p>A cabin crew member breached an airline's "8-hour rule" by half an hour, after checking with a manager and self-breathalysing. The Commission found the rule was scattered across inconsistent manuals and explained to staff with conflicting messaging about whether it was binding <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2154" at="[117]" phrase="fails to capture the nuance that the drug and alcohol policies are spread out over multiple manuals" />, and that training on the same topic left even newly promoted managers disagreeing about whether it was a rule or a guideline <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2154" at="[120]" phrase="lack of nuanced understanding of how Virgin's drug and alcohol policies are structured and communicated" />. No valid reason was found, and the applicant was reinstated <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2154" at="[6]" phrase="no valid reason for Mr Macnish's dismissal" />.</p>
<p class="lesson">A technical breach only carries weight if the rule was actually, consistently communicated — not just written down somewhere.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer won, narrowly</span>
<h3>A clean testing record almost tipped the balance</h3>
<p>Mr Haigh had passed every drug test he'd ever sat, which the Commission found supported his case for harshness and weighed in his favour <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2465" at="[89]" phrase="not harsh, unjust or unreasonable" />. What kept the dismissal standing was the length and clarity of the disclosure process he'd ignored — three months of direct correspondence requiring him to report any change in medication, which he didn't.</p>
<p class="lesson">A clean test history helps on harshness, but it doesn't cure a disclosure failure. Don't assume a good record alone will save a non-disclosure case.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer won, but it was close</span>
<h3>A vague note doesn't preserve a medical-condition defence</h3>
<p>Ms Desmond wrote "personal reasons" on the testing form and claimed she'd mentioned a medical condition verbally; the people she spoke to denied it. The Commission went through the evidence in detail before concluding it was incumbent on her to clearly state the reason for her refusal in her written responses to the allegations <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[144]" phrase="it was incumbent on her to explain the reasons for her refusal" /> — which she never did.</p>
<p class="lesson">If a medical condition is going to be the reason a testing direction was unreasonable, it has to be disclosed specifically, to the right person, at the time — not inferred after the fact from a vague note.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the leftover tablets

      <span>Wed</span><b>Random oral swab: non-negative, confirmed in lab as codeine metabolites</b>
    </div>
    <p>Simone says she didn't know old tablets could still show up two days later, and that she felt completely normal both days. She wasn't asked about medication before her shift, and the policy doesn't say what happens if an employee genuinely didn't realise disclosure was required.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Not yet.</strong> There's a confirmed non-negative result, but no finding yet on whether this was a disclosure failure (she didn't flag the tablets before her shift) or a presence-only breach (an unprescribed opioid in her system) — these are different cases with different evidence needs, as the *Haigh* and *Roach* cases show.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> whether codeine without a current prescription counts as "non-prescribed" under the policy; medical/toxicology evidence on whether two tablets taken on Monday could plausibly still register on Wednesday (unlike the clear-cut Roach timeline, this one is tighter and more contestable); and whether Simone was ever told, at induction or otherwise, that she needed to disclose even leftover medication from an old script.</li>
      <li><strong>Framing:</strong> Put the allegation at the level the evidence supports — "you operated a forklift with an opioid substance in your system, contrary to the policy" is defensible on the lab result alone. Don't allege a failure to disclose unless you can show the policy's disclosure trigger applied and Simone understood it; on these facts, that's the weaker half of the case.</li>
    </ol>
