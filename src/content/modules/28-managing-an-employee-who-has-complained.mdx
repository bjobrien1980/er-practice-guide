<p class="lead">An employee who has complained can still be performance managed, disciplined, restructured or dismissed. What changes is that every step taken afterwards may have to be explained in court by the person who took it. This module is about how to keep managing lawfully once a complaint is on the record: who should decide, who should know, what to keep separate, and what to do about timing.</p>

<div class="notice">
<strong>Before you rely on this:</strong> this module applies the rules in Modules 25 to 27, and the Act wasn't separately re-checked for it. Each case here turned on its own evidence. They show what has worked and what has failed, not rules that guarantee an outcome. <a href="/about#checking">How this guide is checked</a>.
</div>

## What you'll be able to do

- Advise a manager who wants to act against an employee who has recently complained.
- Decide who should and shouldn't make the decision.
- Keep a complaint process and a performance or conduct process separate.
- Judge whether to continue, pause or start performance management after a complaint.
- Recognise the timing and documents that make an employer's reasons hard to believe.

## The complaint isn't a shield, and not nothing

Two things are true together.

**A complaint doesn't stop management.** Employers win these cases when the decision-maker's reasons are real and provable. In <em>Nuttall v Hatch</em>, a manager who had raised concerns about the executive he worked under was moved to a lesser role. The claim failed because that executive <Cite id="[2024] FCA 339" at="[211]" phrase="genuinely formed views about the adequacy of Mr Nuttall's performance and that he acted upon those views" quote />. In <em>Roohizadegan v Technology One (No 6)</em>, a senior manager who had complained about more senior managers was dismissed by the chief executive. After a retrial the court found for the employer: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1619" at="[697]" phrase="I am well satisfied that Technology One has rebutted the presumption at s 361" quote />.

**But the complaint changes the burden.** Once the employee alleges that action was taken because of the complaint, the employer has to prove it wasn't (Module 27). An employer doesn't have to have done anything wrong to lose. In <em>RPS AAP Consulting v Lamb</em>, the trial judge never found that the complaint caused the demotion. He wasn't persuaded that it hadn't, and that was enough. On appeal Raper J agreed: <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1310" at="[96]" phrase="it was enough for his Honour to be unpersuaded by Mr Stamatoudis's denials of any connection" quote />.

So the adviser's question isn't "can we still act?" It is "if we act, who will give evidence about why, and will the documents support them?"

## Keep the person complained about out of the decision

This is a prudent risk-management control, not an absolute statutory recusal rule. The cases show why a person who is the subject of a complaint should ordinarily not control the later decision, but the legal questions remain who materially contributed, what reasons actually operated, and whether the employer can discharge the s.361 onus.

In <em>Lamb</em>, a senior consultant with a good performance rating complained to her supervisor about his own conduct. What happened next is in the judgment's opening paragraphs: <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1310" at="[3]" phrase="In a very short period (namely, seven days) after Ms Lamb made her complaint" quote /> the supervisor decided she should step back from her national role. The employer said two other executives made the decision, for reasons to do with her study commitments. The trial judge found the supervisor was also a decision-maker. He was <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1310" at="[85]" phrase="the author, proponent and primary actor in the adverse action" quote />.

The employer argued on appeal that the judge had wrongly applied a bias test from public law. Raper J rejected that, and described the judge's point as <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1310" at="[88]" phrase="what was obvious and entirely appropriate" quote />: where the supervisor was conflicted, <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1310" at="[88]" phrase="he should not have been an author of the decision to demote, nor an active participant in that process" quote />.

<em>Serco Citizen Services v Parsons</em> is similar. An employee who was the subject of a co-worker's complaints made his own complaint against the operations director. The same director then decided the outcome of the complaint against him and dismissed him. The trial judge found the dismissal was because of his complaint, and the Full Court refused to disturb that: <Cite id="[2025] FCAFC 83" at="[106]" phrase="there is no error identified by Serco in the reasons of the primary judge" quote />.

Two risk-management controls follow.

- **As a risk-control preference, the person complained about shouldn't decide, propose or draft.** It isn't enough to assume that someone else signing automatically cures the risk. In <em>Lamb</em>, the other two executives didn't help, because neither <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1310" at="[85]" phrase="took steps to reverse or slow down the process" quote />.
- **If a complaint is made about the decision-maker part-way through a process, consider stopping and reassigning.** Reassignment reduces risk but doesn't retrospectively cure an earlier tainted step. That's the practical lesson from <em>Parsons</em>.

Sometimes the person complained about is the only person with the knowledge to assess performance. They can be a witness to the decision-maker: they supply facts, in writing, and someone else tests those facts and decides. Module 27 explains the difference between a decision-maker who forms their own view and one who adopts another person's.

## Limit who knows

A decision-maker's lack of knowledge ordinarily makes it difficult to prove that the decision-maker personally acted because of the complaint, but it doesn't necessarily protect the employer if another person materially contributed to the decision and acted for that reason.

In <em>Nuttall</em>, the employee raised his frustrations about the executive with a more senior managing director. The managing director then spoke to the executive about the working relationship, but <Cite id="[2024] FCA 339" at="[204]" phrase="did not share with Mr Moran the specific nature of Mr Nuttall's complaints" quote />. Hespe J accepted that evidence. By then the executive <Cite id="[2024] FCA 339" at="[205]" phrase="had formed strong views about Mr Nuttall's performance" quote />, and those views, not any knowledge of a complaint, caused the decision.

This doesn't mean hiding complaints. A complaint has to be dealt with, and the person complained about is usually entitled to know the allegations when it's investigated. It means thinking about the order of events:

- Record who was told about a complaint, and when.
- Don't forward a complaint to a manager who is about to make a decision about the complainant, unless they need it for that decision.
- If the decision-maker does know, say so. An employer that claims ignorance and is disbelieved is in a worse position than one that admits knowledge and proves other reasons.

## Run two processes, not one: a risk-management preference

A complaint by an employee and a concern about that employee are different matters. As a risk-management preference, keep them procedurally distinct; treating them as one can make the employer's reasons difficult to separate from the complaint. This isn't a rule that every overlap invalidates later action, and using a second decision-maker doesn't automatically cure an earlier tainted step.

<em>Rudakova v St Vincent's Private Hospital</em> shows the two-track approach working. A medical intern lodged a grievance alleging bullying by senior doctors. Separately, two doctors complained about her conduct. Her own counsel described <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1222" at="[5]" phrase="two consecutive and overlapping procedures" quote />. The chief executive who dismissed her knew of the grievance. His evidence, recorded by the trial judge, was that he followed HR advice not to take her complaints into account and to <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1222" at="[16]" phrase="limit his consideration to the allegations in the show cause letter and the information sent to him by HR" quote />. That evidence was accepted, and the appeal failed <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1222" at="[68]" phrase="The appeal should be dismissed" />.

<em>Han v St Basil's Homes</em> is the opposite. A later judge summarised it this way: <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[64]" phrase="Instead of investigating her complaints, the respondent levelled allegations against her which were found to have no substance" quote />. Module 27 covers the liability findings.

What separation looks like in practice:

- **Two files, two decision-makers where possible.** This is a risk-control preference. The person deciding the conduct or performance matter gets the material relevant to that matter and nothing else; the legal question remains whether a contributor's knowledge or reason materially affected the decision.
- **Written instructions to the decision-maker** about what they're deciding and what is outside it. In <em>Rudakova</em>, that advice became evidence.
- **Deal with the complaint on its merits.** Acknowledge it, investigate it properly, and give the employee an outcome. An ignored complaint followed by action against the complainant is the pattern in <em>Han</em>.
- **Don't make the complaint itself the subject of discipline.** An investigation into the tone or motive of a complaint is very hard to defend; see the discussion of <em>Avard</em> in Module 26. If a complaint is thought to be knowingly false, escalate before doing anything.

## Performance management: continue, pause or start?

### Already under way

Performance management that began before the complaint can continue. Stopping it because of the complaint can make later action look like a response to the complaint when it resumes.

In <em>Zabortseva v Commonwealth</em>, an employee who had made complaints alleged that three performance appraisals by her manager were adverse action. Needham J found that honest feedback hadn't harmed her position <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1585" at="[186]" phrase="I find that it did not injure the applicant in her employment" />, and that in any event the manager <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1585" at="[253]" phrase="was not motivated by any prohibited reasons to make the performance appraisals that she did" quote />. The manager didn't give evidence. The employer still succeeded, because the emails written at the time showed what she was thinking: the judge relied on <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1585" at="[252]" phrase="the correspondence I have referred to" quote />. That's unusual, and an employer shouldn't plan on succeeding without its decision-maker. The point is the value of records made at the time.

<em>Roohizadegan (No 6)</em> turned on the same thing. The chief executive's concerns about the manager's performance were of long standing. An email about problems in his office was the final straw, but <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1619" at="[884]" phrase="even he was losing confidence in Behnam well before he read the email" quote />.

### Not yet started

Starting performance management soon after a complaint is where the risk is highest. Ask:

- **Is there a record of the concern from before the complaint?** In <em>Lamb</em>, the employee had <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1310" at="[2]" phrase="received a three out of four performance rating" quote /> before she complained. Nothing on file supported a sudden loss of confidence.
- **Is the normal process being followed?** The judge in <em>Lamb</em> noted that no one had thought <Cite id="[2023] FCA 1310" at="[85]" phrase="to allow for Ms Lamb to undertake a performance improvement plan before any consideration was given to a demotion" quote />. Skipping steps that would be taken for anyone else suggests another reason.
- **Would this be happening if there had been no complaint?** If the honest answer is "probably not yet", wait, or have someone else assess the performance concern from the start.

## Timing and the snap decision

Speed after a complaint is evidence. So is a gap between what the decision-maker says in court and what the documents show.

In <em>Gavin v Svitzer Australia</em>, a port manager was offered a new role in a restructure and sent two emails about it, the first declining it and the second setting out options. The second email was forwarded to the executive responsible on the morning of 22 May 2023. That day she decided he would be made redundant instead. In court she said the decision had been reached over weeks. Needham J didn't accept it: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1623" at="[158]" phrase="The contemporary documentation does not support her version" quote />. The finding was that <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1623" at="[187]" phrase="it was a snap decision which reversed months of careful planning" quote />, and that <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1623" at="[200]" phrase="the substantial and operative reason for Mr Gavin's termination was his exercise of his workplace rights" quote />.

Module 33 covers what that cost. The lessons here:

- **A decision made within hours or days of a complaint needs a strong, documented, independent reason.** If there's none, don't make it.
- **Irritation isn't a lawful reason if it's irritation at the complaint.** The judge found the executive's first reaction was that the employee wasn't excited enough about the opportunity. The emails were the complaint.
- **Don't reconstruct.** The account given in court has to match the emails, meeting notes and drafts. Ask the decision-maker for their reasons, and then check those reasons against the documents before relying on them.

## Restructures and redundancy

A restructure isn't a safe route around a complainant. The reason for selecting that person, or for including their role, is examined in the same way as any other reason.

<em>Gavin</em> is one example. <em>Monash Health v Singh</em> is another. A librarian whose position was made redundant believed the redundancy was <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 166" at="[2]" phrase="a device deployed by Monash Health to rid it of a troublesome employee" quote />. He relied on 13 complaints, proceedings he had brought in the Commission, and personal leave. He won at trial. The Full Court set that aside because the trial judge hadn't worked through the reasons one by one: <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 166" at="[86]" phrase="there was no independent determination, upon analysis of all the evidence, of whether, by reference to each proscribed reason, the statutory presumption was rebutted" quote />. The case was sent back for a new hearing before a different judge <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 166" at="[165]" phrase="the only course available to the Court is to remit the matter for rehearing" />.

That was a win for the employer on appeal, but look at what it means. The employer has to answer each alleged reason, for each decision-maker, at each stage of the restructure. For an employee with many complaints over several years, that is a heavy evidentiary task, and in <em>Singh</em> it had to be done twice.

Before a restructure that affects someone who has complained:

- Record the business case, and when it was first proposed, before selection is discussed.
- Record who decided which roles would go, and what they knew about the person's complaints.
- Apply the same selection method to everyone in scope.
- Treat redeployment in the ordinary way. A role offered and then withdrawn after the person raises questions about it's the <em>Gavin</em> pattern.

## Letters drafted by HR

HR commonly drafts the show cause and termination letters. Once the decision-maker signs, the reasons in the letter are theirs.

In <em>Parsons</em>, the employer argued on appeal that the letter was really the HR specialist's work and should be read that way. The Full Court disagreed. A decision-maker may adopt a draft, but <Cite id="[2025] FCAFC 83" at="[83]" phrase="must engage in an active intellectual process to adopt and make the decision" quote />. After that, <Cite id="[2025] FCAFC 83" at="[84]" phrase="the matters in that letter were statements of the Operations Director" quote />.

Draft from the decision-maker's own account of their reasons. Have them read the letter line by line and correct it. Don't include a ground the decision-maker can't explain or doesn't believe, because they'll be cross-examined on every sentence.

## Practice scenario: the Fernhill performance plan

<Scenario questions={["Can the performance plan go ahead?", "Who should handle what?", "What should happen with the roster change?", "What does the adviser tell Marcus?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Fernhill is a fictional operator of nine childcare centres. Aroha is a room leader. Marcus is her centre director.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>March</span><b>Aroha's annual review, written by Marcus: "meets expectations"</b>
      <span>2 June</span><b>Aroha emails the area manager complaining that Marcus shouts at staff in front of children and changes rosters without notice</b>
      <span>4 June</span><b>The area manager forwards the email to Marcus "for your response"</b>
      <span>9 June</span><b>Marcus moves Aroha from room leader shifts to float shifts "to balance the roster"</b>
      <span>11 June</span><b>Marcus asks HR to draft a performance improvement plan for Aroha, citing late programming documents and "a negative attitude"</b>
    </div>
    <p>Marcus says the late documents are a real problem going back months. He has two reminder emails to Aroha from April. There's nothing else on file. He wants the plan issued this week and asks HR to "word it firmly".</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Not in this form, and not from Marcus.</strong> Aroha's email is very likely a workplace right (Module 25). A performance plan can be adverse action depending on its effect (Module 26). Nine days after a complaint about him, Marcus is proposing it. That's the <em>Lamb</em> pattern: the person complained about as author and proponent. The March review says "meets expectations". The April emails are some support for the late documents. "A negative attitude" has no record at all and, in context, reads as a description of the complaint.</li>
      <li><strong>Separate the two matters.</strong> The area manager, or someone else outside the centre, deals with Aroha's complaint about Marcus and gives her an outcome. A different manager assesses the late programming documents from the start, using the April emails and the documents themselves. Marcus supplies facts in writing. He doesn't decide. If that manager concludes a plan is warranted, it's theirs, it follows the usual steps, and it covers only what the records show.</li>
      <li><strong>Review it now.</strong> The move to float shifts came five days after Marcus read the complaint, and he made it. Unless there's a roster record showing the same change was planned before 2 June or applied to others, reverse it. If it stays, someone other than Marcus needs to be able to explain it.</li>
      <li><strong>That he isn't being disbelieved, and that the process protects him.</strong> If the document problem is real, an independent assessment will show it and the result will be defensible. If he drives the plan himself, the employer will have to prove his reasons had nothing to do with the complaint, and he may be named personally (Module 33). HR won't "word it firmly"; the letter will say what the decision-maker actually concluded. The area manager also needs a word: forwarding the complaint to Marcus before deciding how to handle it made everything after it harder.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">The complaint is investigated, the roster change is reversed, and an independent manager finds a documented problem with late programming: a short, specific plan issued by that manager.</div>
      <div class="lo">Marcus issues the plan and the float shifts continue: two adverse actions within ten days of a complaint about him, a clean review on file, and no one else able to give evidence of the reasons.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
