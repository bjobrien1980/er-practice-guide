<p class="lead">A false timesheet entry, an inflated expense claim, a maintenance checklist signed off before the work was done — the record being wrong is usually easy to prove. Whether the person who made it was dishonest is a different, much harder question, and getting that distinction right decides most of these cases.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Separate "the record was wrong" from "they knew it was wrong when they made it" — and know which one you actually have to prove.
- Apply the Briginshaw standard with the weight a dishonesty finding actually carries for someone's reputation and future employability.
- Recognise the patterns the Commission uses to tell a genuine lie from a genuine mistake, and know when lying about the conduct can become its own separate reason.

## The fact and the state of mind are two different questions

Module 5 flagged the trap: an allegation framed as "knowingly" lodging false reports failed because "knowingly" was never proved, even though the reports themselves were wrong. This module is about that same trap, specifically for falsified business records — timesheets, expense claims, maintenance and safety checklists, sales records, records of customer interactions, qualifications on a resume.

An employer who wants to dismiss someone for **dishonesty** is not just proving the record was inaccurate. It has to prove the person knew what they recorded was false — or was reckless as to its truth — at the time they recorded it. A genuine mistake, a misunderstanding of what was actually required, or plain carelessness is not dishonesty, however wrong the record turned out to be.

*Petrie v Amazon* shows what that looks like when the employer can't clear that bar. Mr Petrie signed off a preventative maintenance work order as complete after doing only a visual check, having previously been told by an earlier supervisor that this was an acceptable practice. Amazon treated this as "falsification of a document or fraud and an act of dishonesty." The Commission disagreed: "This was his honest, but mistaken understanding given, I find, that this was a practice that he had been previously told to adopt by his previous supervisor" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2346" at="[112]" phrase="This was his honest, but mistaken understanding" />. Even though the work order itself was literally inaccurate, "I do not consider that Mr Petrie was dishonest" or that he falsified the document <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2346" at="[114]" phrase="I do not consider that Mr Petrie was dishonest" />. Overall, the Commission was not satisfied the respondent had a valid reason to dismiss him at all <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2346" at="[135]" phrase="not satisfied that the Respondent had a sound, defensible and well-founded reason to dismiss" />, and ordered that he be reinstated <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2346" at="[192]" phrase="that Mr Petrie be reinstated to the position in which he was employed" />.

*Tiavo v Axiom Australia* shows the other side of the same question — where the Commission was satisfied the line into dishonesty had actually been crossed. More on that below.

## Briginshaw: why the standard matters more here

Dishonesty is exactly the kind of allegation Briginshaw was written for. A finding that someone lied, not just that they made an error, carries its own reputational weight — it follows a person into their next job search in a way "you missed a KPI" never will.

*Tiavo* applies the standard directly to a dishonesty allegation about falsified timesheets: "the nature of the relevant issue necessarily affects the 'process by which reasonable satisfaction is attained' and such satisfaction 'should not be produced by inexact proofs, indefinite testimony, or indirect inferences'" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4819" at="[65]" phrase="should not be produced by inexact proofs, indefinite testimony, or indirect inferences" />. Quoting *Greyhound Racing Authority*, the Commission put the practical translation this way: the level of satisfaction needed must be "commensurate with the gravity of the charge" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4819" at="[66]" phrase="commensurate with the gravity of the charge" />. The applicant himself ran this argument, submitting that "in accordance with the principles articulated in Briginshaw an allegation of dishonesty is a grave matter with serious consequences" and the Commission ought be cautious before finding him dishonest <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4819" at="[74]" phrase="an allegation of dishonesty is a grave matter with serious consequences" />.

The Commission accepted the standard but still found dishonesty proved on these facts — which is the point. Briginshaw doesn't mean the Commission can never find dishonesty; it means the finding has to be properly earned on the evidence, not assumed from a wrong number on a form.

## Patterns the Commission looks for

### A pattern beats a one-off

A single discrepancy is usually at least as consistent with a mistake as with a lie. A pattern — especially one that tracks a clear benefit to the employee — is much harder to explain away.

In *Goburdhun v Endeavour Group (BWS)*, a store manager manually altered his clock-out time to 5:30pm on eleven consecutive days, when CCTV showed he had actually left the store up to an hour and a half earlier each time. The Commission was satisfied he had deliberately entered incorrect clock-out times <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3224" at="[116]" phrase="I am satisfied that the Applicant deliberately entered incorrect clock out times" /> and had repeatedly falsified them <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3224" at="[117]" phrase="The Applicant repeatedly falsified his clock out times" />. Critically, the Commission drew the pattern-versus-isolated-incident distinction explicitly: it might have accepted a one-off unapproved early start for operational reasons, "[b]ut in this instance, on his own evidence, he needed to start early for eleven days in a row" and then chose to finish early on all of them too <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3224" at="[132]" phrase="he needed to start early for eleven days in a row" />. He also had a clear, stated motive — to collect his wife from work — which made the pattern make sense as a choice rather than an accident. The Commission found he "chose to deceive his employer" and actively concealed his early departures <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3224" at="[138]" phrase="The Applicant chose to deceive his employer" />, and that falsifying his clock-out times on multiple occasions was a valid reason for dismissal <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3224" at="[119]" phrase="falsifying his clock out times on multiple occasions was a valid reason for his dismissal" />. The dismissal was neither harsh, unjust nor unreasonable <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3224" at="[141]" phrase="The dismissal was neither harsh, unjust, nor unreasonable" />.

*Raitt v G-Force Recruitment* shows the same pattern-logic from a different angle: independent corroboration from people who had no reason to coordinate a story. A disability support team leader "resulted" (marked as completed) appointments with participants she had not actually attended. The employer's dismissal letter noted she had only 39 case entries for the relevant period against a colleague's 196 <Cite id="[2023] FWC 946" at="[9]" phrase="39 case entries for the period from September to December 2022 whereas her colleague in Warrnambool had 196 entries" /> — a comparator that turns "maybe she was just slower" into "something doesn't add up." The Commission accepted that several participants had independently contacted the company to say their scheduled appointments with her had not actually happened <Cite id="[2023] FWC 946" at="[10]" phrase="several participants contacted the company to advise that they had not had their scheduled appointments with Ms Raitt" />, and found she had represented by her own electronic records that she had met with participants when she had not <Cite id="[2023] FWC 946" at="[13]" phrase="she represented to the company by her electronic records that she had met with various participants when she had not" />. The company had good and substantiated reasons to dismiss her <Cite id="[2023] FWC 946" at="[21]" phrase="the company had good and substantiated reasons to dismiss Ms Raitt" />.

### A shifting explanation undermines an "honest mistake" defence

An account that stays the same each time it's tested is doing real work for the person giving it. An account that changes — "someone must have altered the record," then "I don't recall," then "it was too far back" — works against them.

Back in *Tiavo*, when confronted under cross-examination with a discrepancy between his claimed hours and the sign-in sheet, the applicant initially blamed someone else for altering the record, then progressively retreated to "I don't remember," "I don't recall," and "I can't recall that day." He had also received a specific verbal warning not to falsify his time entries before the final incident. The Commission found it "implausible that the further incorrect entry on 10 February 2020 was an innocent or honest mistake", noting his evidence "was evasive, unresponsive and exhibited a high degree of forgetfulness. It was simply not credible" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4819" at="[75]" phrase="I consider it implausible that the further incorrect entry on 10 February 2020 was an innocent or honest mistake" />. Having applied Briginshaw properly, the Commission was not satisfied the dismissal was unfair <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4819" at="[114]" phrase="not satisfied that Mr Tiavo was unfairly dismissed" />.

*Lainas v AMSPEC Australia* shows a milder version of the same thing. Confronted with a timesheet claim inconsistent with the company's own entry and exit records, the applicant offered a shifting mix of excuses — "maybe he was sick," and that this was, in his own words, "maybe" an honest way of dealing with things <Cite id="[2024] FWC 311" at="[47]" phrase="an honest way of dealing with things" />. The Commission found he had incorrectly submitted a timesheet and claimed for hours he did not work, and that this was a further valid reason for dismissal <Cite id="[2024] FWC 311" at="[48]" phrase="incorrectly submit a timesheet and claim for hours that he did not work" />. That finding on the timesheet did not decide the whole case, though — the Commission went on to find the dismissal was still, overall, harsh, unjust or unreasonable because of separate procedural failures by the employer <Cite id="[2024] FWC 311" at="[81]" phrase="I am satisfied that the Applicant was unfairly dismissed and that the dismissal was harsh, unjust" />. A proven dishonesty finding on one ground doesn't automatically carry the rest of the case.

### You don't always need to prove the lie

Sometimes the inaccuracy in the record is enough on its own, without the employer having to resolve whether it was deliberate. In *Stark v Charter Mercantile Agency*, a call-centre employee provided inaccurate timesheets. The Commission didn't need to work out "whether this was a deliberately deceitful act... or whether she genuinely believed that... the time sheets reflected the hours that she worked" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6682" at="[95]" phrase="It is not necessary for me to determine whether this was a deliberately deceitful act" /> — in a workplace handling highly sensitive, confidential information and relying heavily on trust, the inaccuracy itself was enough to found a valid reason. Despite a procedural fairness failure, the dismissal was, on balance, not harsh, unjust or unreasonable <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6682" at="[104]" phrase="the gravity of her conduct means that, on balance, the dismissal of the Applicant was not harsh, unjust or unreasonable" />.

The adviser's lesson here runs the other way from the rest of this module: don't reach for the word "dishonest" if you don't actually need it. If an inaccurate record is a valid reason on its own in this workplace, a dishonesty label just adds a harder thing to prove for no extra benefit.

## Beyond the day-to-day record: resumes and qualifications

The same fact/intent split applies to a misrepresented qualification, not just a day-to-day record. In an extension-of-time decision, *Spottiswood v Brand Collective Australia*, the Commission found the applicant had used the letters "CA" after his qualification on his resume specifically "to give a false impression he was a member of CA ANZ" when his membership had in fact been cancelled <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1837" at="[32]" phrase="He used the description to give a false impression he was a member of CA ANZ" />, and found that he did misrepresent his membership <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1837" at="[33]" phrase="I find that he did misrepresent that he was a member of CA ANZ" />. The Commission observed that, on that basis, "the merits of the case are not strong" <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1837" at="[36]" phrase="the merits of the case are not strong" />.

That comment matters, but it needs to be read for what it actually is. The application itself was dismissed for want of an extension of time, not on a final hearing of the merits — so this isn't a concluded valid-reason finding, it's a preliminary view formed while deciding whether to let the case proceed at all. Treat it as useful evidence of how a resume misrepresentation is likely to be assessed, not as a fully tested outcome.

## A lie told about the conduct can be its own valid reason

Everything so far has been about the underlying record. But lying to the employer *about* the record — or about the conduct generally — during the investigation can be a separate ground in its own right, distinct from whatever the original conduct was.

*Bowen v Queensland Rail Transit Authority* discusses this, by way of a Full Bench decision in *Newton v Toll Transport* which in turn draws on *Streeter v Telstra Corporation Limited*. In *Streeter*, an employee had engaged in consensual sexual activity with a colleague at a work-related social function, within view of other colleagues. When interviewed, she initially denied it and then refused to answer questions. At first instance it was found the underlying conduct, while connected to employment, wasn't serious enough to be a valid reason — but "Telstra also relied on Ms Streeter's lies about engaging in sexual activity during its investigation as a separate valid reason for her dismissal" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 898" at="[144]" phrase="Telstra also relied on Ms Streeter's lies about engaging in sexual activity during its investigation as a separate valid reason for her dismissal" />. On appeal, the majority held the member at first instance had erred: "there was a valid reason for Ms Streeter's dismissal due to her dishonesty in her interviews with Telstra management" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 898" at="[144]" phrase="there was a valid reason for Ms Streeter's dismissal due to her dishonesty in her interviews with Telstra management" />.

The lesson for advisers: if an employee lies to you during an investigation — about the underlying conduct, or about something else entirely — that lie can stand as its own, additional allegation. Put it to them separately and in its own terms, rather than folding it silently into the original allegation.

## Dishonesty discovered after the dismissal

Sometimes evidence of dishonesty only surfaces after someone has already been dismissed for an unrelated reason. The general principle, as a Commission member summarised it while dealing with a separate evidentiary dispute, is that "after-acquired knowledge/evidence improves an employer's case in relation to establishing a valid reason for dismissal" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6394" at="[31]" phrase="after-acquired knowledge/evidence improves an employer's case in relation to establishing a valid reason for dismissal" />, drawing on the Full Bench in *APS Group (Placements) Pty Ltd v O'Loughlin* — but such evidence can weaken, not strengthen, the employer's position on procedural fairness, because the employee was never given a chance to respond to it before the decision was made.

In practice: if you discover after the fact that a dismissed employee had also been falsifying records, you can usually still rely on it to support that a valid reason existed. What you can't do is pretend it was part of a fair process at the time — it wasn't, and s.387(b) and (c) will reflect that.

## Practice scenario: the quality sign-off

<Scenario questions={["Has the employer proven dishonest intent, or only that the record was inaccurate?", "What would turn this from a mistake into dishonesty?", "What's missing before deciding?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>This is a fictional scenario. Dev is a quality control inspector at a mid-sized furniture manufacturer, five years' service, no prior warnings. Each finished batch must be checked against a quality checklist before it ships; the form has a box for "100% of units checked" and a box for "sample checked (minimum 1 in 10)".</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Batch 442</span><b>Checklist submitted: "100% of units checked" ticked</b>
      <span>Line footage (CCTV)</span><b>Shows Dev checking roughly 1 in 10 units before signing off</b>
    </div>
    <p>The production manager wants Dev dismissed for "deliberately falsifying a quality record." Dev says that during the previous production surge, his former team leader (no longer with the company) told the team to "just tick the 100% box, it's what head office wants to see, as long as you've done a proper sample" — and that he has ticked the same box the same way on every rushed batch since, without anyone correcting him. Two other inspectors say they were never told this.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Only that the record was inaccurate, so far.</strong> It is not disputed that Dev checked roughly 1 in 10 units but ticked the "100%" box — but that is the same gap between fact and intent as in the Petrie maintenance case above. Dev has a specific, plausible explanation tracing back to an instruction from a named former supervisor, not a vague "I didn't realise".</li>
      <li><strong>What would tip it toward dishonesty:</strong> if Dev had been specifically told, after the former team leader left, that the 100% box must only be ticked after a full check, and continued ticking it anyway; if there were a personal benefit to ticking it (for example, a throughput bonus tied to "fully checked" batches); or if his account of the former team leader's instruction changed under questioning.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> whether the former team leader's instruction can be corroborated (an email, a message, another inspector who heard it directly); why the two other inspectors say they were never told this — were they on a different line, or is Dev's account simply wrong; how many batches this has happened on, and whether that looks like an isolated practice Dev picked up and never had corrected, or something wider across the shift.</li>
    </ol>
    <p style="margin-bottom:6px"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">No corroboration for the claimed instruction, a personal throughput benefit, and an account that shifts under questioning: closer to a genuine dishonesty finding.</div>
      <div class="lo">The instruction is corroborated or plausible, no one ever corrected the practice, and Dev's account stays consistent: an honest misunderstanding of what the checklist required — a training and process fix, not a dismissal for dishonesty.</div>
    </div>
