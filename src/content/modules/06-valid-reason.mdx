<p class="lead">Before anything else, the Commission asks whether there was a valid reason for the dismissal. If the answer is no, the employer usually loses, however good the process was.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain the legal test in one sentence.
- Tell the difference between "we believe it happened" and "we can prove it happened".
- Spot the failure patterns that most often sink a valid reason at the Fair Work Commission.

## The legal test

A valid reason is one that is **sound, defensible or well founded**, and not capricious, fanciful, spiteful or prejudiced <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[28]" phrase="sound, defensible or well founded" />. The reason must relate to the employee's **capacity** (their ability to do the job) or their **conduct** (how they behaved).

<div class="key">
<h3>The working principles</h3>
<p class="source-note">From the Full Bench in <em>Sydney Trains v Hilder</em> [2020] FWCFB 1373, as quoted in <Cite id="[2023] FWC 65" at="[36]" phrase="not capricious, fanciful, spiteful or prejudiced" /> at [36]. <em>Hilder</em> is quoted second-hand and has not been read in the original for this guide — check the quoted principles against the original before relying on them.</p>

<ol class="principles">
<li>A valid reason is one that is sound, defensible and well-founded, and not capricious, fanciful, spiteful or prejudiced.</li>
<li>Where the reason is misconduct, the Commission decides for itself whether the conduct happened and what it involved.</li>
<li>A reason is valid only if the conduct happened <strong>and</strong> it justified termination. A trivial misdemeanour that happened is still not a valid reason.</li>
<li>The conduct doesn't need to be serious enough for summary dismissal to be a valid reason.</li>
<li>Whether the employer had a contractual right to terminate is irrelevant to whether there was a valid reason.</li>
<li>Mitigating factors, like long service or personal circumstances, aren't weighed here. They come in later under <span class="statute">s.387(h)</span>, as part of whether dismissal was proportionate.</li>
</ol>
</div>

**Proving the facts isn't enough.** Dismissal must be a justifiable response. A reason not of sufficient gravity to justify dismissal is not valid <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[30]" phrase="not of sufficient gravity to justify dismissal" />.

**The Commission doesn't stand in the employer's shoes.** It won't ask what it would have done. It asks whether the reason holds up on an objective look at the facts <Cite id="[2026] FWC 218" at="[31]" phrase="will not stand in the shoes of the employer" />.

## Proving the conduct

In a conduct case, the Commission decides **on the evidence in front of it** whether the conduct actually happened, on the balance of probabilities. An employer's honest and reasonable belief after a proper investigation isn't enough <Cite id="[2021] FWC 13" at="[88]" phrase="balance of probabilities" />.

- **Serious allegations need care.** Where the finding turns on disputed conduct like dishonesty or violence, the Commission is careful before making a finding — in one matter this meant weighing "Briginshaw considerations" alongside the employee's own concessions <Cite id="[2023] FWC 961" at="[42]-[45]" phrase="Briginshaw considerations" />.
- **An investigation report isn't proof by itself.** In *Hijazi*, the employer relied on an external report and called none of the alleged participants as witnesses. The Commission found a valid reason only because of the employee's own concessions, "combined with limited reliance" on the report <Cite id="[2021] FWC 13" at="[92]" phrase="limited reliance on the Grime Investigation" />.
- **Your file has to stand up later.** Witness statements, contemporaneous notes, CCTV, system logs and admissions win cases. Opinions and summaries don't.

## Policy breaches and directions

A substantial and wilful breach of policy will often be a valid reason. But a clear breach doesn't automatically make dismissal fair: every case turns on its circumstances — a Full Bench majority said so in *B, C and D v Australian Postal Corporation*, as quoted in <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[12]" phrase="a substantial and wilful breach of a policy will often" />. *B, C and D* has not been read in the original for this guide — check the quote against the original.

When an employee refuses a direction based on a policy, the Commission focuses on **whether the direction was lawful and reasonable**, not on whether it would have chosen the same policy <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5677" at="[13]" phrase="principally concerned with whether the direction is lawful and reasonable" />.

## Performance and capacity

Capacity covers poor performance, physical capacity, loss of a licence or qualification the job needs, and inability to perform the inherent requirements of the role <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3122" at="[74]" phrase="inherent requirements of the job" />.

*Glading v Advizer* shows what a well-built performance case looks like. A broker missed sales targets in 14 of 18 months. The employer:

- set out the role's inherent requirements, which the employee accepted <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3137" at="[63]" phrase="achieving key sales metrics and targets" />;
- produced the numbers month by month <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3137" at="[61]" phrase="failed to meet sales targets for 14 of the months" />;
- answered his "bad leads" argument by showing that colleagues with similar experience received comparable leads;
- gave tailored training, a new manager and two improvement plans.

The Commission found a valid reason related to capacity <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3137" at="[84]" phrase="sound, defensible or well-founded reason to terminate" />. A performance reason is only as strong as the measurement behind it.

## Out-of-hours conduct

Conduct outside work counts only where it has a real connection to the employment. Under *Rose v Telstra* (a 1998 AIRC decision, not read in the original for this guide — check the quote against the original), conduct viewed objectively must be likely to seriously damage the employment relationship or be incompatible with the employee's duty. The test is applied in <Cite id="[2022] FWC 326" at="[54]-[55]" phrase="incompatible with the employee's duty as an employee" />.

The question for advisers: **what is the link to work?** Write that link into the show cause letter.

## How valid reasons fail

These patterns recur in the decisions. Two are from cases where the employee won; the third is a case where the employer's reasoning was undermined by stale information.

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The reason isn't about the employee</h3>
<p>Dismissed because her parents' employment ended, under an arrangement she never agreed to. <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2368" at="[53]-[56]" phrase="no valid reason for the Applicant's dismissal" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Ask: what did <strong>this employee</strong> do, or fail to be able to do?</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The allegation is pitched too high</h3>
<p>Alleged he knowingly lodged false ATO reports; he genuinely believed they were correct. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 3367" at="[58]-[60]" phrase="not satisfied that there was a valid reason for dismissal" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Frame the allegation at the level you can prove. "Knowingly" must be proved too.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Point of principle</span>
<h3>Stale or incomplete information</h3>
<p>Dismissed under a site-access policy after rules had eased, without checking which sites still required it. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2758" at="[99]-[101]" phrase="did not have complete information" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Re-check the facts on the day you decide.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won, narrowly</span>
<h3>A report, not first-hand evidence</h3>
<p>No alleged participant gave evidence. The employer survived on the employee's own concessions. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 13" at="[92]" phrase="limited reliance on the Grime Investigation" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Keep witnesses available and statements signed.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the pre-start checklist

<Scenario questions={["Is there a valid reason yet?", "What's missing?", "How would you frame the allegation?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Jordan is a maintenance technician with six years' service and no prior warnings, at a logistics depot. Site rules require a pre-start inspection of each light vehicle before use, recorded on an electronic checklist.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>06:02</span><b>Pre-start checklist submitted, LV-14</b>
      <span>06:40</span><b>LV-14 unlocked (telematics)</b>
    </div>
    <p>The supervisor wants Jordan dismissed for "deliberately falsifying safety records". An internal investigator interviewed the supervisor and reviewed the telematics. Jordan wasn't interviewed. Informally, Jordan says the crew routinely completes the checklist in the crib room before walking to the vehicles, and the supervisor has seen it.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Not yet.</strong> The checklist was submitted before the inspection could have happened. But "deliberate falsification" goes to intent, and nothing has tested that yet — that's the same trap as the ATO case above.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> Jordan's account; whether the crib-room practice is widespread and known to supervisors; whether the inspection was done at 06:40; how other crew members' checklists compare; and any past discipline for the same thing.</li>
      <li><strong>Framing:</strong> "You submitted the LV-14 pre-start checklist at 6:02 am, before the vehicle was accessed at 6:40 am, contrary to the pre-start procedure." Add dishonesty only if the evidence supports it.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Deliberate, isolated, safety-critical: likely a valid reason for dismissal.</div>
      <div class="lo">Widespread practice the supervisor knew about: a written warning and a crew-wide reset of the procedure.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
