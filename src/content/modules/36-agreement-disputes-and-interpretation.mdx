<p class="lead">Most of an ER adviser's Commission work isn't about dismissal. It's about what an enterprise agreement means: whether an allowance is payable, how leave loading is calculated, whether a roster change needed consultation. Those disputes usually reach the Commission under s.739, through the agreement's own dispute resolution term. This module covers how the Commission gets jurisdiction, the objections an employer can properly raise, and how an agreement is interpreted when the parties can't agree.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain where the Commission's power in a s.739 dispute comes from, and why the agreement's dispute term sets its limits.
- Check an application against the jurisdictional questions: the kind of dispute, who brought it, whether the steps were followed, and whether arbitration is allowed.
- Build an interpretation argument the way the Commission reads agreements: text first, then context and purpose, assessed objectively.
- Recognise when a matter belongs in a different jurisdiction, such as s.65B or a court.

## Where the power comes from

Every enterprise agreement must contain a term with a procedure for settling disputes (s.186(6)). Section 738 picks up those terms, and s.739 applies when the term requires or allows the Commission to deal with a dispute.

The key point is that **the agreement, not the Act, defines the Commission's role.** In *Transport Workers' Union v FedEx Express Australia*, the Commission explained that, where an agreement provides a procedure for settling a dispute, <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1293" at="[17]" phrase="the Commission's powers to deal with that dispute are limited by the relevant dispute resolution term" />. Section 739 then sets four further rules:

- the Commission **must not exercise any powers limited by the term** (s.739(3));
- it may **arbitrate** only if, in accordance with the term, the parties have agreed it may do so (s.739(4)). Otherwise it may still mediate, conciliate, make a recommendation or express an opinion (s.595(2));
- it **must not make a decision inconsistent with the Act, or a fair work instrument** that applies to the parties (s.739(5)); and
- it may deal with a dispute **only on application by a party** to the dispute (s.739(6)).

Section 740 applies the same limits where the term allows a person other than the Commission, such as a private arbitrator, to deal with the dispute.

So the first document to read in any s.739 matter is the dispute term itself. Read it as carefully as the clause the dispute is about.

## The jurisdictional questions

An employer can properly test whether the Commission has jurisdiction. These objections succeed or fail on the wording of the particular term, so check each one against the clause rather than assuming a general rule.

### Is it a dispute the term covers?

Terms differ. Some cover any dispute about the agreement, the NES or the employment relationship. Others cover only disputes about the application of the agreement. In *FedEx*, the employer argued that a dispute about withholding wages after a work health and safety stop-work direction was really about the Act, not the agreement. It also argued that deciding the dispute would involve the Commission exercising judicial power. On the terms of that agreement's clause, the Commission held: <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1293" at="[88]" phrase="Regarding the question of whether the dispute is arbitrable I find that it is" />. It also found that dealing with the dispute <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1293" at="[91]" phrase="does not involve an impermissible exercise of judicial power" />.

The Commission can interpret laws and instruments in the course of arbitrating. *FedEx* quotes High Court authority that <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1293" at="[85]" phrase="there is no substance in the suggestion that an industrial tribunal cannot interpret laws, awards and other legal instruments" />. What it can't do is make a binding declaration of legal rights, the way a court does. Raise a judicial-power objection only where the relief sought actually asks for that.

### Who can bring it, and were the steps followed?

Most dispute terms set out steps before the Commission is involved, often discussion with a supervisor and then more senior management. In *Cox v The Florey Institute of Neuroscience and Mental Health*, the employer argued that the individual applicants <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1610" at="[17]" phrase="must have followed the procedure in Clause 43.3" /> themselves. Their union had raised the dispute. The Commission read "party initiating the dispute" as a phrase that <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1610" at="[53]" phrase="should be understood as distinguishing between a complainant (whether an individual employee or the union) and the employer" />. It added that <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1610" at="[54]" phrase="the individual who is at the centre of the dispute should not be deprived of agency and ownership over an issue that affects them" />. The Commission was <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1610" at="[55]" phrase="satisfied that the Commission has jurisdiction to deal with Ms Cox's and Ms Survase's dispute" />.

Where the steps genuinely haven't been followed, the Commission won't skip them. In *Zammit v Sydney Water Corporation*, the employee asked for his flexible work dispute to be arbitrated under the agreement instead of s.65B. The Commission said <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[23]" phrase="The dispute settlement process would first need to have been followed" />, and that it would not <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[24]" phrase="substitute one type of application for another to overcome a limit on its jurisdiction" />.

**The adviser's question:** if you're going to object, is the objection about what the clause actually says, or is it a technical point the Commission is likely to read past? Objections that only delay a dispute the Commission will hear anyway cost credibility for the hearing that follows.

### Can the Commission arbitrate?

Check whether the term allows arbitration, and on what conditions. Some terms allow it as of right after conciliation fails. Others need both parties' agreement at the time. Some exclude particular subjects. If arbitration isn't available, the Commission can still conciliate and express an opinion, and an opinion from the Commission often settles a matter in practice.

### Agree the questions early

Arbitration usually proceeds by answering defined questions. In *FedEx*, <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1293" at="[12]" phrase="the parties were unable to reach an agreed position on the questions to be arbitrated" />, and the jurisdictional objections then had to be decided first. Proposing a fair, precise set of questions early is often the most useful thing an employer adviser can do. It narrows the dispute and makes the hearing shorter.

## Interpreting the agreement

Most s.739 arbitrations come down to what a clause means. The Commission applies the principles set out by a Full Bench in *AMWU v Berri*, which later decisions restate. As quoted in *AMWU and CEPU v Lactalis Australia YD*:

- construction <Cite id="[2025] FWC 994" at="[22]" phrase="begins with a consideration of the ordinary meaning of the relevant words" />, read in the context of the agreement as a whole, the clause's place in it, and the legislative context;
- the task <Cite id="[2025] FWC 994" at="[22]" phrase="does not involve rewriting the agreement to achieve what might be regarded as a fair or just outcome" />; and
- the parties' common intention is identified objectively, <Cite id="[2025] FWC 994" at="[22]" phrase="without regard to the subjective intentions or expectations of the parties" />.

Agreements are read differently from modern awards. In the same decision, the Commission noted that <Cite id="[2025] FWC 994" at="[24]" phrase="evidence of surrounding circumstances may be used to assist the construction of an agreement but is limited to evidence" /> that tends to establish objective facts. Where an agreement incorporates award terms, the parties in *Lactalis* disagreed about which set of principles applied to those terms. Check that question in any agreement that incorporates an award.

### What that means for preparing a case

- **Start with the words.** Read the clause, the definitions, and every other clause that uses the same expression. Many disputes are settled by a definition or a neighbouring clause.
- **Look at the history of the clause.** Earlier versions in previous agreements, and how the wording changed, may help as objective context.
- **Separate objective facts from what people intended.** What the employer's negotiator meant a clause to do is not evidence of its meaning. Facts known to both sides when the agreement was made may be. Bargaining records matter here, so keep them well.
- **Don't argue fairness as meaning.** "It would be unfair to pay the allowance on leave" is not an argument about what the clause says.
- **Check the payroll practice before relying on it.** Whether conduct after the agreement was made can help interpret it is contested. In *Lactalis*, the unions relied on it and the employer said it was irrelevant. Either way, a long practice that supports the other side's reading is a risk to assess early.

## When s.739 is the wrong route

- **Flexible work requests** have their own process under s.65B ([Module 35](/modules/flexible-work-requests-and-disputes)). The agreement's dispute term may also apply, but each route has its own requirements, as *Zammit* shows.
- **Dismissals** are generally dealt with under the unfair dismissal or general protections provisions, not as agreement disputes. See [Module 22](/modules/commission-proceedings).
- **Underpayment claims that need enforcement or penalties** belong in a court. The Commission can't impose civil penalties under s.739, though an arbitrated answer about what the agreement requires often resolves the underlying money question.

## What the Commission looks for

- **A dispute that fits the term**, raised by someone the term allows to raise it, after the steps the term requires.
- **An argument about the words**, read in the context of the whole agreement, not about what either side hoped the clause would do.
- **Objective evidence of context**, not witnesses' views of what they intended in bargaining.
- **Clear questions to answer**, ideally agreed between the parties.

## Practice scenario: the travel allowance

<Scenario questions={["What should you read first, and why?", "Is there a jurisdictional objection worth making?", "How would you frame the interpretation argument?", "What questions would you propose for arbitration?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>An agreement pays a "travel allowance" to employees "required to report to a work location other than their usual work location". The employer moved a maintenance crew's start point from one depot to another 20 kilometres away for a six-month project and didn't pay the allowance, on the basis that the new depot had become their usual work location.</p>
    <p>The union has filed a s.739 application. The agreement's dispute term requires discussion with the supervisor, then with the site manager, before referral to the Commission, and allows arbitration if conciliation fails. Two crew members raised it with their supervisor. Nobody spoke to the site manager before the union filed.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>The dispute term,</strong> because it sets the Commission's powers: what it covers, who can refer, the steps, and whether arbitration is available.</li>
      <li><strong>Possibly, but think twice.</strong> The site-manager step was missed. That may justify asking for the matter to be referred back for that discussion, but it won't make the dispute go away, and the Commission may treat it as a technicality if the union and employees have plainly raised it. Offering the site-manager meeting promptly is usually better than a contested objection.</li>
      <li><strong>Interpretation:</strong> start with the words. "Usual work location" isn't defined. Look at how the expression is used elsewhere in the agreement and in earlier agreements, and at the objective facts about the temporary project. Avoid arguing that paying the allowance would be unfair or that the negotiators never meant it to apply.</li>
      <li><strong>Questions:</strong> for example, (1) whether, on the proper construction of the clause, the crew's usual work location changed when they were directed to start at the new depot for the project; and (2) if not, whether the travel allowance was payable for each day they reported there.</li>
    </ol>
