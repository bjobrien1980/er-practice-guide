<p class="lead">ss.387(f) and (g) ask whether the employer's size and HR resourcing should soften what procedural fairness required. s.387(h) is the catch-all — and in practice it's where most of the real argument about fairness actually happens.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain what ss.387(f)-(g) actually test, and why a small, HR-less employer gets some latitude but not a free pass.
- Identify the things that only get weighed under s.387(h) — proportionality, mitigating factors, consistency of treatment — and say why they don't belong under s.387(a).
- Build and respond to a s.387(h) argument the way the Commission does: against the gravity of the conduct, not in the abstract.

## Size and HR resourcing — ss.387(f) and (g)

The Commission must take into account the degree to which the size of the employer's enterprise, and the degree to which the absence of dedicated human resource management specialists or expertise, would be **likely to impact on the procedures followed** in effecting the dismissal.

For a genuinely small, HR-less employer, that latitude is real. In one case, a sales manager at a two-person tile wholesaler was dismissed after the business relationship between him and the general manager — "best mates" outside work — broke down. The Commission found the business's size meant it had no HR or specialist expertise to guide the process, and that this — together with the personal rather than professional nature of the relationship — was **likely why there was no procedural fairness in relation to the dismissal** <Cite id="[2023] FWC 927" at="[25]" phrase="no human resources or other specialist expertise" />. Similarly, an office coordinator at a small catering company with no HR specialists was found to have had her (minimal) process **adversely affected** by that absence <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4929" at="[13]" phrase="adverse effect on the procedures that were followed" />.

Latitude is not immunity, though. In both of those matters, the dismissal was still found unfair — the small size and absent HR function explained the lack of procedure, but didn't excuse it <Cite id="[2023] FWC 927" at="[28]" phrase="entirely lacking in procedural fairness" />.

At the other end, ss.387(f)-(g) simply don't engage. A large employer with its own HR function will typically find these factors neutral <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2203" at="[81]" phrase="would be likely to impact on the procedures followed in effecting Mr Longobardi's dismissal" />. And the test is narrower than it might look: it asks only whether HR expertise was **absent**, not whether the HR specialist who was there did a good job. In one matter the Commission found the employer's HR function had failed badly to inform itself of its own obligations — but that went to a different part of the analysis, because "the consideration is not whether the dedicated human resource management specialist handled the matter poorly or gave incorrect advice" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5905" at="[124]-[125]" phrase="is not whether the dedicated human resource management specialist handled the matter poorly" />. That employer was not small and had no absence of HR expertise that impacted its procedures <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5905" at="[137]" phrase="there is no absence of a dedicated human resource management specialist" /> — so ss.387(f)-(g) gave it nothing to lean on when its process still fell short.

A small employer with genuinely no HR capacity can also find these factors treated as neutral rather than exculpatory, where the Commission considers they simply don't bear on the actual unfairness found <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1229" at="[92]-[93]" phrase="do not weigh in the consideration of whether the dismissal was harsh, unjust or unreasonable" />.

## s.387(h): the catch-all that does most of the work

Section 387(h) lets the Commission weigh **any other matters it considers relevant** — and it is deliberately broad <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2203" at="[83]" phrase="provides the Commission with a broad scope to consider any other matters" />. Three things recur here more than anything else: proportionality, mitigating factors, and consistency of treatment. A fourth, residual category covers procedural matters that don't fit neatly under ss.387(b)-(e), such as the quality of an investigation.

### Proportionality: is dismissal the right-sized response?

Even where there's a valid reason, s.387(h) asks whether dismissal was a **proportionate** response to it. One decision put this directly: "the proportionality of the dismissal to the conduct that is the subject of the valid reason is a matter to be considered in connection with section 387(h)" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1229" at="[95]" phrase="a matter to be considered in connection with section 387(h)" />. Having found a valid reason, the Commission there went on to separately find the dismissal **proportionate to her conduct and performance shortcomings** <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1229" at="[95]" phrase="dismissal was proportionate to her conduct and performance shortcomings" />.

The same idea recurs, almost as a formula, wherever a dismissal is summary: the proportionality of the summary nature of the dismissal "must be weighed against the gravity of" the conduct relied on <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2819" at="[70]" phrase="must be weighed against the gravity of his conduct" /> <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2203" at="[88]" phrase="must be weighed against the gravity of his conduct" />. The classical statement of why proportionality matters to "harsh" specifically — as opposed to "unjust" or "unreasonable" — comes from the High Court in *Byrne v Australian Airlines Ltd*, quoted in one FWC decision: a termination may be harsh in its consequences for the employee, "or because it is disproportionate to the gravity of the misconduct" relied on <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5905" at="[85]" phrase="disproportionate to the gravity of the misconduct" />. *Byrne* is a pre-FWC High Court authority and isn't independently in this corpus — check the quote against the original before relying on it.

### Mitigating factors: not weighed under (a), only here

Long service, an otherwise clean record, and personal or compassionate circumstances don't affect whether there was a valid reason — that question is about the conduct or capacity itself. They come in only once the Commission turns to s.387(h). Sometimes they tip the outcome. A university lecturer dismissed for underperformance was found to have been treated harshly once the Commission weighed her **over 14 years of otherwise unblemished service**, the career damage of a performance dismissal, her very limited prospects of comparable alternative employment, and her sole caring responsibility for elderly parents alongside a mortgage <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[119]" phrase="14 years of otherwise unblemished service" />. On the strength of those s.387(h) factors, the Commission found the dismissal **harsh and unreasonable** <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[120]" phrase="harsh and unreasonable" /> and ordered reinstatement with a 'fair go all round' <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[136]" phrase="fair go all round" />, an outcome a Full Bench majority later left undisturbed by refusing permission to appeal <Cite id="[2020] FWCFB 3571" at="[65]-[66]" phrase="permission to appeal is refused" />.

But weighing mitigating factors doesn't guarantee they'll move the result. A football governing body's Technical Advisor, summarily dismissed for taking on a conflicting coaching role, also had his personal and economic circumstances considered under s.387(h) — a young family, a difficult job market in the middle of COVID-19 <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2819" at="[74]" phrase="significant impact on his personal and economic situation" />. Here, though, those circumstances didn't outweigh the gravity of the conduct, and the dismissal stood <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2819" at="[76]" phrase="was not harsh, unjust or unreasonable" />. The lesson for advisers: identify the mitigating factors early and be honest about their likely weight — they're a genuine consideration, not an automatic trump card.

### Consistency of treatment

Where comparable employees were treated differently for the same conduct, that can bear on fairness under s.387(h) — but only if the comparison actually holds up. The leading formulation, quoted (and quoted again within that quote) in one FWC decision, requires "sufficient evidence of the circumstances of the allegedly comparable cases to enable a proper comparison to be made", so the Commission is genuinely "comparing 'apples with apples'" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2819" at="[68]" phrase="ensure that it is comparing 'apples with apples'" />. The same quoted material holds that an unexplained disparity in treatment doesn't, by itself, make an otherwise justified dismissal unfair — there has to be some basis for finding the comparison genuinely apt <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2819" at="[68]" phrase="troubled by the apparent disparity in the treatment" />.

Applying that test, the Commission in the same matter went through nine examples the employee had put forward as comparators and rejected every one, because none involved an employee in a comparably conflicted position — they were **not comparable to his own** <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2819" at="[69]" phrase="are comparable to his own" />. The lesson: a bare assertion that "others weren't sacked for this" is not a consistency argument. It needs a genuinely matched comparator, with evidence of what that other case actually involved.

### Procedural matters not elsewhere captured — e.g. investigation quality

Section 387(h)'s breadth also catches procedural complaints that don't fit under ss.387(b)-(e) — most commonly, the quality or fairness of an internal investigation, distinct from whether the underlying conduct happened. In one matter, an employee argued the investigation into his conduct was not fair or impartial because people he'd previously complained about were initially involved; the Commission accepted the employer had appropriately handed the investigation to an uninvolved senior manager, and found it **conducted a fair and impartial investigation** <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2203" at="[84]-[85]" phrase="conducted a fair and impartial investigation" />.

## How ss.387(f)-(g) and s.387(h) interact

Put together, the pattern across these decisions is consistent: a small employer's lack of HR resourcing can explain poor process, and the Commission will say so under ss.387(f)-(g) — but it is not, on its own, a reason to find the dismissal fair. Nor does having an HR team automatically protect a large employer: ss.387(f)-(g) test the *absence* of expertise, not its quality, so a large employer whose HR function performs poorly gets no credit under these factors and has to answer for the shortfall elsewhere in the s.387 analysis.

## How these arguments fail

<h3>Mitigating factors outweighed the rest of the case</h3>
<p>14 years' otherwise unblemished service, limited alternative employment and caring responsibilities tipped the result to harsh, despite other findings against the employee. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 416" at="[119]" phrase="14 years of otherwise unblemished service" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Mitigating factors under s.387(h) can decide the case on their own. Gather them properly — length of service, record, personal circumstances — don't leave them as an afterthought.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>No HR team didn't excuse no process</h3>
<p>A two-person business with no HR expertise explained why there was no procedure — but a valid reason plus zero procedural fairness was still unreasonable. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 927" at="[28]" phrase="entirely lacking in procedural fairness" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Small-business latitude under ss.387(f)-(g) narrows the gap; it doesn't close it. Even the smallest employer needs to tell the person what the problem is and hear their side.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>A large HR function is not a shield</h3>
<p>The employer had its own dedicated HR specialist, so ss.387(f)-(g) gave it nothing — and its HR function's failure to properly inform itself of its obligations left no valid reason and an unfair dismissal. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 5905" at="[139]" phrase="harsh, unjust and unreasonable" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Don't treat "we have HR" as itself an answer to a fairness complaint. The question is whether HR actually got it right, and that's judged like anything else.</p>

<h3>Comparator cases need genuine evidence, not assertion</h3>
<p>Nine examples of other employees in supposedly similar dual roles were raised as inconsistent treatment; all nine were rejected as not properly comparable, and the dismissal stood. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2819" at="[69]" phrase="are comparable to his own" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Before running a consistency argument, check that the comparator really did the same thing in the same circumstances — not just something that sounds similar.</p>

## Practice scenario: the long-serving kitchen hand

<Scenario questions={["What ss.387(f)-(g) considerations apply here, and which way do they cut?", "What s.387(h) factors are in play, and how would you weigh them?", "Is dismissal a proportionate response on what's known so far?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Fictional scenario. Mariko has worked as a kitchen hand at a four-outlet suburban café group for eleven years, with a clean record and strong reviews from every site manager she's worked under. The group has no dedicated HR function — rosters, pay and discipline are all handled directly by the two co-owners.</p>
    <p>Yesterday, Mariko was caught on CCTV disabling a fridge temperature alarm during a busy lunch service rather than stopping to deal with it, in breach of the food-safety policy she signed when she started. No food was ultimately spoiled. One of the owners wants to dismiss her immediately for "a serious safety breach," citing a near-identical case 18 months ago where a casual kitchen hand was dismissed on the spot for ignoring a different alarm.</p>
    <p>Mariko, when asked informally, says she silenced the alarm because she thought it was faulty (it had false-alarmed twice that week) and intended to log it with the other owner at the end of the shift, which she did. She has a teenage daughter with a disability and is the sole carer; losing this job on short notice would mean losing the flexible hours that make that caring role workable.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>ss.387(f)-(g):</strong> This is a genuinely small, HR-less employer, so some procedural latitude applies — the owners can't be held to the standard of a large HR department. But that latitude only explains an informal process; it doesn't permit skipping a conversation with Mariko about what happened and why, before any decision is made.</li>
      <li><strong>s.387(h) factors:</strong> Eleven years' clean service and her caring responsibilities are real mitigating factors to weigh if a valid reason is otherwise made out. The earlier dismissal of the casual kitchen hand is a possible consistency argument — but only if that case is actually comparable (same conduct, same risk, same context), which hasn't yet been tested. Asserting "we did it before" isn't enough on its own.</li>
      <li><strong>Proportionality:</strong> Not yet assessable on what's known. If Mariko's account holds up (reasonable belief the alarm was faulty, intent to report, no food actually at risk, and she did in fact report it), dismissal looks disproportionate to the gravity of what occurred. If the alarm silencing was in fact reckless and she didn't report it, the calculus changes substantially.</li>
    </ol>
    <p style="margin-bottom:6px"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what further inquiry shows:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Alarm genuinely posed a live food-safety risk and Mariko didn't report it: dismissal may be proportionate despite her service and circumstances.</div>
      <div class="lo">Alarm was a known nuisance fault, she reported it promptly, and the comparator case doesn't hold up: dismissal is likely to be found harsh once s.387(h) factors are weighed, even if a technical breach occurred.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
