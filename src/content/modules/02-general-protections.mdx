<p class="lead">General protections claims don't ask whether a dismissal was fair. They ask whether it was tainted by a prohibited reason — and once the employee shows enough, the employer has to prove it wasn't.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Identify the four general protections doors that most often open onto a dismissal: workplace rights, industrial activity, discrimination, and the reverse onus that sits over all of them.
- Explain why the reverse onus in s.361 makes a general protections risk assessment completely different from an unfair dismissal one.
- Spot the fact patterns — evidence from the real decision-maker, or the absence of it — that decide these cases in practice.

## Four doors into Part 3-1

General protections dismissal disputes usually turn on one or more of the following. Each is a separate legal question, but they share the same structure: a protected thing the employee did or has, and an adverse action taken because of it.

**Workplace rights (s.340).** A person must not take adverse action against another person because the other person has, has exercised, or proposes to exercise a workplace right <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1138" at="[22]" phrase="A person must not take adverse action against another person" />. A workplace right includes being able to make a complaint or inquiry in relation to one's employment <Cite id="[2024] FWC 840" at="[21]" phrase="is able to make a complaint or inquiry" /> — and whether a communication counts as a complaint is a matter of substance, not form; the employee doesn't need to use any particular words <Cite id="[2024] FWC 840" at="[23]" phrase="a matter of substance, not form" />.

**Industrial activity (s.346).** An employer must not take adverse action against a person because the person is or is not a member of an industrial association, or engages or proposes to engage in industrial activity within the meaning of s.347 — which includes seeking, or not seeking, union representation <Cite id="[2023] FWC 806" at="[96]" phrase="is not a member of an industrial association" />. The protection runs both ways: it protects employees who join in industrial action and employees who refuse to.

**Discrimination (s.351).** An employer must not take adverse action against an employee because of a protected attribute — race, sex, age, disability, religion, political opinion and the rest of the list in s.351(1) <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2822" at="[83]" phrase="because of the person's political opinion" />. This ground is narrower than it looks: s.351(2)-(3) ties it to the general anti-discrimination regimes, so conduct that wouldn't contravene state, territory or federal anti-discrimination law generally won't contravene s.351 either <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2822" at="[84]" phrase="does not follow that conduct which contravenes general anti-discrimination laws" />.

**The adverse action itself (s.342).** Dismissal is always adverse action, but so is injuring the employee in their employment or altering their position to their prejudice <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2475" at="[39]" phrase="alters the position of the employee to the employee's prejudice" />.

## The reverse onus: why this changes everything

In an unfair dismissal case, the employee has to prove the dismissal was harsh, unjust or unreasonable. In a general protections case, s.361 flips that. Once the employee establishes the protected right, activity or attribute, and that adverse action was taken, it is presumed the action was taken for that reason — **unless the employer proves otherwise** <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2475" at="[41]" phrase="unless the person proves otherwise" />.

This isn't a minor procedural quirk. Section 361 creates a statutory presumption that operates whenever it's alleged a person contravened s.340 or a cognate provision like s.346 — a distillation by Wigney J of the High Court's reasoning in *Barclay* and *BHP Coal* <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2475" at="[42]" phrase="s 361 creates a statutory presumption" />. *Barclay* and *BHP Coal* are High Court authorities and have not been read in the original for this guide — see the note under Decisions cited.

**But the onus doesn't shift for free.** The employee still has to establish the protected right, activity or attribute, and the adverse action, as objective facts before s.361 does any work for them <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3264" at="[17]" phrase="needs to establish the existence of the circumstance as an objective fact" />. An applicant who files no evidence at all gets nothing from s.361 — in one matter, an applicant was given repeated extensions and warnings but never filed any material, and the Commission held there was no evidence she had exercised a workplace right at all, so it was unnecessary for the employer to even attempt to discharge the reverse onus <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1138" at="[27]" phrase="there is no evidence before the Commission that Ms Loy exercised a workplace right" />. The presumption answers "why," not "whether."

**The reverse onus also has a boundary.** It applies to the *reason* for an adverse action, not to the jurisdictional question of whether a dismissal happened at all. A casual employee who wasn't offered further shifts still had to prove, on the ordinary onus, that he was "dismissed" within the meaning of s.386 before s.361 could ever come into play on the reason for it <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1685" at="[45]" phrase="the reverse onus does not apply to the question as to whether or not the Applicant was dismissed in the first place" />.

The reverse onus is not a separate protected category and it does not relieve an applicant of proving the underlying statutory facts. First identify the workplace right, industrial activity or protected attribute and the adverse action. Only then ask what evidence the decision-maker can give about the reason. The selected “doors” in this module are screening questions, not an exhaustive list of Part 3-1 risks.

## What actually wins and loses these cases

Once the onus is engaged, the question becomes evidentiary: can the employer show, through the actual decision-maker, what the real reason was? Two decisions on almost identical facts — an employee who asked about unpaid entitlements, then lost their job — show both ends of this.

In one, the decision-maker herself gave evidence under cross-examination that was "consistent and withstood scrutiny," and the Commission accepted the real reason was the business's financial position, not the entitlement inquiry; the respondent discharged the onus <Cite id="[2024] FWC 840" at="[33]" phrase="discharged the onus of proving that no part of that action was taken for the prohibited reason" />. In another, two decision-makers gave specific, detailed, first-hand evidence about the actual events that led to dismissal — so specific that the suggestion their decision was really about the employee's disability was found to lack "any basis" <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2475" at="[47]" phrase="lacks any basis, and was understandably considered offensive" />, and the respondent comprehensively discharged the burden <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2475" at="[43]" phrase="comprehensively and completely discharged that burden" />.

The lesson for advisers: **the person who actually made the decision has to be able to give evidence, with specifics, of their own reasons.** An HR summary, a board paper, or a colleague's recollection of what someone else decided is not a substitute.

## How general protections claims fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The decision-maker never turned up</h3>
<p>An employee who complained about a co-worker and disclosed a pre-existing medical condition was told his role was "redundant." The actual decision-maker gave no witness statement and didn't attend the hearing; the person who did attend gave only unsupported verbal submissions. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 777" at="[26]" phrase="failed to provide any notable evidence to discharge the reverse onus of proof" /></p>
<p class="lesson">If the decision-maker won't or can't give evidence, the reverse onus is very hard to discharge.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employee lost</span>
<h3>The presumption isn't a substitute for evidence</h3>
<p>An applicant alleged dismissal for exercising a workplace right but, despite repeated directions, warnings and an extension of time, filed no evidence at all. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1138" at="[28]" phrase="unnecessary, for Indigo Lawyers to discharge the reverse onus of proof" /></p>
<p class="lesson">The reverse onus rewards an employee who proves their case. It does nothing for one who doesn't try.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won, narrowly</span>
<h3>Specificity beat suspicion</h3>
<p>A dismissal followed closely on an employee's disclosure of autism and a run of client complaints. The employer's two decision-makers gave granular, consistent evidence of each incident relied on. <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2475" at="[47]" phrase="lacks any basis, and was understandably considered offensive" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A tight timeline against the employee is survivable — but only with contemporaneous, specific, first-hand evidence.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Reasoning undermined</span>
<h3>The onus answers the wrong question</h3>
<p>A casual worker argued the reverse onus helped prove he'd been dismissed after being told there was "no work." The Commission held the reverse onus only bears on *why* adverse action was taken — not on whether a dismissal occurred at all. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1685" at="[45]" phrase="the reverse onus does not apply to the question as to whether or not the Applicant was dismissed in the first place" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Check the jurisdictional facts first. The reverse onus never gets there if there's no dismissal to begin with.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the Riverside Distribution redundancy

<Scenario questions={["Has the s.361 reverse onus been engaged on these facts?", "What parallel pathway or protected activity might matter?", "What non-dismissal outcome remains open?", "What is missing from the employer's position right now?", "What would you tell the business to do before finalising the decision?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Riverside Distribution is a fictional mid-size logistics company running a regional warehouse. Amara has worked there for three years as a picker/packer. Four weeks ago she raised a written safety complaint about a forklift near-miss in her aisle, copying the warehouse manager and the WHS representative. Two weeks ago, in a one-on-one meeting, she mentioned to her team leader that she had recently been diagnosed with generalised anxiety disorder and asked for a quieter workstation away from the loading dock.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Week 1</span><b>Amara lodges written forklift safety complaint</b>
      <span>Week 3</span><b>Amara discloses anxiety diagnosis, requests workstation change</b>
      <span>Week 4</span><b>Operations Director tells HR to "action the Riverside headcount review" for Amara's role</b>
    </div>
    <p>This week, HR sent Amara a letter saying her position has been made redundant "as part of a broader restructure of the warehouse floor." No other picker/packer role was removed. The Operations Director, who made the call, is not named in the letter and has not spoken to Amara directly. HR's file contains only a one-line email from the Operations Director: "action the Riverside headcount review for Amara's role — budget pressure."</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Not yet, on what we can see — but it's close.</strong> Amara would need to establish, as objective facts, that she exercised a workplace right (the safety complaint, and arguably the accommodation request) and that adverse action was taken. On these facts that looks straightforward: a complaint and a disclosure, followed by a termination, with only one role cut. Once she does that, s.361 presumes the redundancy was for those reasons unless Riverside proves otherwise.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> any evidence from the actual decision-maker (the Operations Director) beyond a one-line email; any contemporaneous business case for the "headcount review" predating Amara's complaint and disclosure; any explanation of why her role specifically, rather than another picker/packer role, was selected; and any record of how the restructure criteria were applied.</li>
      <li><strong>Before finalising anything:</strong> get a signed, detailed statement from the Operations Director setting out exactly when the restructure decision was made, on what financial or operational basis, and how role selection was decided — ideally with documents (budgets, org charts, a business case) that predate the safety complaint. If that evidence doesn't exist or can't be produced, escalate before the letter goes out: on the two confirmed decisions in this area, a thin, after-the-fact explanation from someone other than the real decision-maker is the single biggest reason employers lose these cases.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Genuine, pre-existing, documented restructure with a transparent selection process: defensible, though still a live risk given the timing.</div>
      <div class="lo">No contemporaneous business case, and the Operations Director can't or won't give first-hand evidence: very likely indefensible.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
