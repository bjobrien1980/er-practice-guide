<p class="lead">Section 387(e) only has something to say if the dismissal is actually about performance. Where it does apply, a warning that doesn't do four specific things won't satisfy it — no matter how firmly it was delivered.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Recognise when s.387(e) applies at all, and when it's simply irrelevant.
- Check a warning against the four things it has to do, not just whether one was sent.
- Spot a warning that sounds serious but is too vague, or aimed at the wrong issue, to count.

## When this limb applies — and when it doesn't

Section 387(e) is not a universal PIP or warning requirement. A performance issue still requires analysis of the expected standard, the employee’s actual capacity, role clarity, support and resources, and whether the concern is really performance rather than conduct or medical capacity. A PIP may be useful, but its necessity depends on the facts and any applicable instrument or policy.

Section 387(e) asks: **if the dismissal relates to unsatisfactory performance, had the person been warned about that unsatisfactory performance before the dismissal?** If a dismissal relates to unsatisfactory performance, s.387(e) requires the Commission to consider whether the employee was warned about it before dismissal <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1966" at="[81]" phrase="requires the Commission to consider whether the employee has been warned" />.

That opening condition matters more than advisers often give it credit for. In *Szkwarek v Omega Network*, the employer dismissed Mr Szkwarek for lateness and failing to notify his supervisor, after a "final warning" about the same behaviour. The applicant argued he hadn't been warned specifically about "timekeeping" as a performance issue. The Commission disagreed with the framing entirely: lateness, failing to notify, and failing to attend a disciplinary meeting were **matters of conduct rather than performance** <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1966" at="[84]" phrase="matters of conduct rather than performance" />. Because the dismissal wasn't for unsatisfactory performance, s.387(e) simply didn't bite — the Commission only went on, in the alternative, to find that he'd been warned anyway. The application was dismissed <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1966" at="[100]" phrase="the application is therefore dismissed" />.

**The adviser's first question isn't "was there a warning?" — it's "is this actually a performance case?"** Module 5 covers that capacity/conduct line in full. If the real reason is conduct, don't reach for this limb at all; arguing it only invites a finding like *Szkwarek*'s.

## What a warning actually has to do

Where the dismissal is genuinely about performance, the Commission applies a four-part test, repeated across many decisions: a warning for the purposes of s.387(e) must clearly identify the areas of deficiency in the employee's performance, the assistance or training that might be provided, the standards required, and a reasonable timeframe to meet them <Cite id="[2022] FWC 520" at="[51]-[53]" phrase="the areas of deficiency in the employee's performance" />.

On top of that checklist, the warning must also **make it clear that the employee's employment is at risk unless the performance issue identified is addressed** — it is not enough for the employer to merely exhort the employee to do better <Cite id="[2022] FWC 520" at="[52]" phrase="not sufficient for the employer merely to exhort their employee" />.

<ol class="principles">
<li>Clearly identify the areas of deficiency in the employee's performance.</li>
<li>Identify the assistance or training that might be provided.</li>
<li>State the standard required.</li>
<li>Give a reasonable timeframe within which to meet that standard.</li>
<li>Make it clear that the employee's employment is at risk unless the issue is addressed — exhortation to "do better" isn't enough.</li>
</ol>
</div>

A "final warning" doesn't need any particular form of words. What it can't skip is that last element: the employee has to be able to read it and understand that failing to improve could cost them their job. A warning that lists faults but never says what happens next is doing half the job.

## The warning has to be about the right thing

A warning that exists, and ticks every box above, still won't help the employer if it isn't about the performance problem the employer later relies on to dismiss. The nature of a s.387(e) warning requires a sufficient nexus between the warning and the substantive matter of concern — the warning must be given **about that unsatisfactory performance**, and not in some disassociated context <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2078" at="[129]" phrase="not in some disassociated context" />.

*Taylor v CJD Equipment* shows what satisfying that nexus looks like in practice. The employer issued written warnings raising continuing concerns about the same KPIs and sales targets that ultimately grounded the dismissal. The Commission found the nexus was sufficiently made out, because the warnings were in writing and made in the context of the same continuing concerns that led to termination <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2078" at="[131]" phrase="the nexus is sufficiently made out" />. The dismissal was not unfair <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2078" at="[139]" phrase="the Applicant's dismissal was not unfair" />.

The practical lesson: if you warn someone about missed KPIs in March and dismiss them in June for a completely different, never-raised performance problem, the March warning does nothing for you under s.387(e). Match the warning to the reason, every time you rely on one.

## What the Commission looks for in practice

- **A document, not a memory.** "I told him a few times" rarely survives cross-examination as well as a dated letter or email.
- **Specifics, not adjectives.** "Your performance needs to improve" identifies nothing. Name the task, the metric, the gap.
- **A path, not just a problem.** What training, support or adjusted timeframe is the employer actually offering?
- **The stakes, stated plainly.** The employee should be able to point to a sentence that says their job is at risk.
- **Currency.** A warning from eighteen months ago, about an issue since resolved, won't support a dismissal for a different lapse today.
- **Consistency with the reason given.** The warning and the termination letter should be about the same thing.

<h3>No warning, full stop</h3>
<p>No evidence the employee was ever warned about the alleged performance issues, or told his job was at risk. <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1449" at="[26]" phrase="no evidence that the Applicant was warned about any alleged performance issues" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A performance file with no warning in it is a performance file with a hole in it.</p>

<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>A warning that only exhorts</h3>

## Practice scenario: the quarterly review

  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>This is a fictional scenario. Priya is a customer service team lead at a mid-sized retail chain, two years' service, no prior warnings. Her manager emails her in February after a rough quarter.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Feb</span><b>Email: "Just a heads up, let's lift our numbers this quarter — I know you can do it!"</b>
      <span>Apr</span><b>No further written contact about performance</b>
      <span>Jun</span><b>Dismissal letter: "ongoing underperformance, including call-handling times and customer satisfaction scores"</b>
    </div>
    <p>Priya's call-handling times have been below target since before February. Customer satisfaction scores were not raised with her at any point before the dismissal letter.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>No.</strong> The February email is encouragement, not a warning. It doesn't name call-handling times or customer satisfaction specifically, doesn't offer any training or support, sets no standard or timeframe, and never says her job is at risk — it's the "please do better" problem from <em>Yap v Club Assist</em> above.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> any mention of the actual metrics relied on in June; any support or coaching offered; a clear standard and date to meet it by; a statement that continued underperformance could lead to dismissal. Customer satisfaction scores were never raised at all before the dismissal letter — a brand new issue, with no warning behind it.</li>
      <li><strong>A proper warning should:</strong> name call-handling time and customer satisfaction scores specifically; state the required standard (e.g. "average call-handling time under X minutes by [date]"); offer a concrete step (coaching sessions, a revised script, a review date); and say plainly that failing to meet the standard by that date puts her ongoing employment at risk.</li>
    </ol>
    <p style="margin-bottom:6px"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what else comes out:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">A proper written warning naming both metrics, a real improvement period, and a clear risk-of-dismissal statement, followed by no improvement: a defensible s.387(e) warning.</div>
      <div class="lo">Dismissing now, on the February email alone: very likely no adequate warning under s.387(e), especially for the customer satisfaction scores never previously raised.</div>
    </div>
