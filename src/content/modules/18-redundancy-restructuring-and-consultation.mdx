<p class="lead">A genuine redundancy can't be an unfair dismissal. But "genuine" is a defined term with three parts, and an employer who gets the business case right can still lose the protection by skipping consultation or overlooking a job the employee could have done.</p>

<div class="notice">
<strong>Current-source gate:</strong> before relying on this module, check the current Fair Work Act provisions on genuine redundancy and redundancy pay, the modern award or enterprise agreement that applies, any contractual redundancy or consultation term, and current Commission procedure for any redundancy-pay application. Record the source/check date. Consultation and redeployment requirements are instrument- and fact-sensitive.
</div>

## What you'll be able to do

- Test a proposed redundancy against each of the three parts of s.389, with evidence for each.
- Tell real consultation from a meeting that announces a decision already made.
- Spot the redeployment options an employer has to consider, including vacancies, lower-level roles and work done by contractors.
- Explain what happens when a redundancy is not "genuine" — and why that doesn't decide whether the dismissal was unfair.

## Three questions, all of which must be answered

Whether a dismissal was a case of genuine redundancy is one of the initial matters the Commission must decide before it looks at the merits <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[8]" phrase="requires the Commission to decide whether a dismissal was a case of genuine redundancy before considering the merits" />. [Module 1](/modules/who-can-claim) covers that gateway in outline. This module covers how to get through it, and what happens if you don't.

<div class="key">
<h3>The three questions in s.389</h3>
<ol class="principles">
<li><strong>Is the job gone?</strong> The employer no longer required the person's job to be performed by anyone, because of changes in the operational requirements of its enterprise — <span class="statute">s.389(1)(a)</span>.</li>
<li><strong>Was consultation done?</strong> The employer complied with any obligation in a modern award or enterprise agreement that applied to the employment to consult about the redundancy — <span class="statute">s.389(1)(b)</span>.</li>
<li><strong>Was redeployment reasonable?</strong> The dismissal is not a genuine redundancy if it would have been reasonable in all the circumstances to redeploy the person within the employer's enterprise or an associated entity's — <span class="statute">s.389(2)</span>.</li>
</ol>
</div>

The first two limbs must be established, and redeployment must not have been reasonable in all the circumstances. The test is not about fairness: in one matter the Commission accepted the outcome for a valued employee could be called harsh, but "fairness generally has no role to play in determining whether the dismissal meets the definition" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1315" at="[22]" phrase="fairness generally has no role to play in determining whether the dismissal meets the definition" /> of a genuine redundancy.

## Is the job really gone? (s.389(1)(a))

**It is the job that must go, not the duties.** A job can be redundant even though its tasks still need doing, where they are absorbed by the remaining staff: "a job may no longer be required even if the duties of the job still need to be performed" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1315" at="[10]" phrase="a job may no longer be required even if the duties of the job still need to be performed" />. That is the ordinary result of a restructure that reduces headcount <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1315" at="[10]" phrase="The continued existence of the duties does not demonstrate that the job is still required" />. What is "critical for the purpose of identifying a redundancy is whether the holder of the former position has" any duties left to discharge <Cite id="[2021] FWC 928" at="[57]" phrase="critical for the purpose of identifying a redundancy is whether the holder of the former position has" />. That formulation comes from *Ulan Coal Mines Limited v Howarth*, a Full Bench decision that has not been read in the original for this guide.

**The business decides what it needs.** The Commission does not ask whether the restructure was a good idea. It is "only concerned with whether the employer acted because of changes in its operational requirements" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[29]" phrase="only concerned with whether the employer acted because of changes in its operational requirements" />. The change can be driven from outside (a lost contract, a downturn) or from inside (a reappraisal of how the work should be organised). As one decision put it, quoting an earlier matter, "It is the enterprise that determines what its operational requirements are" <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1393" at="[65]" phrase="It is the enterprise that determines what its operational requirements are" />.

**But it has to be proved.** The employer carries the practical burden of showing the change and its effect on this job. In *Pinto*, the employer relied on two witnesses who had not managed the employee and did not know his duties. The Commission said "More than mere submissions are required that there were operational requirements" <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1393" at="[51]" phrase="More than mere submissions are required that there were operational requirements" />, and found the employer "failed to present evidence of operational requirements" <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1393" at="[66]" phrase="failed to present evidence of operational requirements" />. It did not help that the employee had been told to hand his duties to his junior on his last day, or that he produced an advertisement for a position overseas "reflecting his job description" <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1393" at="[66]" phrase="reflecting his job description" />.

**Who was chosen is a different question.** Once the Commission is satisfied the job is no longer required, it "does not have the power to review which employees were selected to be retained or made redundant" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1315" at="[9]" phrase="does not have the power to review which employees were selected to be retained or made redundant" /> under s.389. That does not make selection risk-free. A selection influenced by a complaint, an injury, parental leave or union activity is a general protections problem, covered in [Module 2](/modules/general-protections), and a redundancy label gives no protection there.

## Consultation: when it applies and what it requires (s.389(1)(b))

**The obligation comes from the award or agreement, not from s.389 itself.** The section only asks whether the employer complied with an obligation that applied. So the first step is coverage, and it should be checked, not assumed. In one matter both parties told the Commission no award applied; the Commissioner doubted that, given the breadth of the relevant award, and tested the process against the award's consultation terms anyway <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1315" at="[14]" phrase="I am satisfied that Service Stream complied with the model award consultation terms" />.

**What the standard clause may require.** Modern awards commonly carry a consultation term for major workplace change, but the exact obligation comes from the instrument that applies. As described in one decision, once the employer made a definite decision to make major changes likely to have significant effects on employees, the applicable clause required notice, discussion of the changes, their effects and "measures that might mitigate the adverse effect of the changes" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[34]" phrase="measures that might mitigate the adverse effect of the changes" />, and relevant information in writing. Enterprise-agreement clauses vary, so read the one that applies.

**The written information may come first under the applicable clause.** In *Graydon*, the clause required written information before discussions. The Commission held that the obligation to provide information in writing must "be satisfied before any discussions were held with employees" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1905" at="[86]" phrase="be satisfied before any discussions were held with employees" />, and that the employer "did not provide all relevant information in writing about the changes" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1905" at="[87]" phrase="did not provide all relevant information in writing about the changes" /> before it spoke to the employee.

**Announcing is not consulting.** In *Semaan*, the employer held collective and individual meetings and issued the termination letter on the same day. The Commission called it "no more than a box ticking exercise" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[35]" phrase="no more than a box ticking exercise" /> and found the process "merely perfunctory and deprived Ms Semaan of a bona fide opportunity to influence the decision maker" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[36]" phrase="merely perfunctory and deprived Ms Semaan of a bona fide opportunity to influence the decision maker" />. The employer had time to do it properly; it simply didn't use it.

**Adviser point:** the decision to restructure can be definite. The decision about what happens to *this* employee should still be open when the consultation meeting starts. A termination letter prepared or issued before consultation is complete is strong evidence that the individual outcome was predetermined; whether the consultation obligation was breached depends on the applicable instrument and the facts.

## Redeployment: was there somewhere else to put them? (s.389(2))

**A suitable job, not an identical one.** The leading description is in *Ulan Coal Mines Limited v Honeysett* [2010] FWAFB 7578. Its paragraph [34] is quoted in *Graydon* at [92]; the Guide now keeps a direct official source record for the Full Bench authority <Cite id="[2010] FWAFB 7578" at="[34]" phrase="the employee should have the skills and competence required to perform it to the required standard either immediately or with a reasonable period of retraining" />. The employee must have the skills and competence to do the job to the required standard, straight away or after reasonable retraining, and location and pay may also matter. The direct source record is present, but the original decision text has not been independently read in this pass.

**Don't assume competition preserves the redundancy defence.** The *Honeysett* passage warns that where an employer chooses to "advertise the vacancy and require the employee to compete with other applicants" <Cite id="[2010] FWAFB 7578" at="[34]" phrase="where an employer decides that, rather than fill a vacancy by redeploying an employee into a suitable job in its own enterprise, it will advertise the vacancy and require the employee to compete with other applicants" />, that may be strong evidence that redeployment would have been reasonable. It is not an absolute prohibition: the question remains whether redeployment was reasonable in all the circumstances, including suitability, retraining, location, remuneration and the nature of the role.

**The inquiry is wider than the vacancy list.** The High Court has confirmed, in *Helensburgh Coal Pty Ltd v Bartley*, that the redeployment assessment "can include consideration of work being performed by independent contractors" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1315" at="[16]" phrase="can include consideration of work being performed by independent contractors" />. *Helensburgh* is a recent High Court decision quoted in the decision cited here and has not been read in the original for this guide — check it before relying on this point. What it does not mean is that every contractor must be displaced. In *Wang*, a single contractor who had done skilled work on one client contract for years was distinguished: he was "performing regular, ongoing, and important work" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1315" at="[17]" phrase="performing regular, ongoing, and important work" /> and was not engaged on an as-needs basis, and redeploying the employee into that role would have required changes to important facets of the enterprise.

**Have the conversation.** In *Graydon*, the employer advertised a similar role the day after the dismissal. It had not spoken to the employee about whether she could do it: it "did not undertake any consultation with the applicant to identify with her what, if any skill or experience gap there was" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1905" at="[93]" phrase="did not undertake any consultation with the applicant to identify with her what, if any skill or experience gap there was" />. The Commission found "it would have been reasonable in all the circumstances for the applicant to be redeployed" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1905" at="[95]" phrase="it would have been reasonable in all the circumstances for the applicant to be redeployed" /> to that role.

The search has to cover associated entities as well as the employer's own business. Record what was looked at, when, and why each option was or wasn't suitable.

## When the redundancy isn't "genuine", the case isn't over

Failing s.389 removes the employer's shield. It does not decide the case. The Commission then asks the ordinary question: was the dismissal harsh, unjust or unreasonable under s.387? Three decisions show how differently that can go.

<div class="tablewrap">

| Decision | Why it wasn't a genuine redundancy | What happened next |
|---|---|---|
| *Semaan v Country Road* | Award consultation was perfunctory | Not unfair. Consultation was highly unlikely to have changed the outcome. Application dismissed. |
| *Hurley v Tex Onsite* | Award consultation was not done at all | Unfair, but compensation of one week's pay — the time consultation would have taken. |
| *Graydon v Talentko* | No proper consultation, and a suitable role was advertised the next day | Unfair. No valid reason; she should have been redeployed. Compensation ordered. |

</div>

**The operational reason still counts.** In *Semaan*, the Commission treated s.387(a) to (c) as neutral because the employee "was not dismissed for a reason that related to her capacity or conduct" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[47]" phrase="was not dismissed for a reason that related to her capacity or conduct" />, then weighed the genuine operational reason in the employer's favour and the consultation failure against it. It concluded it was "highly unlikely that had it complied with that obligation a different outcome" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[59]" phrase="highly unlikely that had it complied with that obligation a different outcome" /> would have followed. It drew on an earlier observation that a redundancy dismissal will "only be harsh, unjust or unreasonable if the rationale for the decision is seriously undermined" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[60]" phrase="only be harsh, unjust or unreasonable if the rationale for the decision is seriously undermined" /> or there is a serious error in procedure. That observation is from *Maswan v Escada Textilvertrieb* and has not been read in the original for this guide.

**Don't rely on that.** In *Hurley*, on similar facts, the failure to consult was enough: the Commission found that "because of procedural fairness failings the termination of Mr Hurley was harsh, unjust or unreasonable" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4724" at="[38]" phrase="because of procedural fairness failings the termination of Mr Hurley was harsh, unjust or unreasonable" />. Different members weigh a consultation failure differently.

**The remedy is often small — which cuts both ways.** In *Hurley*, the Commission found the employee "would have been further employed and remunerated for a period of one week" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4724" at="[44]" phrase="would have been further employed and remunerated for a period of one week" />, and ordered one week's pay. It had warned the parties early that "any compensation order might be no more than the period that it would take for a termination to be lawful" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4724" at="[4]" phrase="any compensation order might be no more than the period that it would take for a termination to be lawful" />, in a case where the employee's legal costs were understood to be $13,500 already. For an employer, that is a reason to settle a pure consultation failure early. It is not a reason to skip consultation: where a real alternative existed, as in *Graydon*, the loss is measured very differently, because "There is every reason to believe she would have remained in employment" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1905" at="[105]" phrase="There is every reason to believe she would have remained in employment" />.

## Redundancy pay and "other acceptable employment" (ss.119–120)

Redundancy pay under the National Employment Standards is a separate question from whether the dismissal was a genuine redundancy. The scale, and the exclusions for short service and small business employers, are set by the Act — check the current ss.119 and 121 and any more generous award, agreement or contract term.

An employer who finds the employee another job can apply to the Commission to reduce redundancy pay, including to nil. The test is objective, and the new job "does not need to be the same as the job that has been made redundant in order to be acceptable" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2083" at="[24]" phrase="does not need to be the same as the job that has been made redundant in order to be acceptable" />. The employee's individual circumstances count. In a s.120 application, the Commission may also consider whether the employee meaningfully engaged with the alternative employment offered, but the acceptability of the employment remains a fact-sensitive question and the statutory discretion must be checked in its current form.

In *Redpath v Papworth*, the employer lost a mine contract in Tasmania and offered a fitter who lived twenty minutes from site a role at the same hourly rate in remote South Australia. The Commission found it was not acceptable employment: the conditions were "very different from each other, and significantly less favourable" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2083" at="[26]" phrase="very different from each other, and significantly less favourable" /> — a residential job replaced by fly-in fly-out work a day's travel away, with fuel and airport parking at his own cost. The application failed at the threshold, so "the discretion under section 120(2) of the Fair Work Act is not enlivened" <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2083" at="[28]" phrase="the discretion under section 120(2) of the Fair Work Act is not enlivened" />. He had also been given from Monday to Friday to decide.

**Adviser point:** "acceptable" is judged from where the employee stands. Same trade and same rate is not enough if the commute, roster, location or out-of-pocket costs are materially worse.

## What the Commission looks for

- **Evidence of the change, from someone who knows the job.** A witness who managed the role, and documents created when the decision was made, not submissions written afterwards.
- **What happened to the duties.** Who does them now, and whether anyone was hired or a similar role advertised around the same time.
- **Which award or agreement applies,** and whether each step of its consultation clause can be shown: notice, written information, discussion, and genuine consideration of what the employee said.
- **The order of events.** Whether the termination letter came before, during or after consultation.
- **The redeployment search.** Vacancies, roles in associated entities, lower-level roles, and work done by contractors or labour hire — and whether the employee was asked.

## How redundancy dismissals fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The vacancy advertised the next day</h3>
<p>A similar role was advertised the day after the dismissal, and colleagues in the same redundant role had been moved into it. Nobody had asked the employee whether she could do it. <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1905" at="[95]" phrase="it would have been reasonable in all the circumstances for the applicant to be redeployed" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Check vacancies and pending recruitment before the letter goes out, and discuss them with the employee.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won (one week's pay)</span>
<h3>No consultation at all</h3>
<p>A business in financial distress dismissed its chief pilot the day control of the company changed, without the consultation the award required. The redundancy objection was dismissed by consent. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 4724" at="[16]" phrase="It was agreed that it was not complied with" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Urgency doesn't remove the obligation. Even a short consultation period would have kept the s.389 protection.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer's evidence fell short</span>
<h3>Assertion instead of evidence</h3>
<p>The employer's witnesses had not managed the employee and could not speak to his duties; no evidence of the claimed restructure was produced. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1393" at="[51]" phrase="More than mere submissions are required that there were operational requirements" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Call the person who made the decision and produce the documents that record it.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won, despite failing to consult</span>
<h3>A box-ticking process, saved by the operational case</h3>
<p>Consultation was perfunctory, so the redundancy was not "genuine". But the operational reason was real, and consultation would not have changed the result. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 642" at="[59]" phrase="highly unlikely that had it complied with that obligation a different outcome" /></p>
<p class="lesson">The employer still had to run a full unfair dismissal hearing it could have avoided.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>The contractor's role wasn't a redeployment option</h3>
<p>The employee pointed to a contractor doing similar work. The contractor's role was longstanding, skilled and tied to one client, so displacing him was not reasonable. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1315" at="[17]" phrase="performing regular, ongoing, and important work" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Be ready to explain, with evidence, why each contractor role is or isn't available.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employer's application failed</span>
<h3>The alternative job wasn't "acceptable"</h3>
<p>An offer at the same rate, but fly-in fly-out and a day's travel away, did not justify reducing redundancy pay. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2083" at="[26]" phrase="very different from each other, and significantly less favourable" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Compare the whole package from the employee's position, not just the classification and rate.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the regional depot restructure

  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Halden Freight is a fictional transport company with 140 employees and a sister company, Halden Warehousing. After losing a major customer, it decides to close the dispatch desk at its regional depot and run dispatch from the city. Tomasz has been the depot's dispatch coordinator for nine years. His role is covered by a modern award.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Mon 9:00</span><b>All-staff meeting: depot dispatch to close</b>
      <span>Mon 9:40</span><b>Individual meeting with Tomasz; termination letter handed over</b>
      <span>Tue</span><b>City office advertises "Dispatch Planner"</b>
      <span>Fri</span><b>Tomasz's last day</b>
    </div>
    <p>Nothing was given to Tomasz in writing before Monday. In the individual meeting he asked whether he could work remotely for the city dispatch team; the manager said the decision had been made. Two labour-hire drivers continue to work at the depot each week, and Tomasz holds a heavy vehicle licence. Halden Warehousing has a vacant storeperson role at a lower rate.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Probably, if it can be proved.</strong> Losing a customer and centralising dispatch is a change in operational requirements, and the job can be redundant even if dispatch work continues elsewhere. But the "Dispatch Planner" advertisement the next day needs explaining: if it is substantially Tomasz's job, the first limb is in doubt as well.</li>
      <li><strong>No.</strong> No written information came first, the individual meeting followed the announcement by forty minutes, and the termination letter was handed over in it. His suggestion was not considered. That is announcement, not consultation, and an award applied.</li>
      <li><strong>Three are open:</strong> the Dispatch Planner role (was he suitable, immediately or with reasonable retraining, and why was it advertised instead of offered?); the storeperson role at the associated entity, even at a lower rate; and the driving work done through labour hire, given his licence. Nobody asked him about any of them.</li>
      <li><strong>It depends on the redeployment answer.</strong> If consultation was the only failure and no alternative was realistic, the dismissal may still be found fair, or compensation may be limited to the period consultation would have taken. If the Dispatch Planner role was suitable, the more likely finding is that he would have stayed employed, and the exposure is much larger.</li>
    </ol>
