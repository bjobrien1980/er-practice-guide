<p class="lead">When the facts are disputed, the Commission doesn't ask who sounds more sincere. It asks who has something that was written down before the dispute existed — and treats everything produced afterwards with real suspicion.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain why a document made at the time usually beats a confident memory, and why that isn't just folklore.
- Tell a record that was genuinely made at the time from one assembled or embellished after the fact — including from the document itself.
- Build the file while it still matters, instead of trying to reconstruct one once a dispute has started.

## Why timing beats sincerity

Module 8 makes the point that a warning needs to be "a document, not a memory". This module goes one step further: *when* a document (or a memory) was created changes how much it's worth, and the Commission actively tests that timing rather than taking a witness's word for it.

<div class="key">
<h3>The working principle</h3>
<p style="margin:0;font-size:14px;color:var(--muted)">Chief Justice McLelland's observations in <em>Watson v Foxman</em> (1995) 49 NSWLR 315 at 319, as quoted and applied in a Fair Work Commission bargaining dispute <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3513" at="[33]" phrase="human memory of what was said in a conversation is fallible for a variety of reasons" />. <em>Watson v Foxman</em> is a 1995 NSW Supreme Court decision, not an FWC matter and not in this corpus directly — check the quote against the original before relying on it.</p>

<ol class="principles">
<li>Human memory of a conversation is fallible, and the degree of fallibility ordinarily increases with the passage of time.</li>
<li>Fallibility increases further once a dispute or litigation intervenes — memory becomes overlaid, often subconsciously, by self-interest and by what the person now thinks should have been said.</li>
<li>Where a dispute is raised some time after the event, the Commission (like the courts) generally prefers documentary evidence to oral recollection <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3513" at="[38]" phrase="the fallibility of human memory leads the courts, and the Commission, to prefer documentary evidence" />.</li>
<li>A witness who honestly accepts the limits of their own memory is often <strong>more</strong> credible than one who confidently recalls old conversations in precise detail <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3513" at="[35]" phrase="more willing to accept the evidence of those who accept the limits of their memory" />.</li>
</ol>
</div>

That last point matters for advisers as much as for the Commission. A witness statement that reads as word-perfect, months or years later, is not automatically a strength — sometimes it's a sign the statement was written for the hearing rather than remembered from it.

## A record that times out

*Brass v KDR Victoria (Yarra Trams)* shows what happens when a document's own timing betrays it. The employer's manager, Mr Sheikh, said in his witness statement that he had typed up a file note of a conversation shortly after it happened. The applicant's lawyers obtained an electronic copy and checked its properties.

The document's electronic data properties disclosed it was not created on the date of the conversation, but eight months later <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2527" at="[40]" phrase="the document was not created on 25 June 2021, but eight months later" /> — on the very day the applicant was suspended over a second, unrelated incident. Mr Sheikh then said he'd made a mistake and produced a handwritten note instead, made closer to the time.

The timing wasn't a side issue. Because the typed note had been produced eight months after the fact, it contained detail that simply wasn't in the earlier handwritten note <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2527" at="[41]" phrase="eight months later, he included details that are not found in the handwritten file note" /> — including a claimed admission the applicant denied ever making. The Commission found it difficult to accept that a document of that importance would have been forgotten about for eight months, and noted the true creation date only came to light because the applicant's solicitor went looking for it; it wasn't volunteered.

**Even a genuinely contemporaneous document isn't accepted wholesale.** The handwritten note itself, made shortly after the conversation, was still only partly believed — the Commission accepted it was made at the time, but rejected the specific detail it recorded about an alleged admission, preferring the applicant's "convincing denial" on that point <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2527" at="[43]" phrase="I do not accept that it is accurate insofar as it records" />. Contemporaneous beats reconstructed, but it isn't a free pass — it still has to be believable on its own terms. The Commission found Mr Brass was unfairly dismissed <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2527" at="[68]" phrase="unfairly dismissed within the meaning of s 385" />.

## A diary note against someone's word

The principle cuts both ways — it isn't a rule that favours employers. In *Green v Citic Pacific Mining Management*, it was the **employee's** account that lost out to a manager's contemporaneous record.

Mr Green disputed what was said in a phone call about his return to work. The Commission accepted the manager's evidence of that conversation because it was consistent with his diary entry made at the time, "and do[ing] so in preference to any difference with the evidence of Mr Green" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2455" at="[93]" phrase="consistent with his diary entry" />. Where Mr Green's account diverged — that he'd been authorised to delay his return — the Commission rejected it outright: that evidence was "self-serving, inconsistent with the contemporaneous diary note made by Mr Crawford" and implausible against the surrounding circumstances <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2455" at="[94]" phrase="inconsistent with the contemporaneous diary note made by Mr Crawford" />. The Commission went on to find Mr Green's dismissal was not, in an objective sense, unfair <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2455" at="[237]" phrase="not in an objective sense unfair" />.

**The lesson for advisers on both sides:** whoever is relying on their own account of a disputed conversation, with nothing written down from the time, is the one at risk — regardless of which side of the table they sit on.

## When there's nothing to produce

Some disputes aren't about a document that turns out to be unreliable — they're about the complete absence of one.

In *Lakhan v Bus Tech (SA)*, a senior manager gave evidence about what he'd told the applicant in two meetings. The Commission found the manager generally credible — firmer and with clearer recall than the applicant <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6329" at="[19]" phrase="displayed clearer recall than Mr Lakhan" /> — but still would not make a finding on two specific, disputed points, because of "the absence of a contemporaneous documentary record of these meetings, the self-serving potential of such evidence and the lack of sufficient precision in Mr Dinning's recall on these specific questions" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6329" at="[20]" phrase="absence of a contemporaneous documentary record" />. Notably, the Commission had already flagged that same caution about self-serving evidence for a different witness earlier in the same decision <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6329" at="[16]" phrase="treat it with some caution given its self-serving potential" />. The dismissal meeting itself was handled the same way — "[n]othing was exchanged in writing. No record was taken" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6329" at="[92]" phrase="Nothing was exchanged in writing. No record was taken" />. The Commission ultimately found Mr Lakhan was unfairly dismissed because his dismissal was harsh and unjust <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6329" at="[280]" phrase="unfairly dismissed because his dismissal was harsh and unjust" />.

**The point advisers tend to miss:** the absence of a record doesn't automatically mean the other side wins that point. It can simply mean the Commission can't be satisfied either way — which, where the employer needs that fact to make out a valid reason, still means the employer loses it.

*Clark v Reliable Signs and Refinishing* shows the sharper version of the same problem — not a credible witness whose unrecorded account couldn't be relied on, but an unconvincing one. The employer's own witness was found to have dramatised his evidence, including a claim that wasn't in his written witness statement at all <Cite id="[2023] FWC 276" at="[39]" phrase="circumstances where this was not in his written witness statement" />. Where the applicant's and the employer's evidence conflicted, the Commission preferred the applicant's — he had given his evidence "in a calm and considered manner" and conceded points against his own interest <Cite id="[2023] FWC 276" at="[38]" phrase="conceded matters where such concession was not to his advantage" />, leading the Commission to state plainly: "where there is a conflict in the evidence of the Applicant and either of the Mr Hamers I prefer the evidence of the Applicant" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 276" at="[42]" phrase="I prefer the evidence of the Applicant" />. On the warnings the employer said it had relied on, the Commission found there was "no documented information as to warnings that may have been given — either orally or in writing — to the Applicant" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 276" at="[94]" phrase="no documented information as to warnings that may have been given" />. There was no valid reason for the dismissal <Cite id="[2023] FWC 276" at="[101]" phrase="no valid reason for the dismissal of the Applicant" />.

## What the Commission looks for in practice

- **When it was actually made, not just what it says.** A document's own metadata can contradict the story told about it — check creation and modification dates, not just the date printed on the page.
- **Whether late detail appeared.** A later account that adds specifics absent from an earlier, closer-in-time version is treated as reconstruction, not confirmation.
- **Whether an unwritten claim is plausible as unwritten.** If a business ordinarily records something of that kind (a diary entry, a rostering note, an HR file), its absence here is itself evidence.
- **Who is relying on their own word alone.** The side without any record of a disputed exchange is the exposed side — whichever side that is.
- **Honesty about the limits of memory.** A witness who says "I don't recall precisely" can be more credible than one who recites old conversations verbatim.
- **Consistency, not just existence.** A document that exists but is only partly accurate doesn't get to be contemporaneous *and* conclusive — it still has to hold up against other convincing evidence.

## How documentation claims fail

<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>No record, and an exaggerating witness</h3>
<p>The employer's only evidence of warnings was oral assertion; its own witness dramatised his account beyond his written statement. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 276" at="[94]" phrase="no documented information as to warnings that may have been given" /></p>
<p class="lesson">An unwritten warning you can't corroborate is, for practical purposes, no warning at all.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The file note that timed itself out</h3>
<p>A "contemporaneous" typed file note was shown, by its own metadata, to have been created eight months later with added detail. <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2527" at="[41]" phrase="eight months later, he included details that are not found in the handwritten file note" /></p>
<p class="lesson">If you're going to rely on a document's timing, make sure its metadata agrees with you.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>The diary entry beat the recollection</h3>
<p>An employee's own account of a phone call was rejected as self-serving because it conflicted with his manager's contemporaneous diary note. <Cite id="[2022] FWC 2455" at="[94]" phrase="inconsistent with the contemporaneous diary note made by Mr Crawford" /></p>
<p class="lesson">The documentary-record principle isn't an employer's shield — it cuts against whoever lacks the record.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">No finding possible</span>
<h3>A credible witness, but still no record</h3>
<p>Even a witness the Commission found generally credible couldn't get a disputed point decided in his favour, for want of any contemporaneous record of the conversation. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6329" at="[20]" phrase="absence of a contemporaneous documentary record" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Credibility isn't a substitute for a record — it just means you're not disbelieved. You can still not be believed enough.</p>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the reconstructed roster note

  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>This is a fictional scenario. Jun is a shift supervisor at a café chain, three years' service, no prior written warnings. He's dismissed for "repeated failure to follow cash-handling procedure" after a till shortfall is found during a routine audit.</p>
    <p>The area manager says she gave Jun three verbal warnings over the preceding four months, and told him each time that his job was at risk if it happened again — but nothing was written down at the time. Two weeks after Jun lodges his unfair dismissal application, the area manager sends HR a one-page "summary of prior discussions", listing three dates and, for each one, a close paraphrase of what she says she told him.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Apr–Jul</span><b>Three undocumented "verbal warning" conversations (area manager's account)</b>
      <span>2 wks post-dismissal</span><b>"Summary of prior discussions" emailed to HR, with dates and quotes</b>
    </div>
    <p>Jun agrees he had two brief chats about till procedure generally, but denies ever being told his job was at risk, and says he doesn't recognise the specific wording now attributed to him.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>No.</strong> It was created after the dismissal, after Jun had already lodged his application — once the area manager knew the warnings would be scrutinised. That timing is itself the problem, in the same way the typed file note's creation date undid it in <em>Brass v Yarra Trams</em> above: a document assembled once a dispute exists isn't evidence of what was said before the dispute existed.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> anything written at the time of any of the three conversations — a follow-up text or email to Jun, a note in the rostering or HR system, even a dated personal diary entry; and a plausible account of why specific wording has only now, under pressure, become available with unusual precision.</li>
      <li><strong>At the time, this file needed:</strong> a short, dated message to Jun after each conversation confirming what was discussed and that continued issues could lead to dismissal; and a file note made the same day, kept somewhere its creation date could later be checked, rather than reconstructed once a claim had already been lodged.</li>
    </ol>
    <p style="margin-bottom:6px"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what else comes out:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Each conversation was in fact followed by a same-day email or system note to Jun, later located: that record is corroboration, not reconstruction, and the warnings likely stand up.</div>
      <div class="lo">Nothing exists except the after-the-fact summary: on the pattern in <em>Clark v Reliable Signs</em> above, there's no documented information about any warning, and that alone is likely to defeat a valid reason resting on "repeated" conduct Jun was never shown to have been warned about.</div>
    </div>
