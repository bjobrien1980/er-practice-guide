<p class="lead">Lodging an unfair dismissal application doesn't send a case straight to a hearing. It starts with conciliation, representation isn't a given, and the normal rule is that each side pays its own way — whatever the outcome.</p>

<div class="notice info"><strong>Running the hearing:</strong> this module covers the legal and procedural route from application to outcome. For how to prepare and conduct a hearing yourself, including the documents, etiquette and sample wording for each stage, see <a href="/advocacy">Advocacy in the Fair Work Commission</a>. Always check the directions in your matter and the Commission’s current practice notes.</div>

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain what actually happens between an application being lodged and a final decision, and why conciliation comes first.
- Advise a client realistically on whether the Commission will let them bring a lawyer, and what that application needs to show.
- Set correct expectations about costs — including the risk of a costs claim backfiring.

## Conciliation comes first

Once an application is lodged and a response filed, the Commission's standard next step is a conciliation conference, not a hearing. In *Cowper v Trustees of Edmund Rice Education Australia*, a staff conciliator conducted the conference; only once <Cite id="[2020] FWC 316" at="[9]" phrase="the matter was not resolved in conciliation" /> did the matter proceed toward a liability hearing.

**Conciliation is treated as without prejudice.** Parties can speak more freely there than they would in a hearing, because what's said isn't meant to be used against them later. In the same costs dispute, the respondent objected to the applicant relying on anything said at the conciliation conference, on the basis that <Cite id="[2020] FWC 316" at="[36]" phrase="the conference was conducted on a without prejudice basis" />.

Conciliation can end in a few different ways:

- **Settlement.** In *Bell v Singaria (Aust) Pty Ltd*, a telephone conciliation conference produced an agreed outcome — a payment, a resignation and a statement of service — and <Cite id="[2020] FWC 1181" at="[5]" phrase="it was thought that the dispute had been resolved" />.
- **Withdrawal.** An applicant can discontinue rather than proceed.
- **No resolution, so it moves on.** If conciliation doesn't resolve things, the matter is set down for arbitration — a conference or hearing where the Commission actually decides the case on the evidence, rather than facilitating a deal.

**A conciliation outcome can be final, even if nothing was signed on the day.** In *Bell*, the applicant later tried to walk away from the deal reached at conciliation. The Commission found a binding settlement had been reached and <Cite id="[2020] FWC 1181" at="[47]" phrase="I exercise my discretion to dismiss Ms Bell's application under s 587(1)(c) of the FW Act" />; <Cite id="[2020] FWC 1181" at="[48]" phrase="I dismiss her application for an unfair dismissal remedy" />. Treat what's agreed at conciliation as real — confirm your client's instructions before anything is put to the other side, not after.

## Representation: permission, not a right

**Before you rely on this:** before relying on any statement about representation, conciliation, settlement, costs or Commission procedure, check the current Fair Work Act, Commission Rules, current Commission guidance and the procedural orders for the matter. Case law remains useful but does not guarantee that a current procedural practice is unchanged.

Section 596 of the Fair Work Act doesn't let a party simply turn up with a lawyer or paid agent. In *Durmic v Department of Home Affairs*, the Commission described a decision to grant or refuse permission as one that <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1502" at="[12]" phrase="cannot be properly characterised as a mere procedural decision" />, because it can change the whole dynamic of the hearing.

Permission can only be granted on one (or more) of three grounds:

- it would let the matter be dealt with <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1502" at="[13]" phrase="more efficiently, taking into account the complexity of the matter" />;
- it would be unfair not to allow representation because the person can't represent themselves effectively; or
- it would be unfair not to allow it, having regard to fairness between the parties.

The Commission in *Donnola v Silverleaf Constructions* put it simply: it <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6336" at="[7]" phrase="need only find that one of the sub-sections above is satisfied" /> to be able to exercise the discretion — but satisfying a ground doesn't make the grant automatic; it opens a discretion the Commission can still decide not to exercise.

**Who needs permission.** The rule is about lawyers and paid agents acting for a party. As a general rule, a person who is an employee or officer of the party isn't treated as a lawyer or paid agent representing it, so an in-house ER adviser can usually appear for the employer without permission. Check the current text of s.596 before relying on that, especially for in-house lawyers and for employees of a related company.

**Permission is the exception, not the rule.** The Explanatory Memorandum, reproduced in *Durmic*, records that <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1502" at="[20]" phrase="It is envisaged that in most cases legal representation will not be necessary" />. In *Donnola*, the Commission reasoned that <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6336" at="[34]" phrase="must normally appear on their own behalf" /> — representation is the departure from the norm, and the applicant has to make the case for it.

### What persuades the Commission — and what doesn't

*Durmic* shows what a successful application looks like. The Department of Home Affairs faced a jurisdictional objection that turned on the intersection of a Commonwealth statutory scheme and the general protections case law. The Commission accepted that the matter <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1502" at="[30]" phrase="can be dealt with more efficiently if permission for legal representation is granted" />, and separately found that <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1502" at="[54]" phrase="unlikely that the Department could represent itself effectively if leave to be represented was not granted" />.

*Donnola* shows what fails. The applicant's solicitor had been acting since the dispute began and the applicant said he wasn't legally trained, but he hadn't pointed to anything genuinely complex in the case, and the Commission found that <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6336" at="[22]" phrase="the absence of such training in itself is not a sufficient reason" /> to grant permission. Permission was refused: <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6336" at="[35]" phrase="permission is refused" />.

The pattern across both: the Commission wants a specific, demonstrated reason tied to *this* matter — a genuine point of legal or factual complexity, or a concrete inability to self-represent — not a generalised preference for having a lawyer, and not "the other side might get one too" asserted in the abstract.

**At conciliation specifically**, the same permission requirement applies, but the informal, settlement-focused nature of a conciliation conference means parties attend unrepresented far more often than at a hearing — an external lawyer or paid agent should expect to be in the room (or on the phone) as a support person rather than as a permitted representative unless permission has actually been sought and granted. An in-house adviser attends as the employer.

## Costs: each side bears its own, as the default

The basic rule is that nobody pays the other side's costs just for winning or losing. Section 611, reproduced in *Petersen v Kizuri Capital Pty Ltd (T/A Allpet Products)*, states that <Cite id="[2021] FWC 526" at="[57]" phrase="A person must bear the person's own costs in relation to a matter before the FWC" />. The Commission in that case described the costs jurisdiction as sitting within "a statutory framework where <Cite id="[2021] FWC 526" at="[58]" phrase="the primary position is that each party bears their own costs in proceedings before the Commission" />."

Costs orders exist, but they're the exception, and courts and the Commission treat them that way. In *McCaffery v Kizuri Capital Pty Ltd*, the Commission observed that <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2491" at="[42]" phrase="Cost orders in proceedings under the Act are rare" />.

There are two separate statutory routes:

- **Section 611(2)** — costs where an application or response was made **vexatiously or without reasonable cause**, or where it should have been **reasonably apparent** it had no reasonable prospect of success.
- **Section 400A** — specific to unfair dismissal matters, and aimed instead at **conduct during the case**: costs where a party's unreasonable act or omission in connection with the conduct or continuation of the matter caused the other side to incur costs.

**Vigorously defending a claim isn't, by itself, unreasonable.** In *Cowper*, the respondent fought a genuinely contested factual dispute and rejected two settlement offers. The Commission found that <Cite id="[2020] FWC 316" at="[58]" phrase="it is not unreasonable conduct on the part of a losing party to vigorously defend itself" />, and ultimately <Cite id="[2020] FWC 316" at="[82]" phrase="No order for costs is therefore made and the application for costs is dismissed" />. Losing on the merits, even losing badly, doesn't get you costs on its own.

## When a costs claim backfires

*McCaffery* is the sharpest illustration in this area: the respondent brought its own s.611/s.400A costs application against the applicant, but at the hearing <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2491" at="[66]" phrase="The claim that the Application was vexatious was abandoned by the Respondent in the Hearing" />. The applicant then made a reciprocal costs application — against the *respondent*, for bringing that claim. The Commission found the vexatious-application ground had, on the facts known to the respondent at the time, no reasonable prospect of success, and <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2491" at="[73]" phrase="I further exercise my discretion to order that the Respondent pay the Applicant's costs" /> on an indemnity basis for that part of the claim.

A costs application is itself an application — if it's weak, it can expose the person who brought it.

## How representation and costs applications fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Point of principle</span>
<h3>"My client isn't legally trained" isn't a reason on its own</h3>
<p>No complexity was identified in the actual case; the Commission found the lack of legal training alone did not justify representation. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6336" at="[22]" phrase="the absence of such training in itself is not a sufficient reason" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Point to something specific about *this* case — a complex jurisdictional issue, a genuine inability to self-represent — not a general preference.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>A hard-fought defence isn't an unreasonable one</h3>
<p>The respondent contested the facts and rejected two settlement offers for reasoned grounds; no costs order followed. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 316" at="[58]" phrase="it is not unreasonable conduct on the part of a losing party to vigorously defend itself" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Don't assume a costs order follows from a weak or unsuccessful case — the bar is "unreasonable act or omission", not "lost".</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>Bringing a weak costs application cost the respondent money</h3>
<p>The respondent's own vexatious-application claim was abandoned at hearing and had no reasonable prospects when made; it ended up paying the applicant's costs of that part of the dispute on an indemnity basis. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 2491" at="[73]" phrase="I further exercise my discretion to order that the Respondent pay the Applicant's costs" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Assess your own costs application as critically as you'd assess the other side's case — it can turn on you.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Trying to walk away from a conciliation outcome</h3>
<p>Terms reached verbally at a telephone conciliation conference were held to be a binding settlement, and the later application was dismissed as having no reasonable prospects. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 1181" at="[48]" phrase="I dismiss her application for an unfair dismissal remedy" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Make sure your client understands that agreeing at conciliation is agreeing, full stop — get instructions before you concede anything.</p>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the store manager's case

<Scenario questions={["Should the Commission grant Northfield permission to be represented by a lawyer?", "What should you tell Northfield about the upcoming conciliation conference?", "What non-dismissal or settlement outcome remains open?", "Can Northfield recover costs if the claim turns out to be weak?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>This is a fictional scenario. Northfield Mercantile is a mid-sized retail chain with stores in three states. A duty manager, dismissed after a cash-handling discrepancy was found at her store, has lodged an unfair dismissal application. Northfield has one HR generalist and no in-house lawyer. Northfield's external lawyer wants permission to appear, pointing to "the general complexity of retail employment law". The applicant intends to argue that Northfield's cash-handling policy was inconsistently applied across its stores, which will require comparing rostering and audit records from multiple sites.</p>
    <p>Northfield's operations director has told the HR generalist: "This case is obviously weak — she admitted the till was short. Can we get our legal costs back once we win?" A conciliation conference is listed for next week.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Representation:</strong> "General complexity of retail employment law" is the kind of generalised claim that failed in <em>Donnola</em>. A better application would identify the actual complexity in <em>this</em> case — comparing cash-handling audit practices and disciplinary outcomes across multiple sites and reconciling that against the policy as written is a concrete, fact-specific complexity the Commission can engage with, closer to the kind of thing that succeeded in <em>Durmic</em>. Lead with that, not a generic statement about the jurisdiction.</li>
      <li><strong>Conciliation:</strong> Tell Northfield the conference is confidential and conducted on a without-prejudice basis — it's a chance to settle, not a rehearsal for the hearing, and nothing said there is a formal concession. But also tell them that if terms are reached and agreed on the call, that can be treated as a final, binding outcome even without signatures that day, so they should only agree to terms they're actually prepared to live with.</li>
      <li><strong>Costs:</strong> Even if the claim looks weak to Northfield, that alone doesn't support a costs order. The applicant would need to have acted vexatiously, without reasonable cause, or in a way where it should have been reasonably apparent the claim had no reasonable prospect of success — and the Commission reaches that conclusion cautiously. Her own account of being dismissed over an inconsistently applied policy, even if it ultimately fails, isn't obviously groundless. Advise Northfield that costs orders are rare, and that pursuing one on a thin basis carries its own risk — a weak costs application can result in costs being ordered against the party that brought it.</li>
    </ol>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
