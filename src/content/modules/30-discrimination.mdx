<p class="lead">Section 351 prohibits adverse action because of a person's race, sex, age, disability, pregnancy and other listed attributes. It is not a copy of the anti-discrimination statutes. It asks why the decision-maker acted, it comes with the reverse onus, and its reach depends on the State or Territory where the action was taken. This module covers how s.351 works, the exception that catches advisers out, and the attributes that come up most.</p>

<div class="notice">
<strong>Current-source gate:</strong> the current text of s.351 of the Fair Work Act was checked on 14 November 2026. Federal, State and Territory anti-discrimination statutes are a separate source of liability. ## What you'll be able to do

- Say what s.351 protects and how it differs from a claim under an anti-discrimination statute.
- Apply the reasons test to conduct that can be described in two ways.
- Check whether the "not unlawful where the action is taken" exception applies, and know its limits.
- Spot the common patterns for pregnancy, sex, age, race, and family or carer's responsibilities.

## What s.351 protects

The current s.351(1) list includes race, colour, sex, sexual orientation, breastfeeding, gender identity, intersex status, age, physical or mental disability, marital status, family or carer's responsibilities, pregnancy, religion, political opinion, national extraction, social origin, and subjection or alleged subjection to family and domestic violence. The current statutory list, not the historical list in the 2019 cases, governs. The list has changed since <em>Western Union v Robinson</em>; check the current section before advising that an attribute is or is not covered.

The section covers job applicants as well as employees, and any adverse action, not only dismissal (Module 26). The attribute need only be one of the reasons (Module 27).

## A reasons test

A claim under an anti-discrimination statute usually asks about treatment and effect: was the person treated less favourably than someone without the attribute, or did a requirement disadvantage people with the attribute? Section 351 asks a different question. In the Full Court's words, it <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[139]" phrase="is concerned with the mental process in fact engaged in by the relevant individual making the decision for the employer" />.

Three things follow.

### The decision-maker must know of the attribute

In <em>Gavin v Svitzer Australia</em>, the employee said he was made redundant partly because of his family responsibilities. That claim failed, though his workplace right claim succeeded (Module 28). The decision-maker <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1623" at="[194]" phrase="was not aware of Mr Gavin's family responsibilities" />.

### Conduct with two descriptions

Often the same conduct can be described as an expression of the attribute and as something else. In <em>Rumble v HWL Ebsworth Lawyers</em>, a consultant to a law firm repeatedly criticised government agencies in public. The agencies were the firm's clients. He was told to stop, did not, and was dismissed. He said the dismissal was because of his political opinion.

Perram J accepted that his criticism was both an expression of political opinion and a breach of the firm's media policy. That did not decide the case: <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1409" at="[132]" phrase="The question is not what Dr Rumble did but rather why Mr Martinez terminated his employment" />. The managing partner's evidence was accepted. The firm <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1409" at="[14]" phrase="terminated him because he repeatedly disobeyed a reasonable direction to cease from criticising the Firm's clients" />.

The same reasoning decided <em>Western Union v Robinson</em> on disability (Module 29).

### A neutral label does not help if the attribute is the reason

The reasons test protects an employer only when the lawful reason is the real one.

- In <em>ABCC v CoreStaff WA</em>, a labour hire company told a 70-year-old applicant he was unsuccessful because of his age. It was passing on a client's preference. It still contravened s.351 <Cite id="[2020] FCA 893" at="[116]" phrase="because of his age and therefore contravened s 351(1)" />.
- In <em>Fair Work Ombudsman v Austrend International (No 2)</em>, discussed below, an employer's insistence on a medical clearance for a pregnant employee returning from parental leave was not accepted as the real explanation for refusing her return.

### What the reasons test leaves out

Section 351 has no separate concept of indirect discrimination and no general duty to make adjustments. An employee whose complaint is that a neutral rule disadvantages them, or that the employer failed to accommodate them, will usually need to consider the applicable anti-discrimination statute. An employer that can defend its reasons under s.351 may still be exposed there. The rule against bringing both claims about the same conduct is in Module 33.

## The exception that depends on where you are

Section 351(1) does not apply to action that is <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[11]" phrase="not unlawful under any anti-discrimination law in force in the place where the action is taken" />.

Most attributes in s.351 are covered by anti-discrimination law everywhere in Australia. Some are not. Where the local law does not make discrimination on a ground unlawful, s.351 does not apply to action taken on that ground in that place.

### How the courts have read it

In <em>Rumble</em>, the firm argued in the alternative that even a dismissal for political opinion would have been lawful, because New South Wales law did not prohibit it. Perram J did not need to decide the point but dealt with it. He rejected the employee's argument that "not unlawful" means specifically permitted: <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1409" at="[142]" phrase="If an action is not proscribed by any anti-discrimination law then plainly the action is not unlawful" />. So <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1409" at="[143]" phrase="if Dr Rumble had been dismissed in NSW then that action would not have contravened s 351 of the Act" />.

The Full Court applied the same reading in <em>CFMMEU v Quirk</em>. Two union officials were removed from office after criticising the union in the media. They said this was because of their political opinions. The trial judge found the action was taken in Sydney. Katzmann J noted that discrimination on the ground of political opinion was unlawful under the Victorian Act but not the New South Wales Act <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[298]" phrase="Discrimination on the ground of political opinion is rendered unlawful under the latter (see s 6(k)) but not under the former" />, and held that the s.351 claim could not succeed. Her Honour's reason: <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[313]" phrase="s 351 was not intended to expand the scope of federal, State or Territory anti-discrimination laws" />. Colvin J agreed that the finding about where the action was taken had not been shown to be wrong <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[489]" phrase="The primary judge found that the relevant adverse action took place in New South Wales" />.

The exception is not limited to political opinion. In <em>Fair Work Ombudsman v Foot & Thai Massage (No 4)</em>, an employer in the Australian Capital Territory injured a group of workers in their employment <Cite id="[2021] FCA 1242" at="[765]" phrase="for reasons which included their race and national extraction" />. That contravened s.351. The employer also acted because of their social origin, but that part of the claim failed because such action <Cite id="[2021] FCA 1242" at="[765]" phrase="is not unlawful under any of the anti-discrimination laws in force in the ACT at the time of the contraventions" />.

### Where is action "taken"?

It depends on the action. In <em>Rumble</em>, the employee read the termination email at his home in New South Wales, but the action was ending an employment relationship, and <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1409" at="[145]" phrase="Everything about that relationship was centred on the ACT" />. In <em>Quirk</em>, the decision was made at a meeting in Sydney. The officials pointed out that the charges were laid and the letter was sent from Victoria, but the finding that the action was taken in New South Wales stood.

<div class="key">
<strong>Do not treat the exception as a safe harbour</strong>
<ol class="principles">
<li>It turns on the current law of the State or Territory, which changes, and on a finding about where the action was taken, which can be contested.</li>
<li>A dismissal can still be challenged as an unlawful termination under s.772, which lists political opinion and has no equivalent exception.</li>
<li>The same facts usually support a workplace right or industrial activity claim as well.</li>
<li>Unfair dismissal still applies.</li>
</ol>
</div>

The second point comes from <em>Quirk</em>. Katzmann J observed that <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[314]" phrase="employees whose employment is terminated in NSW can bring an action for unlawful termination of their employment for a reason including political opinion but not a general protections application under s 351(1) on the same basis" />. The officials in that case had simply chosen the wrong provision.

The Act has two other exceptions. Action taken because of the inherent requirements of the position is discussed in Module 29. There is also an exception in s.351(2)(c) for certain action by religious institutions, with its own statutory conditions concerning the institution, the employee and the religious susceptibilities of adherents. It was not considered in the cases read for this module and needs specific advice.

## The attributes in practice

### Pregnancy and return from parental leave

<em>Austrend</em> is a short lesson in how these cases go wrong. A sales executive on parental leave arranged to return on an agreed date. Before that date <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1193" at="[48]" phrase="Ms Aragon became pregnant again" />. The employer then refused to let her return, later requiring a fitness certificate and saying this was its duty of care. It eventually admitted that it had contravened s.351 <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1193" at="[2]" phrase="by discriminating against her because of her sex and pregnancy" />.

By the time of the hearing the employee's financial loss had been paid. The court still ordered compensation for hurt, a penalty of $15,500 against the company <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1193" at="[184]" phrase="the appropriate penalty is $15,500" /> and $2,800 against its director personally <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1193" at="[185]" phrase="the appropriate penalty is $2,800" />. The larger cost was elsewhere: on its own evidence <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1193" at="[130]" phrase="Austrend had spent over $246,655 on legal costs" />. Banks-Smith J said: <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1193" at="[182]" phrase="Making matters so difficult for a person seeking to return to work from parental leave simply should not happen in this day and age" />.

Practical points:

- A requirement imposed on a pregnant employee that would not be imposed on anyone else needs a documented, specific safety reason, not a general reference to duty of care.
- Decisions about hours, duties or return dates made "for her own good" are decisions made because of pregnancy.
- The return-to-work entitlements in the National Employment Standards are workplace rights as well (Module 25).

### Sex and sexual orientation

*Morton v CSIRO (No 2)* is the fullest treatment in the corpus. A scientist alleged sexual harassment and sex discrimination by two colleagues and said the employer had contravened s.351 because of her sex or sexual orientation. She argued that s.351 takes in the Sex Discrimination Act, so that anything unlawful under that Act is also adverse action under s.351, and the employer is liable for its employees' conduct in the same way.

Rangiah J rejected that. <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1754" at="[69]" phrase="s 351(1) of the FW Act stands independently from the anti-discrimination laws referred to in s 351(3)" />. Conduct may contravene s.351 whether or not it meets the definitions in the Sex Discrimination Act, and s.351 does not import that Act's rule making an employer liable for harassment by its employees <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1754" at="[79]" phrase="the vicarious liability provision in s 106 of the Sex Discrimination Act has no application in the present case" />. The link between the two laws runs one way only: <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1754" at="[69]" phrase="the employer receives the benefits of any defences, exceptions or exemptions under the Sex Discrimination Act" />.

The s.351 claims failed. On the allegation that the employer had not dealt with the harassment complaint promptly, the judge found it had. He added that if it had not, the failure would have been adverse action, but the managers had each given evidence that sex or sexual orientation played no part in what they did, and <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1754" at="[383]" phrase="I accept their evidence" />.

The employee did succeed on one claim, and it was about process. A later complaint she made about a manager was dismissed without following the grievance procedure in the enterprise agreement. That was adverse action taken because she had made the complaint, in contravention of s.340 <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1754" at="[664]" phrase="I have upheld Claim 4" />. Compensation was $1,000 <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1754" at="[661]" phrase="I find that $1,000 is an appropriate amount of compensation" />, with penalty to be decided later.

Three points for advisers:

- Under s.351 the question is why the employer's own decision-makers acted, not whether a colleague's behaviour was harassment. That question is answered under the Sex Discrimination Act and its State equivalents, where the employer's liability for employees' conduct is wider. An employer can win under s.351 and lose there.
- How a harassment or discrimination complaint is handled is itself a general protections risk. A complaint not dealt with under the applicable procedure can be adverse action against the complainant (Module 28).
- The Fair Work Act now has its own prohibition on sexual harassment in connection with work. It is outside the general protections and is not covered in this guide.

### Age

The age case read for this guide involved recruitment and a stated reason. In <em>CoreStaff</em>, the applicant was told in writing that age was why he was unsuccessful. Do not record or pass on a preference about age, and do not ask a recruiter to apply one.

### Race and national extraction

<em>Han v St Basil's Homes</em> (Module 27) shows race and a workplace right operating together in one dismissal. At the remedies stage the discrimination contravention drew the larger penalty: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[220]" phrase="a penalty of $45,000 should be imposed" />, against $15,000 for the workplace right contravention (Module 33).

<em>Foot & Thai</em> shows race discrimination through treatment during employment. The workers were recruited from overseas, and the court found they were injured in their employment for reasons that included their race. The adverse action was their treatment at work, not dismissal.

### Family or carer's responsibilities

These claims usually arise from requests for flexibility or leave, and often overlap with workplace rights. In <em>Talaugon v Allight</em> (Module 29), a pregnant employee with caring responsibilities was performance managed and dismissed shortly after a flexible working arrangement was agreed. The employer won because its decision-maker's evidence was accepted: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1672" at="[133]" phrase="Allight has discharged its onus of proof" />.

The scope of the attribute is not settled by the cases read here. In <em>Herbert v Star Aviation</em>, a job applicant said an offer was withdrawn because he had spoken up for his daughter, who worked for the same employer. The employer argued that advocacy for a relative is not a "family or carer's responsibility". The trial judge decided the case on the employer's reasons instead, and <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 136" at="[9]" phrase="it was not necessary to consider Star's argument" />.

### Political opinion

<em>Rumble</em> and <em>Quirk</em> are the main examples. Two lessons beyond the exception:

- A policy that restricts public comment about clients or the employer can be enforced, if the reason for enforcing it is the breach and not the view expressed. In <em>Rumble</em>, the judge found the firm was indifferent to the opinion itself, and possibly sympathetic.
- Give the direction first. The firm in <em>Rumble</em> had told the consultant to stop, more than once, before it acted.

## How these cases are won and lost

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Dismissed for breaching a media policy</h3>
<p>The consultant's public criticism was political opinion and a policy breach at once. The decision-maker proved he acted on the breach. <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1409" at="[132]" phrase="The question is not what Dr Rumble did but rather why Mr Martinez terminated his employment" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Give a clear direction, record the breach, and have the decision-maker explain their reason.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Respondent won</span>
<h3>Political opinion, action taken in New South Wales</h3>
<p>The local anti-discrimination law did not cover political opinion, so s.351 did not apply. <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[313]" phrase="s 351 was not intended to expand the scope of federal, State or Territory anti-discrimination laws" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Check the local law for the attribute, but remember s.772 and the other protections still apply.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Regulator won</span>
<h3>Pregnant employee kept from returning to work</h3>
<p>The employer admitted discrimination because of sex and pregnancy. The penalties were modest; the legal costs were not. <Cite id="[2020] FCA 1193" at="[130]" phrase="Austrend had spent over $246,655 on legal costs" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Do not impose conditions on a pregnant employee's return that would not apply to anyone else.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Regulator won</span>
<h3>Race and national extraction; social origin excluded</h3>
<p>Workers were injured in their employment for reasons including race. The social origin part of the claim failed on the local-law exception. <Cite id="[2021] FCA 1242" at="[765]" phrase="for reasons which included their race and national extraction" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Discrimination claims are not only about dismissal. Treatment during employment counts.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Decision-maker did not know of the attribute</h3>
<p>The family responsibilities claim failed, though the employee won on another ground. <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1623" at="[194]" phrase="was not aware of Mr Gavin's family responsibilities" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Record what the decision-maker knew. Winning on s.351 does not answer the other claims.</p>
</div>
</div>

      <span>File 2</span><b>Dev, a duty manager at the Newcastle hotel, posts on his personal social media criticising a political party's tourism policy. A guest complains</b>
      <span>File 2</span><b>The general manager emails the people team in Melbourne: "NSW doesn't protect political opinion, I checked. Can you send him a termination letter?"</b>

      <li><strong>No, not on these facts.</strong> Reducing Imogen's hours and adding a medical requirement are adverse action, and the manager's own message ties both to the pregnancy. If there is a specific safety concern about a particular duty, it should be identified, discussed with Imogen, and dealt with by the least restrictive means.       <li><strong>Start from the policy, and the policy does not appear to be breached.</strong> Dev's profile does not identify Larkspur. Unlike <em>Rumble</em>, there has been no direction and no repeated breach.
