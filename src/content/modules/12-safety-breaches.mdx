<p class="lead">Safety breaches are valid-reason cases with an extra dimension: the Commission weighs not just what the employee did, but what could have happened, how the rule has actually been enforced, and what the employer owes everyone else on site.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain why a safety-critical industry or role raises the bar for a valid reason, and write that into a termination letter.
- Separate "this caused real harm" from "this could have caused harm" without using either to excuse a breach or over-punish a near miss.
- Spot the inconsistent-enforcement and seniority arguments that most often decide safety-breach cases at the Fair Work Commission.

## Safety sits inside s.387(a), not beside it

Section 387(a) doesn't treat safety as a separate factor — it is folded into the valid reason test itself, which asks whether there was a valid reason for the dismissal related to the person's capacity or conduct, "including its effect on the safety and welfare of other employees" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 250" at="[61]" phrase="effect on the safety and welfare of other employees" />. The general valid reason test is covered in [Module 6](/modules/valid-reason); this module is about what changes when the conduct is a safety breach.

A useful Full Bench statement, quoted in *Plesiotis v Huhtamaki*, is that the "fair go all round" standard behind s.387 needs to weigh the employer's own safety obligations alongside the employee's conduct, "especially in inherently dangerous workplaces" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 120" at="[131]" phrase="especially in inherently dangerous workplaces" />. That authority (*BHP Coal Pty Ltd t/a BMA v Schmidt*) has not been read in the original for this guide — check the quote against the original before relying on it.

## The safety-critical nature of the role raises the bar

The more dangerous the environment, the less room there is for an employer to tolerate a breach — and the more weight a long, clean record loses against it.

- In a glass factory with "significant inherent risk from a safety perspective" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 250" at="[11]" phrase="significant inherent risk from a safety perspective" />, a driver's 30-year service and age weighed towards harshness, but the Commission found this was outweighed by "the criticality of safety in a high-risk work environment" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 250" at="[176]" phrase="criticality of safety in a high-risk work environment" />.
- In an electricity network crew where a customer received an electric shock, the Commission put it directly: "in an industry where the risks of injury and death are so high" the employer needs confidence that policies are followed every day, not most days <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1522" at="[235]" phrase="the risks of injury and death are so high" />.
- Even where the breach is described as a "near miss", the starting point in mining is that "the mining industry is an inherently dangerous industry" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 838" at="[148]" phrase="The mining industry is an inherently dangerous industry" /> — which is why the Commission still found a valid reason there despite no injury.

**For advisers:** name the hazard class in the termination letter (confined space, mobile plant, electrical isolation, patient handling, working at heights) rather than leaving "safety breach" unqualified. The seriousness of the reason stands or falls on that specific risk, not a general reference to "safety".

## Actual harm versus a near miss: both cut the wrong way if you let them

Two errors recur. The first is treating "no one was hurt" as a reason to go easy. The second is treating a near miss as automatically as serious as a breach that actually caused harm, with no further inquiry.

On the first: in the Viridian Glass driveway incident, the Commission expressly refused to grade the risk by what actually happened rather than what could have happened, because scoring by actual outcome "would then result in the downplaying of the significance or reporting of near misses" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 250" at="[120]" phrase="the downplaying of the significance or reporting of near misses" />. A near miss is assessed on its potential consequence, not on the fact that this time nothing happened to go wrong.

On the second: in a warehouse tugger collision with no injury and no property damage, the Commission found there was *no* valid reason for dismissal at all <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3924" at="[197]" phrase="I do not consider that the cross aisle incident provides a valid reason for dismissal" />, in part because the incident genuinely "did not result in injury to either person" and was not a high-potential incident <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3924" at="[196]" phrase="did not result in injury to either person" />. Severity still matters — it's the actual-versus-potential *framing* that doesn't excuse a breach, not the underlying facts of how dangerous this particular incident really was.

**The practical test:** ask what could have happened on a worse version of these facts (closing speed, load weight, who was standing where), not only what did happen. Then separately ask whether the facts, properly assessed, were actually serious enough to justify dismissal — a low-potential incident doesn't become high-potential just by calling it a "safety breach".

## Consistency of enforcement

Safety rules that have been enforced patchily in the past don't lose their force — but the employer carries the inconsistency, and the Commission will weigh it.

- An employer is entitled to tighten enforcement of a rule it had let slide, including after a near miss prompts a crackdown <Cite id="[2020] FWC 120" at="[130]" phrase="a lack of previous enforcement does not prevent the reinstatement of stricter rules" />. But that doesn't make the earlier inconsistency irrelevant — it's "certainly relevant to whether the Applicant has been treated in a manner consistent with other employees" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 120" at="[129]" phrase="certainly relevant to whether the Applicant has been treated in a manner consistent with other employees" />, and in that case it was found to weigh, on its own, in favour of unfairness <Cite id="[2020] FWC 120" at="[156]" phrase="inconsistent approach taken previously is a factor that weighs in favour of a finding that the termination is unfair" />.
- Comparators have to be genuinely comparable. A coal mine employee dismissed over a "near miss" succeeded partly because two colleagues involved in an *actual* collision — a more serious incident — were treated more leniently: the differential treatment "renders the termination of the Applicant unreasonable" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 838" at="[152]" phrase="renders the termination of the Applicant unreasonable" />.
- But not every different outcome is inconsistency. A warehouse operator's dismissal survived a consistency challenge because the comparator cases involved different incidents, different histories and no "cookie cutter" policy was being applied — the employer "properly, considers each matter separately, investigates the matter and takes action considered appropriate in the circumstances" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3924" at="[211]" phrase="properly, considers each matter separately, investigates the matter and takes action considered appropriate in the circumstances" />. Even the one directly comparable co-worker from the *same* incident wasn't a useful comparator, because he had under a year's service and no prior record against the Applicant's "close to 30 years" and recent history <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3924" at="[213]" phrase="Mr P had been employed for less than a year and the Applicant for close to 30 years" />.
- Seniority can itself explain a harsher outcome rather than undermine it. Where several employees were investigated over the same incident but only the most senior was dismissed, the Commission accepted that being "ultimately in charge" was the aggravating factor that justified the difference, not evidence of inconsistency <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1522" at="[231]" phrase="forms a basis for the differentiation in disciplinary outcomes is that Mr Paterson was ultimately in charge" />.

**For advisers:** before running (or resisting) a consistency argument, get the comparator's role, seniority, history and the *nature* of their incident on the table. "Someone else wasn't sacked for a safety breach" is not an argument until you know whether it's the same rule, the same risk and a genuinely comparable employee.

## Does experience cut for the employee, or against them?

Both, depending on what it's used to prove.

- Long service is a mitigating factor under s.387(h) generally, but in safety cases the Commission has turned it around: the long period of employment "needs to be balanced against the ... reasonable expectation that such a long serving employee would be in a position to understand and abide by the rules" <Cite id="[2020] FWC 120" at="[162]" phrase="reasonable expectation that such a long serving employee would be in a position to understand and abide by the rules" />.
- The same point was made more bluntly elsewhere: "long employment in the one area can lead to complacency", and that is never an excuse for a safety breach <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3924" at="[243]" phrase="Long employment in the one area can lead to complacency" />.
- Honesty under questioning is credited, but it is not a free pass. A crew leader's Show Cause response was found to be "brave and honest" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1522" at="[232]" phrase="His response to the Show Cause Letter was brave and honest" /> — the Commission weighed that genuinely in his favour, and upheld the dismissal anyway because his seniority made the underlying conduct more serious, not less.

**A related risk-management point advisers should flag on its own footing:** harsh, inconsistent discipline for an honestly self-reported near miss risks teaching a workforce to stop reporting near misses at all — which is the opposite of what a safety system needs. The decisions reviewed for this guide did not include a Commission decision squarely deciding a case on that chilling-effect argument, so don't present it to a client as settled Commission doctrine. It is, however, the same underlying reasoning the Commission used in rejecting an "actual outcome only" approach to risk-rating <Cite id="[2020] FWC 250" at="[121]" phrase="necessarily directed at what could have occurred as a result of an incident rather than what actually occurred" /> — and it is a legitimate reason, independent of case law, to design discipline around the breach and the role rather than around who happened to be unlucky enough to get caught, or honest enough to self-report.

## What the Commission looks for

Across these decisions, the pattern is consistent:

- The specific rule breached, and whether it is genuinely safety-related (not merely administrative dressed up as "safety").
- What could have happened on a worse version of the same facts — not only what actually happened.
- How the rule has actually been enforced across the workforce, including against comparable employees in comparable incidents.
- Whether the employee's role, seniority or experience made the breach more serious (greater responsibility, greater expected awareness of risk) or whether it's being used unfairly as a stand-in for "should have known better" without evidence.
- The employee's own response: candour and acceptance of responsibility are weighed, but they sit alongside the conduct, not instead of it.

<p>Dismissed for a "near miss" breach of mine safety rules while two colleagues involved in an actual, more serious collision kept their jobs. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 838" at="[152]" phrase="renders the termination of the Applicant unreasonable" /></p>
<p class="lesson">If your most severe penalty is reserved for the person who didn't get hurt, check your enforcement record before you rely on it.</p>

<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>Low-potential incident treated as high-potential</h3>
<p>A 30-year warehouse operator was dismissed over a low-speed tugger collision with no injury, no damage, and a comparator driver who was also at fault. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3924" at="[220]" phrase="no valid reason for the dismissal of the Applicant leads me to conclude that the dismissal was harsh, unjust or unreasonable" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Match the sanction to what actually happened and who else contributed, not to the label "safety breach" alone.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Point of principle</span>
<h3>Tightening a rule doesn't erase the history of not enforcing it</h3>
<p>The employer was entitled to start enforcing a pedestrian-walkway rule properly after a near miss, but its own past inconsistency still weighed toward unfairness. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 120" at="[156]" phrase="inconsistent approach taken previously is a factor that weighs in favour of a finding that the termination is unfair" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Document the date you started enforcing a rule properly, and be ready to own the gap before that date.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won, narrowly</span>
<h3>Long service nearly tipped it</h3>
<p>Thirty years' service and the employee's age weighed towards a finding of harshness, but the safety-critical nature of the role was found to outweigh it. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 250" at="[177]" phrase="application is therefore dismissed" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Don't assume a long, clean record is a defence in a high-risk role — but don't assume the business wins by a wide margin either. Build the safety-criticality case properly.</p>

## Practice scenario: the hoist that was "always slow"

  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>This is a fictional scenario for practice. Nadia is a Patient Services Assistant with 14 years' service at a metropolitan hospital, with no prior disciplinary history. The ward's manual-handling policy requires a mechanical patient hoist for any transfer assessed as "high risk", with a two-person lift only as a documented fallback when the hoist is unavailable.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>14:10</span><b>Hoist on Ward 4 logged as "slow to charge" (maintenance ticket, unresolved 6 days)</b>
      <span>14:22</span><b>Nadia and a graduate nurse perform a two-person manual transfer without the hoist</b>
      <span>14:23</span><b>Patient begins to slip; Nadia takes the weight and holds the transfer; no fall, no injury</b>
      <span>14:24</span><b>Nadia reports the near miss to the shift coordinator before finishing the shift</b>
    </div>
    <p>The ward manager wants Nadia dismissed, citing a "zero tolerance" manual handling policy. Two other staff were given a verbal reminder (not a written warning) for similar hoist-avoidance in the past year, but neither incident was escalated at the time. The hospital's hoist fleet has a known pattern of "slow to charge" tickets that often sit unresolved for days.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Not yet.</strong> There is a real safety breach (skipping the hoist without documenting the fallback), but you don't yet know whether the hoist was genuinely unavailable in a way that made the two-person lift a reasonable, if undocumented, fallback — or whether Nadia bypassed a working hoist to save time.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> whether the hoist was actually non-functional at 14:10 or merely slow; whether Nadia raised the fault before the transfer; how the two prior hoist-avoidance incidents were actually handled and by whom; and the hospital's own record on resolving "slow to charge" tickets, since an unmaintained fleet undercuts "zero tolerance" messaging.</li>
      <li><strong>Framing:</strong> "You performed a high-risk manual transfer without the hoist and without documenting the fallback assessment, contrary to policy." Don't frame it as reckless or dishonest — she held the patient safely and reported it herself within the hour. Separately, flag to the client (not as settled law, but as a live business risk) that treating this self-report more harshly than the two undocumented verbal-reminder cases risks signalling to staff that reporting a near miss is worse than staying quiet about one.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Hoist was working and Nadia knew it: a serious safety breach, and her experience cuts against her — she should have known better.</div>
      <div class="lo">Hoist fleet genuinely unreliable and others were only reminded verbally for the same thing: a written warning and a fix to the hoist maintenance backlog, not dismissal.</div>
    </div>
