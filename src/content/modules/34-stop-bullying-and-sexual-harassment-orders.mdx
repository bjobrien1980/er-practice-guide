<p class="lead">This module is about the employer's side of a worker's own application to the Commission: an application for an order to stop bullying, or for an order to stop sexual harassment. These are not dismissal cases. The Commission is not asked whether someone should lose their job. It is asked whether something has happened at work and whether it is at risk of continuing, and its orders look forward, not back.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Tell the stop-bullying and stop-sexual-harassment jurisdictions apart, and identify which provisions an application actually engages.
- Test an application against the two things the Commission must be satisfied of before it can make an order, including the future-risk requirement that decides many of these matters.
- Recognise when an employer's own management action, investigation and grievance process will shape the outcome.
- Explain to a manager why a stop-order application ending does not always end a sexual harassment dispute.

## Two jurisdictions with the same shape

[Module 13](/modules/bullying-and-harassment) deals with bullying and harassment as grounds for dismissing the person who did it. This module deals with the separate case where the person on the receiving end applies to the Commission.

There are two separate statutory schemes. Check which one an application is brought under before doing anything else.

| | Stop bullying | Stop sexual harassment |
|---|---|---|
| Where it sits | Part 6-4B of the Fair Work Act | Part 3-5A of the Fair Work Act |
| Application | s.789FC, by a worker who reasonably believes they have been bullied at work | s.527F, which can include an application for a stop sexual harassment order and can also ask the Commission to deal with the dispute in other ways |
| When an order can be made | s.789FF(1): the worker has been bullied at work, **and** there is a risk they will continue to be bullied at work by the same individual or group | s.527J(1): the person has been sexually harassed in contravention of Division 2, **and** there is a risk they will continue to be harassed by the same person or persons |
| What an order can't do | Require payment of money | Require payment of money |

The stop-bullying scheme covers **workers** in the work health and safety sense, not only employees (s.789FC(2)). A contractor, labour-hire worker or volunteer can be an applicant, and people other than the employer can be named. Part 3-5A uses its own terms: an "aggrieved person" and a "respondent". Check who it covers in the Act itself rather than assuming it matches the bullying scheme.

Sexual harassment applications moved in March 2023. Before 6 March 2023, a stop-sexual-harassment application was made under s.789FC, alongside bullying. From that date, new applications go under s.527F. The Fair Work Commission Rules keep the old s.789FC route for some conduct that started before that date. Check which provision the application names, because the procedure and the available outcomes are different.

## The first seven days

If your organisation is named as the employer or principal in either kind of application, it **must** lodge a response within 7 days after being served (rules 86 and 87 of the Fair Work Commission Rules 2024). An individual named as the person alleged to have engaged in the conduct may lodge a response but is not required to.

The Commission moves quickly on stop-sexual-harassment applications. Section 527J(2) requires it to start dealing with the stop-order part of the application within 14 days after the application is made. Expect a conference or directions early, and don't wait for the response deadline to start working out the facts.

In the first week, an adviser should:

- confirm who has been named: the employer, a principal (for a contractor), and any individuals;
- find out whether the applicant is still working, where, and alongside whom;
- collect any complaint already made internally and any investigation already under way or finished;
- check whether the individuals named have since left, moved roles or been separated from the applicant; and
- decide who will speak for the organisation, because the named individuals' interests and the employer's may not match.

## First question: has it happened, as the Act defines it?

### Bullying

A worker is bullied at work if, while at work in a constitutionally-covered business, an individual or group **repeatedly behaves unreasonably** towards them and that behaviour **creates a risk to health and safety** (s.789FD(1)). Section 789FD(2) says, to avoid doubt, that this does not apply to **reasonable management action carried out in a reasonable manner**.

Advisers often treat that subsection as a defence the employer has to prove. The Commission reads it differently. In *Yu v Star Track* it described s.789FD(2) as guidance on whether behaviour is unreasonable at all, using a three-part test: the behaviour must be management action; <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1853" at="[10]" phrase="it must be reasonable for the management action to have been taken" />; and <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1853" at="[10]" phrase="the management action must have been carried out in a manner that is reasonable" />.

So performance management, directions, work allocation and discipline don't stop being management action just because the worker experienced them as hostile. But the manner matters as well as the decision. A legitimate performance process run with sarcasm, public criticism or shifting goalposts can fail the third part of the test.

### Sexual harassment

The stop-sexual-harassment order depends on a contravention of Division 2 of Part 3-5A. Module 13 explains the objective test for sexual harassment, which comes from s.28A of the Sex Discrimination Act. The same test applies here. The respondent's intention does not answer it.

## Second question: is there a risk it will continue?

This is where many applications end. An order can only be made if there is a risk the conduct will continue, and that risk has to be real.

The Commission summarised the Full Bench principles in *Holt v GivEnergy Australia*. The scheme <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2117" at="[17]" phrase="is not directed at punishing past bullying behaviour or compensating the victims of such behaviour" />. The Commission <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2117" at="[17]" phrase="must be satisfied that there is a real (and not simply conceptual or hypothetical) risk" />. The same decision noted that Full Bench authority is that <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2117" at="[17]" phrase="Section 789FF does not limit the persons against whom orders can be made" />. Orders can be directed at the individuals involved and at their employer.

In *Yu*, the Commission described the risk as <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1853" at="[11]" phrase="the possibility of danger to health and safety, and not necessarily actual danger" />. It added that <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1853" at="[11]" phrase="the risk needs to be more than conceptual" />.

### When the working relationship has ended

The clearest situation where there is no risk is where the applicant no longer works with the people named.

- In *Application by Seonghee Joh*, the Commission's provisional view was that, <Cite id="[2025] FWC 131" at="[3]" phrase="as Ms Joh had moved on to other employment, there was no longer any risk that she would be sexually harassed in connection with work" />. The stop-order application was dismissed.
- In *Application by Wulantuya Fnu*, the applicant <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1331" at="[2]" phrase="had been dismissed and there was no longer any risk that she would be sexually harassed in connection with work" />.
- In *Holt*, the employer had stopped trading and gone into external administration. The applicant argued that the administration and the creditor process kept her connected to the person she had named. The Commission held that conduct is not at work merely because it has <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2117" at="[19]" phrase="a substantial connection to work" />. It also held that the Commission proceedings themselves didn't create a risk at work, because <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2117" at="[22]" phrase="the proceedings are not conducted at work" />.

Don't turn this into a rule that resignation or dismissal always ends the matter. *Holt* also notes that ceasing work raises the question, <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2117" at="[18]" phrase="But those circumstances will not always mean it is appropriate to dismiss" /> the application. A worker on leave, on a fixed-term break, or likely to return to the same site or roster may still be at risk.

**Don't engineer the outcome.** Moving or dismissing an applicant so that a stop order becomes impossible can be adverse action because they made a complaint or exercised a workplace right. See [Module 28](/modules/managing-an-employee-who-has-complained). Separating the parties to protect the applicant is a different thing. It is often exactly what should happen, and it may also be why the Commission later finds there is no continuing risk. Record why the step was taken.

## What orders can do, and what shapes them

Orders under both schemes look forward and cannot require payment of money (s.789FF(1) and s.527J(1)). They can be directed at behaviour, at contact between people, at how work is arranged, or at the employer's policies and processes.

When deciding the terms of an order, the Commission must take into account (s.789FF(2) and s.527J(3)):

- the final or interim outcomes of any investigation by another person or body;
- any procedure available to resolve grievances or disputes; and
- the outcomes of any such procedure.

This is where an employer's own work matters. A prompt, competent investigation, a grievance process that was actually used, and interim controls already in place are things the Commission is told to consider. See [Module 19](/modules/investigations). The applicant's conduct can count too. In *Yu*, the Commission noted that <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1853" at="[12]" phrase="An applicant's own conduct is a matter that the Commission may consider relevant" /> when it considers the terms of an order.

## A sexual harassment dispute doesn't end with the stop order

A s.527F application can do two things: seek a stop order, and ask the Commission to deal with the dispute. If the application is not only for a stop order, s.527R requires the Commission to deal with the dispute other than by arbitration, and any conference for that purpose is held in private. *Joh* and *Fnu* both show the two parts running separately. In *Fnu*, after the stop-order part was dismissed, the Commission said <Cite id="[2024] FWC 1331" at="[2]" phrase="I then conducted a private conference to deal with the dispute pursuant to s 527R" />. In *Joh*, a certificate issued after <Cite id="[2025] FWC 131" at="[2]" phrase="all reasonable attempts to resolve the dispute (other than by arbitration) had been, or were likely, to be unsuccessful" />.

After a certificate, the dispute can go further:

- **Consent arbitration (s.527S).** If both sides jointly notify the Commission within 60 days of the certificate, it can arbitrate, and arbitration orders **can** include compensation and lost remuneration.
- **Court (s.527T).** Otherwise the aggrieved person may apply to a court, generally within 60 days after the certificate is issued.

Advise managers accordingly. Getting the stop-order part dismissed because there is no future risk may leave a compensation claim about past conduct still alive. The no-prospects dismissal power works differently too. In the provision quoted in *Holt*, s.587(2) stops the Commission dismissing a s.527F application on that ground if it <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2117" at="[5]" phrase="does not consist solely of an application for a stop sexual harassment order" />.

## WHS law runs alongside

Both schemes say expressly that making an application doesn't stop proceedings under work health and safety law about the same conduct (s.789FH and s.527L). A stop-bullying application may arrive with, or after, a psychosocial hazard complaint to a regulator. Make sure the person handling the Commission matter knows what has been said to the regulator, and the other way round.

## What the Commission looks for

- **Both requirements, separately.** Was the worker bullied or harassed as the Act defines it, and is there a real risk it will continue? An application can fail on either.
- **Current facts.** Future risk is assessed when the Commission considers the application, not when the complaint was first made. Tell it what has changed since.
- **Management action described as management action.** What was decided, why, and how it was done. "We were entitled to performance manage" doesn't answer the question about manner.
- **What the employer has already done.** Investigation outcomes, grievance processes and interim measures are matters the Commission must take into account.

## How employer responses go wrong, and when they hold

<p>The employer had ceased trading and the applicant's employment had ended. Contact through the insolvency process and the Commission proceedings was not contact "at work". <Cite id="[2026] FWC 2117" at="[22]" phrase="the proceedings are not conducted at work" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Future risk is a question of fact. Give the Commission current, specific facts about whether the people involved will work together again.</p>

<p>The allegations included a threat to change the applicant's delivery region. ## Practice scenario: the leading hand and the night roster

<Scenario questions={["Which application has been made, and which provisions decide it?", "What would you need to show the Commission about the performance discussions?", "Does the roster change end the future-risk question?", "What should you tell the operations manager about where this could go next?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Priya, a process operator, has applied to the Commission for an order to stop bullying. She names her leading hand, Craig, and the employer. She says that over three months Craig criticised her in front of the crew at pre-starts, put her on the least popular tasks, and twice told her that "people like her don't last on this crew". Craig had also started a documented performance conversation about two missed safety checks.</p>
    <p>A week before the application, the operations manager moved Priya to a different crew on a different roster "to keep things calm". Priya says she didn't ask for this, that it cost her shift allowances, and that she still crosses paths with Craig at handover twice a week.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>A stop-bullying application under s.789FC.</strong> The order turns on s.789FF(1): was she bullied at work as s.789FD defines it, and is there a real risk it will continue? Lodge the employer's response within 7 days after service.</li>
      <li><strong>Performance discussions:</strong> raising missed safety checks is management action and may well be reasonable to take. The remarks at pre-starts and the "people like her" comments are a different matter. They go to unreasonable behaviour and to how the action was carried out. Gather dated records of the performance conversation separately from the evidence about the remarks.</li>
      <li><strong>Future risk:</strong> not necessarily. Twice-weekly handover contact may be a real risk. The move may also be challenged as adverse action against her because she complained, especially as it cost her allowances. Record why it was done, and consider whether there was a less detrimental way to separate them.</li>
      <li><strong>Next steps:</strong> the Commission must take into account any investigation and grievance outcomes. A prompt, properly scoped investigation into Craig's conduct may help Priya and the employer's position. Explain that stop-bullying orders can't award compensation, but that the move may lead to a separate general protections claim.</li>
    </ol>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
