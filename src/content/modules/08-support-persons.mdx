<p class="lead">Section 387(d) rarely decides a case on its own. But it trips up employers who think it means more than it does — and advisers who think it means less.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- State what s.387(d) actually asks, and explain why it isn't a duty to offer a support person unprompted.
- Tell the difference between limiting what a support person does in a meeting and refusing to let them be there at all.
- Recognise why this factor almost always weighs neutrally in practice — and spot the rarer decisions where it doesn't.

## The legal test

Section 387(d) asks two things, in order: did the employer refuse to allow the person to have a support person present to assist in discussions relating to the dismissal, and if so, was that refusal unreasonable <Cite id="[2026] FWC 75" at="[55]" phrase="whether there was a refusal by an employer to allow an employee to have a support person present, and if so whether such refusal was unreasonable" />.

There is **no positive obligation on an employer to offer** an employee the opportunity to have a support person <Cite id="[2022] FWC 3249" at="[201]" phrase="There is no positive obligation on an employer to offer an employee the opportunity to have a support person" />. The same proposition appears in close to identical wording in <Cite id="[2023] FWC 793" at="[101]" phrase="There is no positive obligation on an employer to offer an employee the opportunity to have a support person" /> — this looks like a stock paragraph used across many FWC matters, not a one-off observation, so don't treat it as novel reasoning when you see it quoted again elsewhere.

That proposition is not a universal operating instruction. An award, enterprise agreement, contract, policy or established process may require an employer to offer an opportunity, permit representation or follow a particular meeting process. The first question is therefore always: what source governs this workplace?

The factor only comes into play once a **request** has been made. Where no discussions about the dismissal took place before the employee was terminated, there is nothing for the employee to have asked for, and so no refusal to assess <Cite id="[2024] FWC 953" at="[53]" phrase="did not make any request for a support person" />.

## Support, not advocate

A support person's role is to **support** — not to advocate, negotiate, or answer questions on the employee's behalf. An employer who spells this out to a support person at the start of a meeting is not thereby refusing their presence <Cite id="[2022] FWC 3249" at="[63]" phrase="The role of a support person is not to be an advocate" />. The same limitation appears in a show-cause letter in a different matter, telling the employee in advance that his support person could not ask questions or advocate on his behalf <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1439" at="[17]" phrase="cannot ask questions or advocate on behalf of" />.

Where a support person goes beyond that role — interrupting, becoming aggressive, or trying to speak **for** the employee rather than beside them — an employer is on solid ground responding to that conduct. In one matter, a support person "began to interrupt the meeting in an aggressive manner and attempt to speak on behalf of the Applicant" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 3249" at="[67]" phrase="attempt to speak on behalf of the Applicant" />.

## Restricting participation is not the same as refusing presence

This is the distinction advisers most often get wrong in either direction. An employer that excludes a support person from attending **at all**, going forward, because that person was disruptive or tried to advocate the employee's case, is not thereby making an unreasonable refusal under s.387(d) — provided the conduct relied on actually happened <Cite id="[2022] FWC 3249" at="[203]-[204]" phrase="attempted to advocate the Applicant's case" />.

In that matter, the Commissioner gave two reasons: the exclusion happened before any discussions relating to dismissal took place, and the support person had been disruptive, argumentative and had tried to advocate the applicant's case at the meetings in question. The result: the factor weighed neutrally <Cite id="[2022] FWC 3249" at="[205]" phrase="This factor weighs neutrally in my consideration" />.

**What this is not** a licence for: silencing a support person who hasn't done anything, or treating an unequal level of professional representation — the employer's lawyer versus the employee's friend — as itself a s.387(d) problem. The provision tests refusal of presence, not equality of arms, and a submission that conflates the two has been rejected as misconceived <Cite id="[2026] FWC 75" at="[55]" phrase="This submission is misconceived" />.

## When has the Commission actually found an unreasonable refusal?

Rarely, on this research pass — and when it has, the fact pattern tends to be a plain, specific refusal, not a dispute about the support person's role. In one matter, the employer refused the employee's request that the dismissal discussion continue in the site office where his union representative was located; the Commission found that repeated refusal unreasonable <Cite id="[2023] FWC 3399" at="[41]" phrase="I am satisfied that Adcon did unreasonably refuse to allow a support person" />.

In another, the finding rested on thin ground: the employee asserted in her submissions that her request for a support person had been refused, but — unusually — "this factor was not further explored during the conference nor challenged by the Respondent" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2485" at="[45]" phrase="this factor was not further explored during the conference nor challenged by the Respondent" />. Left unanswered, the assertion stuck, and the Commission found the refusal unreasonable <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2485" at="[46]" phrase="I find the Respondent did unreasonably refuse to allow the Applicant to have a support person present at discussions relating to the dismissal" />. **The lesson for a respondent isn't about the law — it's about not leaving a factual assertion on the file unanswered.**

## How much weight does this factor carry?

Be honest with yourself about this before you spend much time on it. Across the decisions reviewed for this module, s.387(d) is treated as a **neutral** consideration far more often than it moves the outcome either way — including in matters the employer otherwise won <Cite id="[2022] FWC 3249" at="[205]" phrase="This factor weighs neutrally in my consideration" /> and in matters the employer otherwise lost <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1439" at="[55]" phrase="I therefore consider the criterion under s.387(d) of the Act to be a neutral consideration" />. On this research pass, it only carried independent weight where the refusal itself was clear-cut and specific — not where the complaint was really about how the support person was allowed to participate. Don't let a client over-invest in this ground; check the process factors in <span class="statute">s.387(b)</span> and <span class="statute">s.387(c)</span> first.

## How support person arguments fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>An unchallenged assertion is enough</h3>
<p>The employee said her request for a support person was refused. The employer never challenged it. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2485" at="[46]" phrase="I find the Respondent did unreasonably refuse to allow the Applicant to have a support person present at discussions relating to the dismissal" /></p>
<p class="lesson">If an applicant alleges a refusal, respond to it in evidence — silence reads as a concession.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>A specific, reasonable request, flatly refused</h3>
<p>The employee asked for the discussion to continue where his union representative was. The employer said no. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 3399" at="[41]" phrase="This repeated request was refused" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A concrete, logistically simple request for a support person's presence is hard to refuse safely. Think twice before saying no outright.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Unequal firepower isn't the test</h3>
<p>The employee argued that the employer's in-house counsel attending created an "imbalance of arms" requiring disclosure. Rejected as misconceived. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 75" at="[55]" phrase="This submission is misconceived" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Don't run a s.387(d) argument about the quality of representation on each side — it tests refusal of presence, nothing else.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won, narrowly</span>
<h3>Disruption, not presence, was the problem</h3>
<p>The support person was barred going forward after being disruptive and trying to advocate — not merely being present. <Cite id="[2022] FWC 3249" at="[203]-[204]" phrase="attempted to advocate the Applicant's case" /></p>
<p class="lesson">This only holds up if you can describe specific conduct, not a general preference for a quieter room. Write down what happened at the time.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the payroll show-cause meeting

<Scenario questions={["Is there a s.387(d) problem here, and if so, where exactly?", "Does telling the support person not to speak at all cross the line into an unreasonable refusal of presence?", "What should the manager have done when the nominated support person couldn't attend?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Leila is a payroll officer at a regional aged-care provider, facing a show-cause meeting over an alleged payroll data breach. She asks to bring her sister, who isn't a lawyer or paid agent, as a support person. The HR manager agrees, but tells the sister at the door: "You're here to support Leila, not to speak. If you say anything at all, I'll ask you to leave."</p>
    <p>Partway through the meeting, the sister says quietly, "Can I just clarify — the date on that email, is that the 3rd or the 13th?" The HR manager stops the meeting, reminds the sister of the rule, and warns that she will be removed if she speaks again. The meeting proceeds without further interruption.</p>
    <p>A second meeting is needed to finalise the outcome. Leila says her sister can't make the date proposed because of a hospital appointment, and asks for one extra day. The HR manager says the matter needs to be finalised on schedule and proceeds without any support person present at all.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>The first meeting is likely fine.</strong> A rule that the support person is there to support, not to answer or argue the case, matches the accepted role of a support person — and a single clarifying question about a date isn't advocacy, so reminding her of the rule (rather than making anything of it) was a reasonable response, not a refusal of presence.</li>
      <li><strong>Not from this alone.</strong> A blanket "don't say anything at all" rule announced at the door is more exposed than a rule tied to actual conduct — if the sister had been removed over a single clarifying question, that would start to look like excluding her for being present, not for misbehaving. The manager's warning stayed short of that line here, but it's close enough that advisers should flag it.</li>
      <li><strong>The real problem is the second meeting.</strong> Leila made a specific, modest request — one extra day, for a documented reason — and the manager proceeded without any support person rather than accommodating it or offering an alternative. That's much closer to the pattern the Commission has found unreasonable: a concrete request, simply refused.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what else is in the file:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">If the rest of the process was solid, this factor alone probably still won't decide the case — s.387(d) carries limited weight on its own.</div>
      <div class="lo">If other factors are also shaky, a flat refusal to accommodate a one-day, documented request adds a genuine data point against the employer, not just a technical one.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
