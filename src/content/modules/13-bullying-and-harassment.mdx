<p class="lead">This module looks at bullying, harassment and sexual harassment from one angle only: when an employer dismisses the person who did it. Each is tested differently, and none of them is proved by the label in the termination letter.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Tell workplace bullying, harassment and sexual harassment apart as legal tests, not just as words in a policy.
- Apply the objective test for sexual harassment correctly — including why the alleged harasser's own intent doesn't answer the question.
- Recognise why this module's treatment of the "positive duty" under the Sex Discrimination Act needs a currency check before you rely on it.

## Three different things, one angle

Keep the pathways separate at the outset. A bullying or sexual-harassment complaint may engage complaint handling, the Fair Work Act, the Sex Discrimination Act, the employer’s positive duty, WHS duties, discrimination law, general protections and—separately—the question whether the respondent’s conduct is a valid reason for discipline. An allegation is not a disciplinary finding, and no single process is prescribed for every workplace.

This module is about conduct dismissals: the employer says the employee bullied or harassed someone, and dismissed them for it. That is a completely different question from an employee bringing their **own** application — a stop-bullying order under s.789FC, or a sexual harassment application under Part 6-4B of the FW Act — against the employer or a colleague. Those are different jurisdictions with different tests and different remedies. This module doesn't cover them; it only flags that they exist, because an adviser handling a conduct dismissal should know the complainant may have a separate avenue open to them.

## Workplace bullying as a conduct ground

Workplace bullying has a specific legal shape: an individual or group **repeatedly** behaves **unreasonably** towards a worker, and that behaviour **creates a risk to health and safety**. A single incident, on its own, is not enough — even a serious one.

That test cuts both ways. Where it's made out, bullying conduct is a valid reason for dismissal:

- *Bale v Centrex* — Mr Bale sent emails to a colleague that were, on their face, belittling and insulting <Cite id="[2024] FWC 3231" at="[2]" phrase="The emails were, on their face, belittling and insulting" />. The Commission found this conduct was serious and self-evidently a valid reason for dismissal <Cite id="[2024] FWC 3231" at="[109]" phrase="Mr Bale's poor conduct was serious and was self-evidently a valid reason for dismissal" />.
- *Pawelczyk v Commonwealth Bank of Australia* — Mr Pawelczyk sent more than 50 messages to his manager over six days after she asked him to stop. It did not matter whether he intended harm; later she effectively begged him to cease what she felt was harassment <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2115" at="[11]" phrase="later effectively begged him to cease what she felt was harassment" />. The Commission found a valid reason for his dismissal <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2115" at="[56]" phrase="I am satisfied that there was a valid reason for the dismissal of Mr Pawelczyk" />.

But where the test isn't met, there's no valid reason — even if the employer's policy uses the word "bullying":

- *Walsh v Unitywater* — allegations arose from a single heated exchange after a colleague's promotion. The Commission found the evidence didn't support a finding that the conduct was repeated and unreasonable, or that it created a risk to health and safety, so it didn't rise to workplace bullying <Cite id="[2025] FWC 2853" at="[176]" phrase="the evidence does not support a finding that it was repeated and unreasonable nor that it created a risk to the health and safety of Ms Pohlner or others" />. There was no valid reason for the dismissal <Cite id="[2025] FWC 2853" at="[178]" phrase="I therefore conclude that there was no valid reason for the dismissal related to Mr Walsh's capacity or conduct" />.

**The adviser's question:** can you point to a pattern, not an incident, and to an actual risk to health and safety, not just hurt feelings or an awkward moment?

## Sexual harassment as a conduct ground

The legal meaning of "sexually harass" comes from s.28A of the Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth) <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1997" at="[62]" phrase="is found in section 28A of the Sex Discrimination Act 1984" />: an unwelcome sexual advance, an unwelcome request for sexual favours, or other unwelcome conduct of a sexual nature, in circumstances where a reasonable person would have anticipated that the person harassed would be offended, humiliated or intimidated. It's an **objective** test.

That matters for how an adviser frames the allegation:

- **Intent is irrelevant.** Whether the alleged harasser meant harm, or thought the conduct was consensual, doesn't answer whether a reasonable person would have anticipated that reaction <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1997" at="[70]" phrase="The intention of a person who engages in sexual harassment is irrelevant" />.
- **It's conduct that will ordinarily justify dismissal.** In *Swift v Highland Pine Products*, the Commission found Mr Swift sexually harassed three colleagues over an extended period <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1997" at="[67]" phrase="I find that Mr Swift sexually harassed Mr D, Ms B and Ms A" />, and held that sexual harassment is conduct of the kind that will ordinarily justify dismissal <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1997" at="[68]" phrase="Sexual harassment is conduct of the kind that will ordinarily justify dismissal" />.
- **Separate incidents can land differently.** In *Josey v OS MCAP*, the Commission found that conduct on a flight constituted sexual harassment, while a separate incident constituted harassment that wasn't sexual in nature <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2731" at="[154]" phrase="conduct relating to Incident 1 constituted harassment and her conduct as it relates to Incident 2 constituted sexual harassment" />. Applying the objective test, the Commission found a reasonable person would have anticipated the complainant would be offended, humiliated or intimidated <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2731" at="[150]" phrase="a reasonable person would have anticipated the possibility that a person would be offended, humiliated or intimidated by the conduct" />, and found a valid reason for dismissal <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2731" at="[156]" phrase="I find there was a valid reason for Ms Josey's dismissal" />.

**For serious or disputed allegations, the Commission takes care before making the finding** — the same heightened caution over inexact proof and indirect inference that applies to any serious misconduct allegation (see [Module 6](/modules/valid-reason)). A sexual harassment finding still has to be made on the evidence, not assumed from the label.

Record the compilation/check date; do not treat a later FWC decision's description or a party submission as a substitute for the primary source. This module's drafting pass did **not** independently verify the current wording, section numbering or commencement date of that duty against the Act itself; `law_current_as_at` is deliberately left unset in this module's frontmatter for that reason. Treat everything below as a description of how the FWC decisions reviewed for this guide have talked about the duty, not as a current statement of the law.

Decisions reviewed for this guide describe the positive duty's purpose, drawing on the Explanatory Memorandum to the Respect at Work reforms, as shifting the focus from employers responding to conduct that has already occurred, to requiring employers to proactively prevent discrimination and harassment in their workplaces <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1551" at="[29]" phrase="shift the focus from employers responding to conduct that has already occurred, to one which requires employers to proactively prevent discrimination and harassment in their workplaces" />. That decision is an enterprise-agreement dispute about how an employer handled a sexual harassment complaint, not an unfair dismissal matter — it's cited here only for that explanation.

In unfair dismissal matters, the duty has started to appear as part of why sexual harassment is treated as a serious conduct ground: in *Swift*, the Commission said that employers have a positive duty to take reasonable and proportionate measures to eliminate, as far as possible, unlawful sexual harassment, and that this duty applied to the employer at the time of the dismissal <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1997" at="[68]" phrase="employers have a positive duty to take reasonable" />. But *Swift*'s own dismissal took place well before the decision was handed down — a timing detail that, on its own, should prompt an adviser to check exactly when the duty commenced, rather than assume every post-2022 decision is applying it to a dismissal that occurred after it took effect. In *Smout v BHP Coal*, the employer's own submission (not an independent finding) invoked "its positive duty to prevent or eliminate workplace sexual harassment" <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2062" at="[106]" phrase="its positive duty to prevent or eliminate workplace sexual harassment, sex discrimination and sex-based harassment under the SDA" />, and in *Josey v OS MCAP* the Commission simply noted a similar submission without independently testing it <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2731" at="[155]" phrase="their legislated positive duty to eliminate sexual harassment in the workplace" />.

The practical point for an adviser: don't build an argument on "the employer has a positive duty, therefore X" without first checking the Act's current text and commencement provisions yourself. This is exactly the kind of recent, fast-moving area where last year's summary can be wrong.

## What the Commission looks for

- **The right test for the right label.** Bullying needs repetition and a health-and-safety risk; sexual harassment needs unwelcome conduct of a sexual nature assessed objectively. Don't let a termination letter's choice of word substitute for which test actually applies.
- **Contemporaneous evidence**, not a characterisation written after the fact: messages, dated complaints, witness accounts of what was actually said or done.
- **The reasonable-person test, not the respondent's account of their own intent** — "I didn't mean it that way" or "I talk to everyone like that" doesn't answer the legal question.
- **A genuine process**, run at a pace driven by fairness to the employee, not by a deadline that suits the employer.

## How bullying and harassment dismissals fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>The conduct didn't meet the legal bar</h3>
<p>A heated, one-off exchange after a colleague's promotion was treated as workplace bullying. The evidence didn't show repetition or a risk to health and safety. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 2853" at="[176]" phrase="the evidence does not support a finding that it was repeated and unreasonable nor that it created a risk to the health and safety of Ms Pohlner or others" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A strong label ("bullying") isn't a substitute for the statutory elements. Check repetition and the health-and-safety risk separately.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Reasoning undermined</span>
<h3>Valid reason, but the process was rushed for convenience</h3>
<p>Sexual harassment was proven and gave a valid reason to dismiss. But the process was compressed because of an impending ownership transfer and nobody wanting to work over a long weekend — not because of anything about the employee. <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2062" at="[181]-[182]" phrase="This rushed process was all because of the pending ownership transfer of the mine and because no one from the Respondent's HR team wanted to work over the Easter long weekend" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Even a well-proven conduct case can still produce an unfair dismissal if the employer's own scheduling convenience drives the process.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Defence rejected</span>
<h3>"I didn't mean it that way" isn't the test</h3>
<p>An employee argued he sent similar messages to friends and relatives and didn't think they were inappropriate. The Commission applied the objective test regardless of his own view. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1997" at="[69]-[70]" phrase="The intention of a person who engages in sexual harassment is irrelevant" /></p>
<p class="lesson">When advising on the allegation, frame it around what a reasonable person would think, not around what the respondent says they meant.</p>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the after-hours messages

<Scenario questions={["Is there conduct here that could be a valid reason for dismissal, and under which test?", "What separate complaint, safety or positive-duty pathway may also be engaged?", "What non-dismissal outcome remains open?", "What's missing before you could advise dismissal?", "How would you frame the allegation in a show cause letter?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Deon is a senior merchandising coordinator at a retail head office. Tess, a junior colleague, reported to HR that over several weeks Deon repeatedly commented on her appearance in front of others and sent her personal messages outside work hours after she told him she wasn't interested in socialising beyond work. There has been no physical contact alleged.</p>
    <p>HR holds message records showing the pattern continued after Tess's request to stop. Deon, when informally asked, says the comments were meant as compliments, that he talks to all his colleagues that way, and that he didn't think anything of it.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Possibly, under more than one test.</strong> The repeated, unwanted comments and messages after a clear request to stop could support a bullying finding (repeated, unreasonable, capable of creating a health-and-safety risk) and, depending on their content, could also meet the objective test for sexual harassment. Deon's own view that he "didn't mean anything by it" doesn't resolve either question — both tests ask how the conduct would be viewed, not how he intended it.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> the actual content and dates of the comments and messages (not a paraphrase); whether Tess told Deon to stop and when; whether anyone witnessed the comments; whether Deon has made similar comments to other colleagues; and Deon's full, considered response to specific examples, not just an informal reaction.</li>
      <li><strong>Framing:</strong> set out specific dated messages and comments, state that they continued after Tess asked him to stop, and identify precisely which policy or conduct standard each instance breached — rather than a single broad allegation of "inappropriate behaviour".</li>
    </ol>
    <p><strong>Non-dismissal outcome:</strong> the immediate response might be a properly scoped complaint process, interim risk control, conduct direction or facilitated resolution rather than a disciplinary finding. That depends on the evidence, the parties' safety and wishes, the applicable sources and the organisation's arrangements.</p>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
