<p class="lead">"Stand down" and "suspend" are not interchangeable. One is a narrow statutory power to stop paying for work that genuinely doesn't exist. The other needs a basis of its own, and getting that basis wrong can turn a routine investigation into a repudiation of the contract.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Apply the narrow statutory test for standing an employee down without pay under s.524, and recognise when a withdrawal of work isn't a stand down at all.
- Explain why suspension pending a disciplinary investigation needs its own legal basis, and why paid suspension carries far less risk than unpaid.
- Spot the pattern where an employer calls something a "stand down" to justify not paying someone, with nothing in s.524 or the contract to support it.

## Statutory stand down: s.524

Section 524 lets an employer stand down an employee **without pay** during a period in which the employee cannot usefully be employed because of one of three specific causes:

<div class="key">
<h3>The words of the section</h3>
<p class="source-note">Reproduced in <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1774" at="[6]" phrase="cannot usefully be employed because of one of the following circumstances" />.</p>
<ol class="principles">
<li>Industrial action (other than industrial action organised or engaged in by the employer).</li>
<li>A breakdown of machinery or equipment, if the employer cannot reasonably be held responsible for the breakdown.</li>
<li>A stoppage of work for any cause for which the employer cannot reasonably be held responsible.</li>
</ol>
</div>

This is **not** a general "we have no work for you" power. It doesn't cover a quiet trading period, a lost contract, or a manager deciding someone is surplus to requirements for the week. It only applies where the employee cannot usefully be employed, and that inability has to be caused by one of the three things above.

A Full Bench put the limiting principle squarely: s.524 "concerns circumstances in which there is an absence of work to be done, rather than a circumstance in which there is work to be done but the employee is not willing to perform it" <Cite id="[2025] FWCFB 274" at="[52]" phrase="an absence of work to be done, rather than a circumstance in which there is work to be done but the employee is not willing to perform it" />. The premise of the whole section is that the employee is ready and willing to work and there's genuinely nothing for them to do.

### The two-step test

The Commission applies a two-step test to decide whether a stand down is authorised:

1. **Can the employee be usefully employed during the stand-down period?** If there's useful work available — anywhere in the business, not just in the employee's usual role — s.524 doesn't apply and no further inquiry is needed.
2. **If not, is that because of one of the three causes in s.524(1)?** Where the cause relied on is a stoppage of work, the employer additionally has to show there was an actual stoppage, and that it can't reasonably be held responsible for it <Cite id="[2024] FWC 3306" at="[149]" phrase="cannot reasonably be held responsible for the stoppage of work and could not reasonably have prevented the stoppage" />.

In *APESMA v Wollongong Resources*, a coal mine was stood down after a government regulator issued a Prohibition Notice stopping coal production outright. The Commission found there was a genuine stoppage of work, no useful work available, and — critically — that the employer had engaged independent experts, cooperated with the regulator, and taken every reasonable step to lift the notice. That combination meant it could not reasonably be held responsible for the stoppage, and the stand down was lawful <Cite id="[2024] FWC 3306" at="[149]" phrase="cannot reasonably be held responsible for the stoppage of work and could not reasonably have prevented the stoppage" />. Contrast that with a stoppage the employer caused or dragged its feet on fixing — the same test would likely go the other way.

### Industrial action is a cause, not a label

The industrial-action limb catches genuine attention: it's tempting to assume that if an employee is engaged in protected industrial action and isn't being paid, s.524(1)(a) must apply. It doesn't automatically. The cause has to be that the industrial action means there's **no useful work at all** — not that the employee has declined to do a particular task as part of a work ban while other useful work exists. A Full Bench corrected exactly this error on appeal, where a Commissioner had treated an employee's refusal (as protected industrial action) to accept redeployment to another ward as itself satisfying s.524(1)(a), even though there was useful work available on that other ward <Cite id="[2025] FWCFB 274" at="[53]" phrase="was not entitled to stand down Ms Coombes under s 524(1)(a)" />.

## Suspension pending a disciplinary investigation

Suspension is not automatically routine or low-risk. Check the source of the power, whether pay may be withheld, the purpose and duration of the suspension, any applicable instrument or policy, and whether the decision appears to prejudge the outcome. Safety, evidence-preservation and wellbeing considerations may justify interim action, but they do not remove the need to identify the legal and contractual basis.

Suspension is a different tool entirely, and it is **not a standalone statutory right**. There is no equivalent of s.524 that lets an employer suspend an employee — paid or unpaid — simply because it wants to investigate an allegation. The power to suspend has to come from somewhere: an express term of the contract of employment, an award, an enterprise agreement, or some other statutory source.

Where that basis exists and is followed, suspension may be available, but its legal and practical risk still depends on the purpose, duration, pay position, process, facts and applicable instrument. In *Kerig v Victoria University*, the applicable enterprise agreement set out a clear two-step clause: first, whether the relevant officer considered the allegations serious enough to warrant the employee not being at work <Cite id="[2020] FWC 1461" at="[63]" phrase="the allegations are serious enough to warrant the staff member not being at work" />; second, whether the resulting suspension should be with or without pay. Because the University followed that clause, the Commission found it had proper grounds to commence the misconduct process and to suspend the employee without pay while it did so <Cite id="[2020] FWC 1461" at="[146]" phrase="grounds on which to commence the serious misconduct process with respect to Mr Kerig" />.

Where there is **no** such basis, unpaid suspension is exposed. A Federal Court decision squarely on this point has been quoted, in a litigant's own correspondence, for the proposition that "at common law, an employer has no right to suspend an employee without pay" <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1530" at="[18]" phrase="an employer has no right to suspend an employee without pay" />. That authority has not been checked against its own text for this guide — see the note under Decisions cited — but the underlying point is consistent with everything else in this module: withholding pay needs a source of authority, and "we're investigating" isn't one by itself.

**Paid suspension is a different risk category.** If an employee keeps being paid while an investigation runs, the financial harm that drives most disputes about stand down and suspension simply doesn't arise, and there's rarely an incentive to litigate the suspension decision itself (as opposed to what the investigation ultimately finds). Unpaid suspension, by contrast, puts the employer squarely in the position of withholding money owed under the contract — and if there's no clause, award term or statute that allows that, the employer is at serious risk of repudiating the contract.

### Withholding pay without a basis is repudiation risk, not a free option

The clearest illustration, among the decisions reviewed for this guide, of what happens when an employer withholds work and pay with **no** basis in either the contract or s.524 is *Poulter v JA Concreting* — not framed as a disciplinary suspension, but the underlying principle is identical. The applicant's own submission captured it directly: "an employer does not have the right to stand an employee down without pay at common-law" <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1441" at="[11]" phrase="an employer does not have the right to stand an employee down without pay at common-law" />. The Commission agreed there was no evidence any of the s.524(1) causes existed <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1441" at="[25]" phrase="there is no evidence before me those circumstances existed with respect to Mr Poulter" />, and found that withholding work and pay without authority amounted to a repudiation of the contract which the employee had accepted — meaning the employment was terminated at the employer's initiative <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1441" at="[29]" phrase="terminated at the initiative of his employer, JA Concreting, within the meaning of s 386(1)(a)" />. The same reasoning applies whether the employer calls it a "stand down" or a "suspension": the label doesn't matter, the authority does.

## What the Commission looks for

- **For a s.524 stand down:** a specific, identifiable cause within s.524(1)(a)–(c); genuine absence of useful work (checked across the business, not just the employee's usual duties); and, for the stoppage-of-work limb, evidence the employer took reasonable steps and isn't itself responsible for the stoppage.
- **For suspension pending investigation:** an actual source of power — a contract clause, award term, enterprise agreement clause, or statute — that was properly engaged; whether the decision to suspend (and whether to withhold pay) followed whatever process that source requires; and, if unpaid, whether there was any evidential basis for withholding pay specifically, as distinct from suspending at all.

## How stand-downs and suspensions fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employer's position failed</span>
<h3>No basis, no pay, no defence</h3>
<p>Work and pay were withheld with nothing in the contract and none of the s.524 causes present. The Commission treated this as a repudiation the employee had accepted. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 1441" at="[25]-[26]" phrase="there is no evidence before me those circumstances existed with respect to Mr Poulter" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Before you stop paying someone, identify the specific source of authority. If you can't point to a clause or a s.524(1) cause, don't do it.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Reasoning overturned on appeal</span>
<h3>A work ban isn't automatically "no useful work"</h3>
<p>A Commissioner accepted that an employee's refusal of redeployment, as protected industrial action, meant she couldn't be usefully employed "because of" industrial action — even though there was useful work available. The Full Bench held this conflated unwillingness to perform available work with a genuine absence of work. <Cite id="[2025] FWCFB 274" at="[52]-[53]" phrase="an absence of work to be done, rather than a circumstance in which there is work to be done but the employee is not willing to perform it" /></p>
<p class="lesson">"The employee won't do this task" and "there is no task for the employee to do" are different findings. Only the second supports s.524(1)(a).</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Wrong tool, wrong label</span>
<h3>A vaccination direction is not a stand down</h3>
<p>A union argued its member had been unlawfully stood down under s.524 when his employer stopped paying him for not complying with a vaccination requirement. The Commission found the employer never purported to rely on s.524 at all — the real dispute was about the lawfulness of a workplace direction, a different legal question with a different pathway. <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1774" at="[31]-[32]" phrase="did not stand Mr Salha down under section 524 of the Act, and nor did it purport to stand him down" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Work out which legal question you're actually asking before you reach for s.526. "Not being paid" isn't the same as "stood down".</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Challenge failed — the basis was there</span>
<h3>An express clause did the work</h3>
<p>An employee argued his unpaid suspension pending a misconduct investigation was invalid. The enterprise agreement had its own two-step suspension clause, the employer followed it, and the challenge failed. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 1461" at="[146]" phrase="grounds on which to commence the serious misconduct process with respect to Mr Kerig" /></p>
<p class="lesson">This is what a defensible unpaid suspension looks like: a specific clause, properly engaged, properly followed. Build that paper trail before you act, not after.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the warehouse and the "stood down" email

<Scenario questions={["Was Kiri's absence a valid s.524 stand down?", "Is Dion's unpaid suspension defensible as it stands?", "What should the ER adviser tell the operations manager to do differently?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Fictional scenario. Birchgate Logistics runs a regional distribution warehouse. Two things happen on the same Monday.</p>
    <p><strong>Kiri</strong> operates the warehouse's only reach forklift. The forklift's hydraulic mast fails mid-shift. Birchgate's maintenance contractor diagnoses a cracked hydraulic cylinder and says a replacement part is three days away. There is no other forklift on site and no other role Kiri is qualified or available to perform that day. The operations manager sends Kiri home and marks her "stood down under s.524, unpaid" for the three days.</p>
    <p><strong>Dion</strong> works in the same warehouse as a picker. A supervisor reports that stock is going missing from Dion's aisle. The operations manager emails Dion: "You're stood down, effective immediately, unpaid, while we investigate." Dion's contract is silent on suspension. The modern award covering the site has no suspension clause either. There is picking and packing work available elsewhere in the warehouse that Dion could do.</p>

  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Kiri: likely yes, on these facts.</strong> There is a breakdown of equipment (s.524(1)(b)), Birchgate is not shown to be responsible for a cracked hydraulic cylinder failing without warning, and there is no useful work available for Kiri during the parts delay. That is a textbook stand-down scenario — provided the business genuinely checked for other work for Kiri across the site, not just in her usual role.</li>
      <li><strong>Dion: no, not as it stands.</strong> This isn't a stand down at all — none of the s.524(1) causes exist; the business simply doesn't want Dion at work during an investigation. Dion's contract and the award are both silent on suspension, so there's no identified source of power to suspend him, let alone to suspend him unpaid. On top of that, there's useful work available elsewhere in the warehouse that Dion could do while the investigation runs, which would defeat a s.524 argument even if one were attempted.</li>
      <li><strong>What should change:</strong> stop calling Dion's situation a "stand down" — it isn't one. Check whether there is any other source of a right to suspend (a policy incorporated into the contract, for example). If there isn't, the safer course is to suspend Dion on full pay while the investigation runs, rather than risk a finding that withholding his pay without authority repudiated his contract. Document the decision, the reason, and the expected timeframe either way.</li>
    </ol>
