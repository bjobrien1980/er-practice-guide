<p class="lead">A post made at home, after hours, to a closed group of "friends" still ends up in front of the Commission more often than people expect — and "it was private" protects an employee far less reliably than most people assume.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Apply the out-of-hours connection-to-work test specifically to online conduct, using the multi-factor test the Commission applies to decide whether a post is "sufficiently connected" to the employment.
- Explain why a post shared only with "friends" or in a closed group isn't reliably shielded from employer action — and why, counter-intuitively, the same privacy setting can also work against a finding of a valid reason.
- Spot when a social media policy actually covers the conduct relied on, and when the employer has reached for the wrong clause.

## The connection-to-work test, extended to social media

Connection to work is not established merely because an employer dislikes an opinion or because a post is embarrassing. Check for workplace rights, industrial activity, political opinion and other protected attributes; confirm the post’s authenticity, completeness and lawful source; and ask whether the policy actually covered the conduct and was communicated.

Module 5 covers the general out-of-hours test from *Rose v Telstra*: conduct must, viewed objectively, be likely to cause serious damage to the employment relationship, damage the employer's interests, or be incompatible with the employee's duty. For online conduct specifically, a Full Bench of the Commission — described in one decision as *Sydney Trains v Bobrenitsky* (not independently located in this corpus under that citation; check the quote against the original before relying on it) — refined that test further: "it is axiomatic that for conduct to indicate a rejection or repudiation of the employment contract, the out of hours conduct must be sufficiently connected to the employee's employment. Not every connection between out of hours conduct and employment, will constitute a valid reason for dismissal" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 907" at="[116]" phrase="the out of hours conduct must be sufficiently connected to the" />.

Working out whether that connection exists means looking at the whole picture, not just whether a post exists: "the nature of the out of hours conduct and what it involved; where the out of hours conduct occurred; the circumstances in which the out of hours conduct occurred; the nature of the employment; the role and duties of the employee concerned; the principal purpose of the employee's employment; the nature of the employer's business; express and implied terms of the contract of employment; the effect of the conduct on the employer's business; and the effect of the conduct on other employees of the employer" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 907" at="[116]" phrase="the nature of the out of hours conduct and what it involved" />.

A post's permanence also matters. A Facebook post "does not stop once work recommences. It remains on Facebook until removed, for anyone with permission to access the site to see" — and "it would therefore be foolish of employees to think they may say as they wish on their Facebook page with total immunity from any consequences" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3030" at="[32]" phrase="It would therefore be foolish of employees to think they may say as they wish on their Facebook page with total immunity from any consequences" />.

## Public, "friends only", or screenshotted: what actually matters

A genuinely instructive pair of decisions shows that "private" versus "public" is not itself the test — and that a private setting can cut either way.

In one matter, a swim instructor replied to a stranger's request for a recommendation inside a **16,100-member private community Facebook group**, naming a competing swim school instead of her own employer. A colleague who happened to see the reply through her own Facebook notifications took a screenshot of the entire thread and forwarded it to management <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1952" at="[13]" phrase="took a series of screenshots of the entire conversation" /> — even though both women had their profiles set to private <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1952" at="[12]" phrase="it would not have been apparent to a reader of the" />. A private audience is not a sealed one: anyone inside it, including a colleague, can copy and carry content straight to an employer, with or without malice.

But the Commission's own reasoning then cut the other way on liability. Because the Applicant's settings were private, "it would not have been apparent to persons reading her post, other than those directly familiar with her employment history, that she worked for the Respondent" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1952" at="[45]" phrase="the Applicant's Facebook settings were private" />. That absence of any visible link to the employer was one of the reasons the Commission found the dismissal was not consistent with the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1952" at="[46]" phrase="not consistent with the Code" /> and was ultimately not satisfied a valid reason for dismissal was established at all <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1952" at="[52]" phrase="I am not persuaded that the conduct was of such gravity that it established a valid reason" />.

Contrast a fully public post where the Applicant had identified himself on his own profile as an employee of the Respondent <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3030" at="[38]" phrase="identify on his Facebook profile page that he was an employee of the Respondent" />. The Commission accepted it would be "fanciful" to think the public would attribute his personal opinions to the business — but found that naming the employer on his profile nevertheless "associated the organisation with those Facebook postings, such that a person may perceive the opinions to be representative of the Respondent" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3030" at="[44]" phrase="associated the organisation with those Facebook postings" />. A valid reason was found, and the dismissal was not unfair <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3030" at="[69]" phrase="the Applicant's dismissal was not unfair" />.

Read together: what matters is (1) whether the content in fact reached people who matter — which privacy settings do not reliably prevent — and (2) whether, on an objective look, a reader could actually connect the post to the employer and to the employee's role in it. A truly untraceable private post is a weaker case for dismissal than a fully public one naming the employer, even where both are seen by similar numbers of people.

A third matter shows the same tension again. The Applicant there argued that the conduct in his private Facebook/messenger group "continue[d] not to be in the public domain" and that, but for his dismissal and the ensuing proceedings, there was no reason to think it ever would be <Cite id="[2023] FWC 907" at="[83]" phrase="is not currently in the public domain" />. He also argued that, to amount to harassment, conduct must be "directed towards a person" and that "the context was a private group of consenting men" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 907" at="[88]" phrase="must be directed towards a person" />. Neither argument prevented a valid reason being found — because the post the Commission actually relied on (an on-shift comment disrespectful of management) breached a policy whose application didn't turn on publicness at all.

## Posts about colleagues, managers, clients or the business

Three recurring patterns, regardless of audience size:

- **Discriminatory content.** Public Facebook posts described by the Commission as "vituperative criticism of Aboriginal people, women, and persons in the media" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3030" at="[43]" phrase="a reasonable person would be offended by the language used" />. Intent to offend is irrelevant to the finding — "whether the Applicant sought to offend by his diatribe(s) is of no consequence" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3030" at="[43]" phrase="of no consequence" />.
- **Sexual or demeaning content about a named colleague.** A sexually explicit meme referencing a specific female colleague, posted to a personal but public Facebook page <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2570" at="[256]" phrase="the sexually explicit and public nature of posting the first meme to social media" />. The employee argued a "robust" sales-floor culture made it acceptable; the Commission held the conduct was "still sufficiently serious and inappropriate so as to constitute a valid reason" regardless <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2570" at="[257]" phrase="the Applicant's conduct was still sufficiently serious and inappropriate so as to constitute a valid reason" />.
- **Disrespectful comments about management, even inside a closed group.** An on-shift post mocking a manager's new vehicle was found to breach a Bullying and Harassment Policy defining harassment as "any unacceptable action, conduct or behaviour that a reasonable person would find unwelcome, humiliating, intimidating or offensive" <Cite id="[2023] FWC 907" at="[120]" phrase="Harassment is any unacceptable action, conduct or behaviour that a reasonable person would find unwelcome" />.

None of these findings turned on the post being public. What mattered was the content, and who or what it targeted.

## Social media policies: what they can — and can't — validly restrict

A social media policy survives scrutiny when its reach is tied to the employment connection, not to an employee's life generally. In one matter the Commission was satisfied a policy "provided lawful and reasonable instruction" because "the Policy's scope did not overreach. It sought only to regulate the social media use of its employees to the extent where employees made reference to the Respondent, or by inference, the staff member could be seen to be associated with the Respondent" <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3030" at="[36]" phrase="the Policy's scope did not overreach" />. A policy that tried to control an employee's entirely unconnected private opinions would not get the same answer.

Equally, the specific policy an employer relies on has to actually cover the conduct. In the private-group matter discussed above, the employer pointed to several policies — a Bullying and Harassment Policy, a Code of Conduct, a Social Media Statement and the contract of employment — but the Commission's eventual valid-reason finding rested on two particular breaches it identified itself: the Bullying and Harassment Policy's harassment definition, for the on-shift post about a manager, and a separate requirement for Defence approval before publishing photos of the worksite, for an unrelated photo <Cite id="[2023] FWC 907" at="[121]" phrase="On the basis of the two breaches of the Respondent" />. A broad "Social Media Policy" argument that doesn't map onto the actual post relied on won't do the work an employer needs it to do.

## What the Commission looks for

- **The whole factual matrix**, not a single fact like "it was public" or "it was private" — nature of the conduct, where and how it occurred, the employee's role, the nature of the business, the contract terms, and the effect on the business and on colleagues.
- **A real, demonstrable link to the employment** — the post names the employer, identifies the person as an employee, targets a named colleague, manager or client, or is otherwise traceable back to the workplace by someone who sees it.
- **A policy clause that actually covers the conduct**, not just a policy that exists somewhere in the handbook.
- **Content and target, not audience size** — a defamatory, discriminatory or harassing post about a colleague or the business is assessed on what it says and who it's about, whether ten people saw it or ten thousand.

## How social media dismissals fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>No one could tell she worked there</h3>
<p>A private-group post with no visible link to the employer; the Commission wasn't satisfied a valid reason was established. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1952" at="[52]" phrase="I am not persuaded that the conduct was of such gravity that it established a valid reason" /></p>
<p class="lesson">If no reader could connect the post to your business, the connection-to-work test is hard to satisfy — however embarrassing the post is privately.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Naming your employer turns your opinions into the business's risk</h3>
<p>A public, discriminatory post on a profile that identified the poster as an employee. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3030" at="[44]" phrase="associated the organisation with those Facebook postings" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Identifying your employer on a public profile converts your own views into a reputational exposure for the business — whether or not anyone thinks the business shares them.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer won on the posts, lost overall</span>
<h3>Right policy breach, wrong process</h3>
<p>Two real policy breaches were found, but inconsistent treatment of a comparable colleague who kept his job made the dismissal unfair overall. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 907" at="[157]" phrase="Mr Gregory remains employed" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A valid reason doesn't end the inquiry. Check how comparable conduct by others was actually treated before you rely on it.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>"Everyone talks like that here" isn't a defence</h3>
<p>A sexually explicit meme about a named colleague, defended as normal for a "robust" workplace culture. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2570" at="[257]" phrase="the Applicant's conduct was still sufficiently serious and inappropriate so as to constitute a valid reason" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Even a genuinely crude workplace culture has a floor. Demeaning a named colleague on social media can cross it regardless.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the community group post

<Scenario questions={["Is there a valid reason yet?", "What protected-expression or non-dismissal outcome remains open?", "What's missing before you could rely on it?", "How would you frame the allegation?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>This scenario is fictional. Priya is a team leader at a mid-size homewares retail chain, with four years' service and no prior warnings. Outside work, in a local "buy, swap, sell" Facebook group for her suburb (a closed group of about 4,000 residents, not public), Priya posts a complaint about a rostering dispute:</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Fri 9:47pm</span><b>Priya posts: "Shoutout to my manager Craig at [Store Name] for cancelling my leave again. Love working for a business that treats staff like this 🙃"</b>
      <span>Sat</span><b>A regular customer, who is a member of the group and recognises Priya from the shop floor, screenshots the post and emails it to the store's head office.</b>
    </div>
    <p>Priya's profile doesn't say where she works. The store's name appears only in her post, spelled out in full. Craig is named by first name only. The company has a one-paragraph "Social Media Guidelines" clause buried in the staff handbook, which says employees should "behave professionally online," but it has never been raised in training and doesn't mention naming the business or colleagues.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Arguably, but it's thin.</strong> Priya named the store and her manager, which supplies the objective connection the test asks for — this isn't an anonymous complaint nobody could trace. But the content is a single sarcastic comment about a rostering decision, not discriminatory, harassing or defamatory material, and there's no evidence yet of actual damage to the business's reputation or relationship with Craig.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> whether the post was seen by anyone beyond the one customer who forwarded it; whether Craig's leave decision was itself reasonable (if it wasn't, the post may be an accurate, if undiplomatic, complaint rather than misconduct); whether the handbook clause was ever communicated to staff in a way that would let the business rely on it; and how similar venting by other staff has been treated in the past.</li>
      <li><strong>Framing, if you proceed at all:</strong> "You published a post on [date] naming the store and your manager by name, viewable to members of a 4,000-person public-facing community group, in terms that were disrespectful of your manager." Avoid framing it as a breach of "the Social Media Policy" unless that policy actually addresses naming colleagues or the business — on these facts, it doesn't clearly reach this conduct.</li>
    </ol>
    <p style="margin-bottom:6px"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Repeated pattern of similar posts, after a clear and communicated policy, with demonstrated reputational effect: a written warning is more defensible than dismissal on a first instance like this.</div>
      <div class="lo">One-off venting about a genuine grievance, no policy ever communicated, no evidence anyone beyond one customer saw it: no valid reason — this looks closer to the "no visible connection" failure pattern above than to a case naming the business to the world.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
