<p class="lead">Absenteeism and abandonment sound like the same topic. They aren't. One is an ordinary valid-reason question. The other can mean there was never a dismissal to assess at all.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Treat unauthorised or excessive absence as an ordinary s.387(a) valid-reason question, not a jurisdictional one.
- Recognise when prolonged, unexplained non-contact might instead be genuine abandonment — a different legal concept that can end the matter at the jurisdictional gate under s.386.
- Apply the three things the Commission looks for before accepting that an employee abandoned their job, so you don't wrongly treat a hospitalised or incapacitated employee as having walked away.

## Two different problems, not one

**Absenteeism** is a conduct or performance question. The employee is still employed; the question is whether their pattern of absence — unauthorised days off, a Monday/Friday pattern, failing to notify or provide evidence — gives the employer a valid reason to dismiss under s.387(a). It's assessed exactly like any other valid-reason case (see Module 5).

**Abandonment of employment** is a different question entirely, and it sits at the jurisdictional gate covered in Module 1. The idea is that the *employee's* conduct — viewed objectively — can itself bring the employment to an end, meaning the employer never dismissed anyone within the meaning of <span class="statute">s.386</span>. If the Commission accepts genuine abandonment, the unfair dismissal application fails for want of a dismissal, regardless of how good or bad the merits would otherwise have looked.

**Why the distinction matters for an adviser:** get this wrong on either side and the result is bad. Tell an employer "that's abandonment" too readily, and you can lose a dismissal that was otherwise defensible on the merits, or wrongly write off an employee who was in hospital. Tell an employee "you were dismissed" when the Commission finds they walked away, and the claim fails at the first hurdle.

## Absenteeism as a valid reason (s.387(a))

Persistent, unauthorised absence — and a persistent failure to follow lawful and reasonable directions about notifying and evidencing absences — can be a valid reason for dismissal in its own right.

*Clark v Woolworths Group* is a clear illustration. The employee had accumulated 92 days of absence in the prior year, had been directed on multiple occasions (October 2022, May 2023, August 2024, February 2025) to notify the company of intended absences and provide supporting documentation, and continued not to comply even after a final written warning. The Commission found <Cite id="[2025] FWC 2226" at="[32]" phrase="two valid reasons to dismiss Mr Clark" />: the absenteeism itself meant he was not meeting the inherent requirement of attending for work, and separately, he had <Cite id="[2025] FWC 2226" at="[33]" phrase="repeatedly failed to follow the company's directions to provide notice of absences from work" />.

**Sympathetic circumstances don't automatically excuse non-compliance.** The employee in that case had genuine personal hardship — his own illness and his son's mental health — and the Commission accepted that. But it still found his explanations for repeatedly failing to produce evidence unconvincing, asking why he couldn't see a different doctor, use a telehealth service, or try another pharmacy for a witnessed declaration <Cite id="[2025] FWC 2226" at="[41]" phrase="find Mr Clark's reasons for often not being able to obtain evidence for absences to be unconvincing" />. Genuine hardship explains an absence; it doesn't automatically excuse a failure to follow a reasonable, previously-warned-about process for evidencing it.

**But don't over-reach the allegation.** In *Umerez v Flanders Electric*, the employer relied on several absence days as part of its reason for dismissal. For two of those days — taken close in time to a period of authorised, certified sick leave — the Commission found no valid reason, even without a medical certificate for those specific two days <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1217" at="[91]" phrase="even where he did not provide a medical certificate to cover those days" />. By contrast, a separate instance of arriving 1.5 hours late, where the employee made no claim of illness, injury or emergency, <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1217" at="[95]" phrase="was a valid reason for the dismissal" />. The lesson: not every absence is equally weak just because it lacks paperwork, and not every absence is equally strong just because it's unexplained — look at what else is going on around it (recent certified leave, a declared emergency) before deciding it supports a valid reason.

## Abandonment of employment (s.386)

The core test comes from a Full Bench "Abandonment of Employment" test case, quoted (and confirmed against the quoting decision's own text) in two decisions in this corpus:

<div class="key">
<h3>The test for abandonment</h3>
<p style="margin:0;font-size:14px;color:var(--muted)">From the Full Bench "Abandonment of Employment" test case, *[2018] FWCFB 139*, as quoted in <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2471" at="[16]" phrase="ceases to attend his or her place of employment without proper excuse or explanation" /> and again in <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6333" at="[37]" phrase="renunciation is a species of repudiation which entitles the employer to terminate the employment contract" />. The original Full Bench decision is not in this corpus and has not been independently checked — check the quote against it before relying on it.</p>

<p>"Abandonment of employment" describes a situation where an employee ceases to attend work without proper excuse or explanation, thereby evincing an unwillingness or inability to substantially perform their obligations under the employment contract — a renunciation of the contract. The test is objective: whether the employee's conduct is such as to convey to a reasonable person in the employer's situation a renunciation of the contract as a whole, or of the employee's fundamental obligations under it. Although it is the employer's own act that formally ends the contract, the employment relationship is treated as ended by the <strong>employee's</strong> renunciation.</p>
</div>

A separately-cited authority puts the same idea slightly differently: the test is whether the <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2471" at="[20]" phrase="employee's conduct is such to convey to a reasonable person in the position of the employer" /> and based on facts reasonably known to the employer at the time, that the employee has repudiated their obligations — from *Shamrock Consultancy Pty Ltd v Norma Ah San*, not independently located in this corpus; check it against the original before relying on it.

### What the Commission requires before accepting abandonment

Three things recur across the decisions in this corpus before a prolonged, unexplained absence will be treated as abandonment rather than something else:

1. **Genuine attempts to contact the employee.** <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2471" at="[67]" phrase="the employer must attempt to contact the employee to ascertain the employee's answer to that question" /> — an employer that merely assumes and acts, without checking, is at risk. In *Subedi v Crispy Bakehouse*, the employer had a reasonable basis to suspect abandonment after the employee failed to return from overseas leave as planned — but the Commission held <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3612" at="[15]" phrase="it was incumbent on CBC to seek to confirm this with Mr Subedi before putting its understanding into practice" />, and because it never did, the abandonment objection failed and a later dismissal was found instead.
2. **A reasonable period of silence**, not a single missed day. In *Fathers v Global Line Marking Services*, the employee missed <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6333" at="[20]" phrase="failing to attend five consecutive rostered shifts without advising any staff members of his absence in advance" />, after a manager had tried to reach him. The Commission found <Cite id="[2021] FWC 6333" at="[42]" phrase="I find that the Applicant abandoned his employment" />. Similarly in *Cornell v Action Events Group*, the employee went silent for weeks after his last certified sick leave period expired, ignoring a letter warning that continued non-contact would be treated as abandonment, and the Commission found <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2322" at="[11]" phrase="Mr Cornell's conduct manifested a decision to abandon employment" />.
3. **No plausible alternative explanation for the silence.** This is the protective side of the test, and it's the one that matters most for employees who are genuinely unable to make contact — through hospitalisation, incapacity, or a documented condition the employer already knows about. In *Liu v Itsumo Aus*, the employer argued the employee had abandoned his job during a period of non-contact. The Commission disagreed: the employee was on workers' compensation, complying with his rehabilitation provider's return-to-work plan, and the employer knew it. It found he <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2471" at="[71]" phrase="did not abandon his employment" /> — the absence had a complete, employer-known explanation, so it could not be read as a renunciation of the contract.

### The jurisdictional stakes

If the Commission accepts genuine abandonment, there was no dismissal "on the employer's initiative" within the meaning of <span class="statute">s.386(1)(a)</span> — so the application fails at the gateway covered in Module 1, before anyone gets to s.387 at all. That cuts both ways for advice:

- **Acting for the employer:** don't assume abandonment protects you from an unfair dismissal claim just because the employee went quiet. Unless you can show genuine, documented attempts to contact them, a reasonable period, and no plausible innocent explanation, you risk simply being found to have dismissed them anyway — without any of the s.387 process you'd otherwise have run.
- **Acting for the employee:** if there's a real, demonstrable reason they couldn't make contact, that is often the whole case at the jurisdictional stage — get the medical or other evidence of incapacity in early, because it goes directly to whether there was a dismissal at all, not just to harshness later.

## How absenteeism and abandonment arguments fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employer lost the jurisdictional point</span>
<h3>Acted on suspicion without confirming it</h3>
<p>The employer had a reasonable basis to think the employee had abandoned his job after he didn't return from leave as expected — but never checked with him before treating the job as over. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3612" at="[15]" phrase="it was incumbent on CBC to seek to confirm this with Mr Subedi before putting its understanding into practice" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A reasonable suspicion is not the test. Make, and document, an actual attempt to contact the employee before you act on it.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employer's objection failed</span>
<h3>Mistook incapacity for renunciation</h3>
<p>The employer treated a workers' compensation absence as abandonment. The employee was complying with his rehabilitation plan the whole time, and the employer knew it. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2471" at="[71]" phrase="did not abandon his employment" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A plausible, employer-known explanation for the silence defeats an abandonment argument before it starts. Check what you already know before asserting it.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Allegation pitched too wide</span>
<h3>Absences next to protected leave, without a certificate</h3>
<p>Two absence days taken close to an authorised, certified sick leave period were found not to be a valid reason, even without a certificate covering those exact days — while a separate late arrival with no claimed illness or emergency was. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 1217" at="[91]" phrase="even where he did not provide a medical certificate to cover those days" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Don't lump every gap in paperwork together. Look at what surrounds each absence before deciding it supports your case.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Applicant failed</span>
<h3>Weeks of silence, no explanation offered</h3>
<p>After his last certified sick leave period ended, the employee didn't respond to the company for weeks, including ignoring a letter warning that silence would be treated as abandonment. <Cite id="[2021] FWC 2322" at="[11]" phrase="Mr Cornell's conduct manifested a decision to abandon employment" /></p>
<p class="lesson">If there's a genuine reason for the silence, raise it early and document it. Silence with no explanation is exactly what the abandonment test is built to catch.</p>
</div>
</div>

  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Not yet abandonment, and the manager should stop.</strong> There's a four-day unexplained absence and genuine attempts to contact Priya by phone, text and email — that much is in the manager's favour. But the moment the sister's call came in, the clinic had a plausible, serious alternative explanation for the silence: hospitalisation. Treating this as abandonment now, after being told why Priya couldn't respond, would very likely fail in the same way the workers' compensation case above did — the explanation defeats the inference of renunciation.</li>
      <li><strong>Still to do:</strong> contact the sister (or ask Priya, once able) for confirmation and likely timeframe; hold off on any termination letter; document the hospitalisation information and when it was received; and only revisit the question if Priya, once well, still doesn't engage for a further unexplained period with no update.</li>
      <li><strong>If it had instead been "she decided not to come back":</strong> that changes the analysis substantially. Four unanswered days, multiple genuine contact attempts across three channels, and a clear prior warning that silence would be treated as abandonment start to look like the missed-shifts and Christmas-silence cases above — conduct that, viewed objectively, conveys renunciation. The three prior short unauthorised absences would be irrelevant to that question (they're a separate, ordinary s.387(a) issue, not evidence of abandonment) but could become relevant later if abandonment isn't made out and the matter is instead argued as a conduct dismissal.</li>
    </ol>
