<p class="lead">The Fair Work Act protects union membership, non-membership and lawful union activity in the same way it protects workplace rights: no adverse action because of them, with the employer bearing the onus on its reasons. This module is about managing employees who are members, delegates or health and safety representatives, and about what counts as industrial activity.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Say what s.346 protects, including the right not to be a union member.
- Recognise industrial activity when it happens in a workplace.
- Take performance or conduct action against a delegate or representative without acting because of the role.
- Identify the timing and evidence problems that make these cases hard to defend.
- Know where the delegates' rights provisions sit and what to check.

## What Division 4 protects

Section 346 was set out by the Full Court in the <em>Bay Street Appeal</em>. A person must not take adverse action against another person because the other person <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 192" at="[52]" phrase="is or is not, or was or was not, an officer or member of an industrial association" />, or because the person engages or does not engage in industrial activity.

Three features matter in practice.

**It protects both choices.** Being a member and not being a member are protected equally. So are taking part in union activity and declining to.

**It applies to everyone.** The prohibition is on "a person", not only an employer. In the <em>College Crescent Case</em>, a union delegate on a building site refused to let two workers start because they were not financial members. <Cite id="[2020] FCA 757" at="[9]" phrase="He did so because neither Bernaldo nor Pragnell were members of the Union" />. An employer, head contractor or labour hire client that goes along with a demand of that kind is taking the same action for the same reason.

**"Industrial activity" is defined by a list.** Allsop CJ explained that <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 192" at="[19]" phrase="Section 347 is definitional" />: it supplies the subject matter for ss.346, 348 and 349. The activities in the list all concern <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 192" at="[25]" phrase="protection of participation (or not) in activities of, in, with, on behalf of or in relation to industrial associations" />.

### The list

As quoted in the <em>Bay Street Appeal</em>, a person engages in industrial activity if they do, or do not, do any of these:

- become involved in establishing an industrial association;
- <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 192" at="[52]" phrase="organise or promote a lawful activity for, or on behalf of, an industrial association" />;
- <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 192" at="[52]" phrase="encourage, or participate in, a lawful activity organised or promoted by an industrial association" />;
- comply with a lawful request or requirement of an industrial association;
- <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 192" at="[52]" phrase="represent or advance the views, claims or interests of an industrial association" />;
- pay a fee to an industrial association;
- <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 192" at="[52]" phrase="seek to be represented by an industrial association" />.

In an ordinary workplace this covers handing out union material, recruiting members, collecting signatures on a bargaining petition, speaking for members in a meeting, attending a lawful union meeting, and asking for a union representative. It equally covers refusing to do any of them.

The rest of the list deals with unlawful activity and industrial action. There the protection runs one way: a person is protected for not organising or joining unlawful activity, not complying with an unlawful union request, and not taking part in industrial action. Protected industrial action has its own protection as a workplace right (Module 25).

One limit from the cases: the "request or requirement" item is about requests a union makes of others. In <em>CFMMEU v Quirk</em>, Katzmann J held that <Cite id="[2023] FCAFC 163" at="[283]" phrase="s 347 does not apply to a request or requirement of an industrial association made to, or imposed on, its own employees" />.

## Delegates: status, conduct and manner

A workplace delegate may be protected as an officer or member of an industrial association, and representation and other conduct may constitute industrial activity, depending on the statutory definitions and the facts. The role also engages the separate workplace-delegate rights in ss.350A and 350C. Do not assume that every delegate is necessarily an officer of the union or that every act performed in the role is protected. That does not put a delegate beyond management; it means the employer must identify the particular status, conduct and statutory protection before acting.

<em>Salama v Sydney Trains</em> shows an employer doing that. A transport officer who was a union delegate and health and safety representative was suspended, asked to show cause and dismissed. He had advocated for members, raised a dispute about union rights and brought stop-bullying proceedings. The show-cause letter referred to some of those events.

On the claim based on status alone, he accepted that his case went no further than <Cite id="[2021] FCA 251" at="[251]" phrase="his general status as a union delegate and HSR" />. On the claim based on what he had done, Burley J applied the High Court's approach as stated in earlier cases. The protection is not against action taken because of conduct that happens to be industrial activity. <Cite id="[2021] FCA 251" at="[362]" phrase="It is protection against adverse action being taken by reason of that act or omission having the character of a protected industrial activity" />.

The judge accepted that some of the employee's conduct was protected. He still found for the employer: <Cite id="[2021] FCA 251" at="[363]" phrase="no part of the decision to take the adverse action was for the reason that any of Mr Salama's conduct had the character of protected workplace rights or industrial activity" />. The decision-makers' reason was the way he communicated with managers and colleagues and his refusal to take part in counselling about it, over many months.

The same distinction between conduct and reason appears in Modules 29 and 30. In this setting it needs particular care:

- **The concern must be about how, not what.** An employer can act on abuse, dishonesty or refusal to follow a lawful direction. It cannot act on the fact that the delegate raised a dispute, represented a member or took an unwelcome position.
- **Advocacy is often forceful.** A delegate arguing a member's case is not being insubordinate. Before treating a delegate's manner as misconduct, ask whether the same words from a manager arguing the other side would be treated that way.
- **Keep protected events out of the allegations unless they are needed.** In <em>Salama</em>, the decision-maker had to explain in cross-examination why the stop-bullying proceedings were mentioned in the show-cause letter. The judge accepted that they were raised <Cite id="[2021] FCA 251" at="[332]" phrase="purely to illustrate what he considered to be another example" /> of a pattern. Another judge might not have.
- **Counsel first, and record it.** The employer in <em>Salama</em> could show repeated informal and formal counselling before any disciplinary step.

## Knowledge and timing

An employer cannot act because of a delegate's role if the decision-maker does not know of it. But a claim of ignorance will be tested, and timing can make it hard to believe.

In <em>RTBU v Metro Trains Sydney</em>, an employee started work on 1 March 2019. <Cite id="[2019] FCA 546" at="[8]" phrase="On or around 5 March 2019 he became the union delegate" />, the only one at the workplace. In late March he circulated a petition the union needed to start bargaining. He was dismissed on 27 March, during probation. The employer's reason was that he had been dishonest in a pre-employment health form.

The union applied to the court for interim reinstatement. Robertson J found a serious question to be tried. Of the decision-maker's evidence he said: <Cite id="[2019] FCA 546" at="[47]" phrase="I regard his reasons, including his motives, as open to challenge" />. On whether the decision-maker knew the employee was a delegate, <Cite id="[2019] FCA 546" at="[55]" phrase="There is an apparent inconsistency between Mr Dickinson's assertion" /> and other evidence.

The judge would have ordered reinstatement but for one thing. The employer undertook to keep paying the employee until an early trial. One of the matters weighing towards reinstatement was <Cite id="[2019] FCA 546" at="[61]" phrase="the potential damage to the position of the first applicant union within the workplace" />. On the undertaking, interim relief was refused <Cite id="[2019] FCA 546" at="[64]" phrase="I refuse the interlocutory relief sought by the applicants" />. The final result was not found.

Lessons for an employer's adviser:

- **Probation is not a shelter.** The general protections apply from the first day, and a dismissal within weeks of an employee becoming a delegate will be examined closely.
- **Check what the decision-maker actually knew** before relying on lack of knowledge. Emails, comments in meetings and what supervisors reported will all come out.
- **A dismissal of a delegate during an organising or bargaining campaign invites an injunction application**, with no Commission stage first (Module 33). Be ready to produce the decision-maker's evidence within days.
- **Remarks about the union are evidence.** The employee in that case gave evidence that a general manager had said he was disappointed that union membership was growing.

## Health and safety representatives

A health and safety representative holds a role under work health and safety law. That is a workplace right under s.340, not industrial activity under s.346, but the management problem is the same and the two roles are often held by one person.

In <em>CFMMEU v Melbourne Precast Concrete Nominees</em>, an employee was appointed as a representative, made complaints to the safety regulator, and was then selected for redundancy. The director said the selection was based on a skills evaluation sheet on which the employee scored second lowest. O'Callaghan J found the sheet was <Cite id="[2020] FCA 931" at="[109]" phrase="a contrivance by Mr Pichler to conceal the real reason for Mr Hes's dismissal" />. The director also claimed not to have known of the appointment or the complaints, and was not believed: <Cite id="[2020] FCA 931" at="[111]" phrase="I also reject Mr Pichler's assertion that he was not aware of Mr Hes's appointment as HSR" />.

The contraventions were made out <Cite id="[2020] FCA 931" at="[116]" phrase="to the extent they relate to Mr Hes's role as HSR or his WorkSafe complaints" />, and the director was personally liable: <Cite id="[2020] FCA 931" at="[138]" phrase="I find that Mr Pichler was involved in MPC's contraventions" />.

A selection matrix protects an employer only if it is genuine: prepared by the person said to have prepared it, at the time it is said to have been prepared, and applied to everyone. Module 28 covers restructures that follow a complaint. The same care is needed when a representative or delegate is in the selection pool.

## Coercion, misrepresentation and inducement

Division 4 has three more prohibitions about industrial activity. They are stated here from the statute and need checking against the current text.

- **Coercion (s.348).** As quoted in the <em>Bay Street Appeal</em>: <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 192" at="[52]" phrase="A person must not organise or take, or threaten to organise or take, any action against another person with intent to coerce the other person, or a third person, to engage in industrial activity" />.
- **Misrepresentation (s.349).** A person must not knowingly or recklessly make a false or misleading representation about another person's obligation to engage in industrial activity, or to disclose whether they are a member.
- **Inducement (s.350).** An employer must not induce an employee to take, or propose to take, membership action, which means becoming, not becoming, remaining or ceasing to be a member.

For an employer the points to watch are statements in recruitment and induction ("you have to join" or "we don't employ union members"), incentives tied to membership status, and pressure on employees to resign from a union during a dispute. Module 32 covers coercion and misrepresentation in more detail.

## Delegates' rights

The Act now also gives workplace delegates positive rights, separate from the protection against adverse action. Those rights apply differently to employees, regulated workers and regulated businesses. In summary, and subject to checking the current text:

- A workplace delegate is entitled to represent the industrial interests of members and eligible employees, to reasonable communication with them, and to reasonable access to the workplace and its facilities. The provisions also regulate the position of regulated workers and regulated businesses. Employers other than small businesses must give reasonable access to paid time for delegate training (s.350C), subject to the statutory conditions.
- An employer must not unreasonably fail or refuse to deal with a delegate, knowingly or recklessly make a false or misleading representation to a delegate, or unreasonably hinder, obstruct or prevent the delegate's exercise of their rights (s.350A).
- Modern awards and enterprise agreements must contain a delegates' rights term. Compliance with the applicable term may engage the Act's deeming rule, but it is not a substitute for checking whether the instrument, the statutory conditions and the regulated-worker provisions apply.

No Federal Court decision applying these provisions was found in the corpus. The live Commission database, observed on 13 and 14 November 2026, contains agreement-approval and award decisions that refer to them, but none was read closely enough to cite. Before advising on a delegate's request for time, access or facilities, read the delegates' rights term in the award or agreement that applies.

Refusing a delegate's reasonable request and then disciplining the delegate for pressing it would raise both sets of provisions at once.

## How these cases are won and lost

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>A delegate dismissed for how he dealt with managers</h3>
<p>Some of his conduct was protected. The decision-makers proved they acted on his communication and his refusal to engage with counselling. <Cite id="[2021] FCA 251" at="[363]" phrase="no part of the decision to take the adverse action was for the reason that any of Mr Salama's conduct had the character of protected workplace rights or industrial activity" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Act on manner and refusal, counsel first, and keep the record.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Serious question found</span>
<h3>New delegate dismissed in probation during a petition</h3>
<p>The employer relied on dishonesty in a health form and on not knowing he was a delegate. Both were open to challenge. <Cite id="[2019] FCA 546" at="[47]" phrase="I regard his reasons, including his motives, as open to challenge" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Probation gives no protection here. Test the decision-maker's knowledge and reasons before acting.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>Safety representative selected by a skills matrix</h3>
<p>The matrix was found to have been created to hide the real reason, and the director was personally liable. <Cite id="[2020] FCA 931" at="[109]" phrase="a contrivance by Mr Pichler to conceal the real reason for Mr Hes's dismissal" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Selection documents must be genuine and made when they are said to have been made.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Regulator won</span>
<h3>No membership, no start</h3>
<p>Two workers were kept off a site because they were not financial union members. <Cite id="[2020] FCA 757" at="[9]" phrase="He did so because neither Bernaldo nor Pragnell were members of the Union" /></p>

## Practice scenario: a petition at Kingsford Linen Services

<Scenario questions={["Can Kingsford end Tomas's probation?", "What about Carla's comment and the roster change?", "How should the timesheet issue be handled?", "What should the adviser expect if Tomas is dismissed?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Kingsford is a fictional commercial laundry with 85 employees and no enterprise agreement. Tomas started as a machine operator nine weeks ago on six months' probation.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Week 5</span><b>Tomas joins the union and is elected delegate; the union emails the general manager to say so</b>
      <span>Week 7</span><b>Tomas starts collecting signatures at lunch for a petition to begin bargaining</b>
      <span>Week 7</span><b>The production manager, Carla, tells a supervisor in the lunch room: "We never had this trouble before he turned up"</b>
      <span>Week 8</span><b>Carla moves Tomas from day shift to the 4 am shift "for operational reasons"; no one else is moved</b>
      <span>Week 9</span><b>Payroll reports that Tomas's timesheet for one day shows a 3.30 pm finish when the swipe record shows 3.05 pm</b>
      <span>Now</span><b>Carla asks HR to end his probation for "dishonesty", and says she did not know he was a delegate</b>
    </div>
    <p>Other operators' timesheets have not been checked against swipe records. Kingsford's usual response to a first timesheet discrepancy is a conversation with the supervisor.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Not on this material.</strong> Probation does not remove the general protections. Tomas is a delegate, so his status is protected, and collecting petition signatures is industrial activity. The dismissal would come two weeks after the petition started, on the proposal of a manager who has made a hostile comment. This is close to <em>RTBU v Metro Trains Sydney</em>. Kingsford would have to prove that neither his role nor the petition was any part of Carla's reason.</li>
      <li><strong>Both are problems already.</strong> The comment is evidence of her attitude to the activity. The shift change is adverse action in its own right (Module 26): it was applied to him alone, a week after the petition began, with no recorded operational reason. Review it now and reverse it unless a documented reason exists that pre-dates the petition. Her statement that she did not know he was a delegate is contradicted by the union's email to the general manager unless that email never reached her, and it will be tested.</li>
      <li><strong>As it would be for anyone else, and by someone else.</strong> A 25-minute discrepancy on one day may be an error or may be dishonest. The usual response is a conversation. Departing from the usual response for the delegate, without checking anyone else's timesheets, suggests another reason. Have a manager other than Carla ask Tomas about it, check a sample of other operators, and apply the ordinary outcome. If the inquiry shows deliberate and repeated falsification, a different decision-maker can consider what follows.</li>
      <li><strong>An urgent court application.</strong> The union can go straight to court seeking interim reinstatement, without a Commission conference. The court will look at the strength of the case and at the effect on the union's campaign if its only delegate is removed. Kingsford would need Carla's evidence within days, and she would be cross-examined on the comment, the shift change and what she knew.</li>
    </ol>
    <p class="spectrum-label"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">The shift change is reversed, the timesheet is dealt with by a supervisor in the usual way, Carla is briefed, and the petition runs its course.</div>
      <div class="lo">Tomas is dismissed for dishonesty: an injunction application within the week, two adverse actions tied to the petition by timing and by Carla's own words, and personal exposure for her.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
