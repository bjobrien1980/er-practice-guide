<p class="lead">When an employee can't currently do the job because of injury or illness, "valid reason" stops being about proving what happened and starts being about a structured, three-part medical question — and almost every mistake in these cases comes from skipping one of the three parts.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Apply the three-part inherent-requirements test, and explain why the order of the three questions matters.
- Weigh competing medical evidence — treating practitioner against independent medical examiner — the way the Commission actually weighs it.
- Judge whether an employer has waited long enough before dismissal for incapacity becomes defensible, and spot when "we should have been given more time" is not, on its own, a winning argument.

## Capacity, not conduct

Capacity is a distinct category of valid reason under s.387(a) from conduct. It covers an employee's ability to do the work they're employed to do, including an inability to perform the inherent requirements of the job <Cite id="[2021] FWC 3122" at="[74]" phrase="because of some injury, illness or other disability" /> — because of an injury, illness or other disability. Nothing here turns on fault. The question is whether the person can do the job, not whether they're to blame for not being able to.

## The three-part test

The leading formulation, applied across this corpus, comes from a Full Bench of the Commission and asks three separate, ordered questions:

<div class="key">
<h3>The three questions</h3>
<p style="margin:0;font-size:14px;color:var(--muted)">From a Full Bench in <em>Jetstar Airways Pty Ltd v Neeteson-Lemkes</em> [2013] FWCFB 9075, as quoted in <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3684" at="[11]" phrase="capable of performing the inherent requirements of her role as at the date of dismissal" /> at [11]. <em>Jetstar</em> is not in this corpus as a direct record — check the quoted principle against the original before relying on it.</p>

<ol class="principles">
<li>Was the employee capable of performing the inherent requirements of their role as at the date of dismissal?</li>
<li>Would the employee be able to perform the inherent requirements of their role at some time in the future?</li>
<li>Was there some reasonable adjustment which could be made to the role to accommodate any current or future incapacity?</li>
</ol>
</div>

All three questions are assessed by reference to the facts as they existed **at the time of the dismissal** — not with hindsight, and not by reference to events afterwards. In one matter, a treating doctor's subsequent certificates issued after the dismissal couldn't be taken into account in assessing the employee's future capacity, because that question has to be answered on what was known when the decision was made <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3684" at="[67]" phrase="significant degree of uncertainty as to whether Ms Montgomery would be able to perform the inherent requirements of her role" />. On the facts in that matter, the Commission found no reasonable adjustment could accommodate the employee's incapacity either <Cite id="[2020] FWC 3684" at="[73]" phrase="there were no reasonable adjustments which could have been made to Ms Montgomery" />.

**The first question is about the substantive role, not a modified one.** The inherent requirements of a role are ascertained objectively, by what the job actually requires — not a temporary light-duties arrangement, and not the employee's own view of which tasks matter. The focus is on the job's essential elements, not its peripheral tasks <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1871" at="[39]" phrase="essential elements of the job, not its remote or peripheral tasks" />. In one matter, a forklift operator argued he should be allowed to keep driving and nothing else; the Commission rejected this because driving the forklift was only one part of the role, and manual tasks like shovelling spillage and climbing ladders remained inherent to it even though they happened less often than driving.

**Safety can itself be an inherent requirement.** Performing duties without unreasonable risk to the safety of fellow employees is, as a matter of law, an inherent requirement of employment <Cite id="[2022] FWC 786" at="[103]" phrase="unreasonable risk to the safety of fellow employees is, as a matter of law, an inherent requirement of employment" />. This matters in physically demanding or safety-critical roles: an employer isn't limited to asking "can they physically do the movements" — it can properly ask "can they do them safely."

## Weighing the medical evidence

Most of these cases turn on whose medical opinion the Commission accepts, and the pattern across the corpus is fact-specific rather than a fixed hierarchy between a treating practitioner and an independent medical examiner (IME).

**An unchallenged, thorough IME is hard to beat.** In one matter, an employer obtained a Fitness For Duty report from an occupational physician who examined the employee, consulted his treating doctors directly, and reviewed his full medical history. The employee didn't produce any medical evidence of his own to challenge it, despite being under the care of six different practitioners <Cite id="[2022] FWC 1871" at="[33]" phrase="the Applicant does not challenge (by way of other medical evidence or medical opinion) the findings" />. An uncontradicted, well-founded expert report will usually decide the question.

**But a long-standing treating practitioner's evidence can outweigh episodic specialist reports.** In another matter, the Commission gave substantial weight to a GP's consistent, two-year view that the employee was permanently restricted from a key part of his role, over additional reports from a psychologist and psychiatrist who had seen him only intermittently <Cite id="[2022] FWC 786" at="[221]" phrase="the consistent view of his treating GP who saw the Applicant the most frequently" />. Frequency and continuity of contact carry real weight, not just seniority of qualification.

**Two reports can look like they conflict without actually conflicting.** An employee received one report saying he was fit for duty and another saying he wasn't, and understandably felt whipsawed. The Commission explained the reports weren't really in conflict: they were commissioned by different entities, for different purposes — one examined physical fitness only, for a workers' compensation insurer; the other considered both physical and psychological fitness, at the employer's specific request <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1125" at="[16]" phrase="these reports were commissioned by different entities, for different purposes" />. Before treating two medical opinions as genuinely conflicting, check what each doctor was actually asked to assess.

**The Commission makes its own finding on the medical evidence — it doesn't just defer to the employer's view of it.** In a capacity-related dismissal, the Commission is required to make its own findings as to whether the employee suffered the alleged incapacity at the time of dismissal, based on the medical and other evidence actually before it <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1125" at="[15]" phrase="the Commission must make findings as to whether at the time of the dismissal the applicant suffered from the alleged incapacity" />, drawing on the reasoning of a Full Bench in *CSL Limited T/A CSL Behring v Chris Papaioannou* [2018] FWCFB 1005 — not in this corpus as a direct record, but the quote is confirmed against the quoting decision. A good medical report doesn't win the case by itself; it wins the case because the Commission, independently, finds it persuasive.

## WHS obligations and fitness-for-duty directions

An employer with a genuine concern about an employee's capacity isn't limited to waiting for the employee to volunteer medical information. A direction to attend an IME, or to provide medical information, can be a lawful and reasonable direction — the Full Federal Court has held an employer has a right to request an employee attend an IME where there is a genuine indication of a need for it and the request is reasonable <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2906" at="[93]" phrase="a right to request an employee to attend an IME if there is genuine indication of a need for it and it is reasonable for your employer to make such a request" />. That reasoning (from *Blackadder v Ramsey Butchering Services Pty Ltd*, not in this corpus as a direct record) sits comfortably alongside an employer's own WHS duty to satisfy itself an employee can do a role safely before letting them resume it.

Failing to comply with such a direction, without a satisfactory explanation, can itself become part of a valid reason for dismissal. In one matter, an employee had been absent for over two years with an unchanged prognosis <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2906" at="[100]" phrase="The Applicant had been absent from work for over two years" />, was directed on three separate occasions to attend an IME or a show cause meeting, and didn't comply with any of them. The Commission found the directions lawful and reasonable and was not satisfied the dismissal was unfair <Cite id="[2023] FWC 2906" at="[136]" phrase="I am not satisfied that the Applicant was unfairly dismissed" />.

**For advisers:** a fitness-for-duty direction should (a) identify a genuine, articulable concern about capacity or safety, (b) be proportionate — telehealth, reasonable notice, and a genuine chance to respond, not an ambush, and (c) be followed through consistently if ignored, with each step documented.

## How long does an employer have to wait?

There's no fixed rule — the Commission looks at what's reasonable on the facts, not a set number of weeks or months. A few consistent threads run through the corpus:

- **"Within a reasonable time frame" is itself a recognised basis for dismissal.** One employer's termination letter put the question directly: was there a reasonable prospect of the employee performing the inherent requirements of his role within a reasonable time frame <Cite id="[2020] FWC 5557" at="[56]" phrase="his inability to perform the inherent requirements of his role within a reasonable time frame" />? On facts showing over a year's absence with no medical evidence of an end point, the Commission found a valid reason for dismissal related to capacity <Cite id="[2020] FWC 5557" at="[68]" phrase="I am satisfied there was a valid reason for the termination of Mr Palibrk's employment relating to his capacity" />.
- **An uncertain end date weighs more heavily than a long elapsed time.** What tends to matter most isn't simply how many weeks or months have passed, but whether the medical evidence gives any indication of *when* capacity might return. Absence with a clear, approaching recovery date is a different case from absence with no prognosis at all.
- **"A few more weeks" needs its own evidence, not just an assertion.** An employee argued that if the employer had waited a little longer, his doctor might have revised her opinion. The Commission rejected this: he'd already been absent for nearly three months on unpaid leave, the clinical opinion was clear, and the company had already afforded him a reasonable time to recover <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1125" at="[23]" phrase="It had already afforded him a reasonable time to recover" />; the dismissal was found not unfair <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1125" at="[20]" phrase="the company's decision not to give Mr Arora more time was not unfair" /> and the Commission ultimately found the company had a good and substantiated reason to dismiss <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1125" at="[27]" phrase="The company had a good and substantiated reason to dismiss Mr Arora" />.
- **Once incapacity is current and permanent on the medical evidence, alternatives to dismissal don't have to be exhausted first.** An employee argued his employer should instead have extended his leave without pay, or tried redeployment. The Commission found both arguments failed: extending unpaid leave would have been of no utility given the medical evidence of permanent incapacity <Cite id="[2026] FWC 554" at="[57]" phrase="Extending Mr Rennick's leave without pay would have been of no utility" />, and it wasn't persuaded redeployment was appropriate in any event <Cite id="[2026] FWC 554" at="[59]" phrase="I am not persuaded that re-deployment was appropriate in any event" />. The underlying incapacity finding carried the case <Cite id="[2026] FWC 554" at="[35]" phrase="I find that Mr Rennick's incapacity to perform the inherent requirements of his job is a valid reason for his dismissal" />, and the application was ultimately dismissed <Cite id="[2026] FWC 554" at="[61]" phrase="Mr Rennick's application for an unfair dismissal remedy is dismissed" />.
- **Patience earns the employer credit on the "fair go" side of the ledger, even where it doesn't have to be shown.** Keeping a role open through a long absence, even at real operational cost, tends to count in the employer's favour when the Commission stands back and assesses the whole picture <Cite id="[2024] FWC 880" at="[54]" phrase="Oz Design could not reasonably have been expected to continue to watch the performance of its Ballina store decline" />.

## Disability discrimination risk — a brief flag

A capacity dismissal connected to injury or illness can also engage disability discrimination risk under s.351 of the general protections regime, which is covered in depth in [Module 2](/modules/general-protections). The short version for this module: running the three-part test properly, on genuine medical evidence, and genuinely considering reasonable adjustments, is also the best insurance against a parallel discrimination claim — the two risks are reduced by the same careful process, not by separate ones.

## How these dismissals fail

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Employer won, narrowly</span>
<h3>No notice before the decision was made</h3>
<p>An employer had a strong medical case but didn't tell the employee, before deciding, that his inability to perform the inherent requirements of his role was the reason under consideration. The lack of prior notice and opportunity to respond added real weight to the argument that the dismissal was unfair. <Cite id="[2024] FWC 880" at="[68]" phrase="These procedural deficiencies add weight to Mr Thomas's argument" /></p>
<p class="lesson">A medically airtight capacity case can still be put at real risk by skipping s.387(b) and (c). Tell the employee what you're considering, and why, before you decide — not after.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Reasoning undermined</span>
<h3>A valid reason isn't the whole case</h3>
<p>An employer's capacity-based dismissal was confirmed as a valid reason on the medical evidence, but the Commission's final view of the overall fairness of the dismissal still turned on separate issues — in this matter, around how the employee's notice entitlement had been handled. <Cite id="[2020] FWC 5557" at="[68]" phrase="I am satisfied there was a valid reason for the termination of Mr Palibrk's employment relating to his capacity" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Getting the medical capacity question right is necessary, but it doesn't make every other part of the dismissal process irrelevant. Check the whole of s.387, not just (a).</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee's argument tested and rejected</span>
<h3>"You should have tried X first" needs its own proof</h3>
<p>An employee argued his employer should have pursued rehabilitation, extended leave, or redeployment before dismissing him. Each argument was tested on its own facts and rejected — not because such arguments can never succeed, but because none of them was supported by evidence capable of outweighing the medical evidence of current, permanent incapacity. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 554" at="[54]" phrase="It is not relevant to whether there was a valid reason for his termination" /></p>
<p class="lesson">For advisers on either side: "there was another option" is not self-proving. It has to be shown the option was genuinely available and would have made a practical difference.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">A risk worth naming</span>
<h3>Treating two reports as conflicting when they answer different questions</h3>
<p>An employee had one medical report saying he was fit for duty and another saying he wasn't. Properly understood, the reports weren't actually in conflict — they were commissioned by different entities, for different purposes, and examined different things. <Cite id="[2023] FWC 1125" at="[16]" phrase="these reports were commissioned by different entities, for different purposes" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Before treating two medical opinions as a genuine conflict (and before telling an employee you're relying on one over the other), check what each doctor was actually asked to assess. A real conflict needs the same question, with different answers.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the Harlow Home & Garden storeroom

<Scenario questions={["Where are we against the three-part test right now?", "What's missing before a decision can be made?", "Has the business waited long enough?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Harlow Home & Garden is a fictional mid-size homewares and garden retail chain. Marcus has worked for four years as a storeroom and stock replenishment assistant at a suburban store, a role that involves regular lifting of bagged mulch and potting mix (up to 20kg), restocking high shelving, and operating a pallet jack. Fourteen weeks ago he injured his lower back lifting a pallet.</p>
    <div class="telemetry">
      <span>Week 1</span><b>GP certificate: no capacity for work, 2 weeks</b>
      <span>Week 6</span><b>GP certificate: capacity for suitable duties only, no lifting over 5kg, reviewed fortnightly</b>
      <span>Week 14</span><b>Store manager asks HR to "move things along" on Marcus's return to work</b>
    </div>
    <p>Marcus has provided a new certificate every two weeks, each repeating the same 5kg restriction with "review in two weeks." He hasn't been offered an independent medical examination. The store has tried him on a register-only roster for six weeks, but register shifts are limited and another part-timer already covers most of them. Marcus says his physio has told him informally that he's "getting there" but this hasn't been put in writing. HR wants to issue a show cause letter this week citing an inability to perform the inherent requirements of his substantive role.</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>First question (current capacity):</strong> on the certificates alone, no — Marcus cannot currently perform the inherent requirements of the storeroom role (lifting, shelving, pallet jack use). <strong>Second question (foreseeable future):</strong> genuinely unclear. Six fortnightly certificates with an unchanged restriction could mean a plateau, or could simply mean nobody has asked the GP for a prognosis. That gap needs to be closed before this question can be answered either way. <strong>Third question (reasonable adjustment):</strong> register-only work has been tried, but whether it's a genuinely available, ongoing adjustment (not just a temporary stopgap) hasn't been tested — six weeks of makeshift rostering around another part-timer is not the same as a considered, documented adjustment decision.</li>
      <li><strong>Missing:</strong> a specific question to the treating GP (or an IME, if there's a reasonable basis for one) asking directly whether the restriction is likely to change, and by when; a documented assessment of whether register-only or another modified role is a genuinely available, ongoing position, not an ad hoc arrangement; and the physio's "getting there" view in writing, if it's going to be relied on by either side.</li>
      <li><strong>Has the business waited long enough?</strong> Fourteen weeks is not, by itself, decisive either way — the issue is that nobody has yet asked for a prognosis, so there's no way to tell whether this is "almost there" or "indefinite." Issuing a show cause letter this week, on this file, answers the first question but skips the second and third. Get a dated, specific answer on prognosis and on the viability of the modified role first; only then is a show cause letter — or a decision not to issue one — actually defensible.</li>
    </ol>
    <p style="margin-bottom:6px"><strong>Where it could land, depending on what the further inquiries show:</strong></p>
    <div class="spectrum">
      <div class="hi">Clear medical evidence of no foreseeable return and no viable ongoing adjustment: a capacity dismissal is likely defensible.</div>
      <div class="lo">A specific, time-bound prognosis showing full or near-full recovery within a short period, or a genuinely viable permanent modified role: dismissal now would be premature.</div>
    </div>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
