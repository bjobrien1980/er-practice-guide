<p class="lead">Dismissal is the obvious adverse action, but it's one of many. An investigation, a suspension, a transfer, a roster change, a refusal to hire and a threat can each be adverse action. This module is about what s.342 covers, and about the steps in an ordinary disciplinary process that an adviser should assume are covered.</p>

Some older authorities, including the High Court's decision in <em>Patrick Stevedores</em>, are described only as later judges have summarised them. <a href="/about#checking">How this guide is checked</a>.

## What you'll be able to do

- Identify which item of the s.342(1) table applies, and which of the four kinds of employer action is alleged.
- Decide whether a step short of dismissal — an investigation, a suspension, a transfer, a direction — is likely to be adverse action.
- Tell a threat from a warning or a show cause letter.
- Explain why "the agreement allows it" doesn't take an action outside s.342.

## The table in s.342(1)

Section 342(1) is a table. Each item pairs a person who acts with a person acted against. Item 1, employer against employee, is the one advisers use most. It has four limbs: the employer "(a) dismisses the employee, (b) injures the employee in his or her employment, (c) alters the position of the employee to the employee's prejudice, or (d) discriminates between the employee and other employees of the employer" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[89]" phrase="discriminates between the employee and other employees of the employer" />.

Other items cover a prospective employer and a job applicant, a principal and an independent contractor, an employee acting against an employer, and an industrial association acting against a person. They're set out under "The rest of the table" below. Read the table before assuming an item does or doesn't apply.

Two general points come first.

**The employee has to prove it.** The reverse onus in s.361 is about the reason for the action. It doesn't cover whether the action was adverse. As Kennett J put it in *Avard*, the nature of the action "is not part of the issue as to which, under s 361, the respondents bear the onus of proof" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[110]" phrase="is not part of the issue as to which, under s 361, the respondents bear the onus of proof" />.

**Effects count; motive doesn't.** Whether an action is adverse depends on what it did to the employee. The employer in *Avard* said its actions were protective, taken out of concern for the employee. That was the wrong stage for the argument. The reasons for an action are relevant to why it was taken, but whether it was adverse "must turn on the objective effects of the action" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[110]" phrase="must turn on the objective effects of the action" />.

## The four limbs of item 1

### Dismisses

This limb is rarely disputed. In the Commission, where general protections applications usually follow a dismissal, it's typically conceded <Cite id="[2024] FWC 2475" at="[40]" phrase="It was not disputed that termination of employment is adverse action" />. Whether there was a dismissal at all, as with a resignation or the end of a fixed term, is a separate question covered elsewhere in this guide.

### Injures the employee in their employment

"Injures" is a wide word, but the injury must be to the person as an employee. "The harm that constitutes the injury must be harm to rights or interests that the employee has or enjoys as an employee" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[116]" phrase="The harm that constitutes the injury must be harm to rights or interests that the employee has or enjoys as an employee" />. Loss of pay and reduction in rank are the plain examples.

Distress alone doesn't qualify. In *Avard*, a senior doctor described feeling anxious, powerless and overwhelmed after being directed not to attend work. The judge held that s.340 "is concerned with abuse of the power of an employer, not with the infliction of harm generally" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[117]" phrase="is concerned with abuse of the power of an employer, not with the infliction of harm generally" />. Psychological harm may matter to compensation once a contravention is proved, but it "is not sufficient in itself to render an action" adverse <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[117]" phrase="is not sufficient in itself to render an action" />.

Damage to professional reputation is different. The same judge would have accepted that an email to staff which lowered the doctor's "reputation among colleagues was an injury to her" in her employment <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[201]" phrase="reputation among colleagues was an injury to her" />.

### Alters the employee's position to their prejudice

This is the broadest limb and the one most process steps fall under. It is "a broad additional category of adverse action which covers not only legal injury but" any deterioration in the advantages the employee enjoyed before <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[96]" phrase="a broad additional category of adverse action which covers not only legal injury but" />. No legal right needs to be lost.

There are two limits. The prejudice must be "real and substantial rather than merely possible and hypothetical" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[97]" phrase="real and substantial rather than merely possible and hypothetical" />. And the employee "must be in a worse situation after the relevant conduct than before it" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[97]" phrase="must be in a worse situation after the relevant conduct than before it" />, with the employer's conduct as the cause.

Pay isn't the test. In *Avard*, a suspension decision led to the doctor working for nearly a year in a lower-status role that didn't use her specialist skills. "It altered her position to her detriment and was injurious to her in her employment" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[186]" phrase="It altered her position to her detriment and was injurious to her in her employment" />.

### Discriminates between the employee and other employees

Judges have read this limb two ways: as any different treatment, or as less favourable treatment. In *Morton v CSIRO (No 2)*, Rangiah J noted the tension and preferred the narrower reading: the limb "requires less favourable treatment of an employee (or a group of employees) in comparison to other employees of the employer" <Cite id="[2019] FCA 1754" at="[72]" phrase="requires less favourable treatment of an employee (or a group of employees) in comparison to other employees of the employer" />. This is a single judge's view on a point that has divided the Court. In practice, less favourable treatment will usually fall under the "prejudice" limb as well.

## The rest of the table

The full table is reproduced in *Robinson v BMF (No 2)*. Items 2 to 7 matter more often than their place in the section suggests, because they reach people who aren't the employer and people who aren't employees.

### Job applicants: item 2

A prospective employer takes adverse action if it <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1191" at="[36]" phrase="refuses to employ the prospective employee" quote />, or discriminates against the applicant in the terms it offers. This is covered under "Job applicants" below.

### Contractors, and the people who work for them: items 3 and 4

A principal takes adverse action against an independent contractor if it <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1191" at="[36]" phrase="terminates the contract" quote />, injures the contractor in relation to the contract's terms, alters the contractor's position to its prejudice, or <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1191" at="[36]" phrase="refuses to make use of, or agree to make use of, services offered by the independent contractor" quote />. Item 4 applies the same idea to a principal proposing to engage a contractor.

Two points are easy to miss.

- **"Independent contractor" is read widely.** In *Azad v Mithila Family Trust*, a doctor engaged by a clinic as a contractor relied on item 3. Dowling J noted that the words <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1650" at="[68]" phrase="are not limited only to such contractors who might be seen as analogous to employees" quote />. A business-to-business supplier can be covered.
- **The protection extends to the contractor's workers.** Item 3 covers action by the principal against the contractor <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1191" at="[36]" phrase="or a person employed or engaged by the independent contractor" quote />. A host business that tells a labour hire agency to remove a worker, or a head contractor that has a subcontractor's employee taken off a site, is within the table even though it doesn't employ that person. If the reason is the worker's complaint, union role or protected attribute, the host is exposed in its own right. Module 33 discusses a host being sued as an accessory as well.

### Action against an employer: items 5 and 6

The table also runs the other way. An employee takes adverse action against the employer if the employee <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1191" at="[36]" phrase="ceases work in the service of the employer" quote /> or takes industrial action against it, and item 6 says the same of a contractor. Items 5 and 6 define the adverse-action element in a reverse-direction claim; they don't independently create liability. A viable claim still requires the relevant protected matter, the applicable prohibition and the statutory causal elements. Whether conduct is protected industrial action is governed by the industrial-action provisions, not answered by s.342 alone. No decision applying items 5 or 6 was read for this guide, so any reliance on these items should be escalated.

### Unions, officers and members: item 7

An industrial association, or an officer or member of one, takes adverse action against a person if it organises or takes industrial action against the person, or <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1191" at="[36]" phrase="takes action that has the effect, directly or indirectly, of prejudicing the person in the person's employment or prospective employment" quote />. For a contractor, the test is prejudice in relation to a contract for services. For a member, it's where the association <Cite id="[2022] FCA 1191" at="[36]" phrase="imposes a penalty, forfeiture or disability of any kind on the member" quote />.

In *Fair Work Ombudsman v Australian Workers' Union*, the regulator alleged that a union laid disciplinary charges against two members because they hadn't joined protected industrial action. The union applied to have the case dismissed early, arguing among other things that nothing had yet been imposed on the members. Bromberg J refused: <Cite id="[2017] FCA 528" at="[92]" phrase="the application for summary judgment must be dismissed" quote />. That was a decision that the case could proceed, not a finding of contravention.

For an employer, item 7 matters in two ways. A union official who has a worker excluded from a site for not being a member takes adverse action under this item, and an employer that carries out the exclusion takes it under item 1 or 3 (Module 31). And a union that is itself an employer can be caught by more than one item: in *CFMMEU v Quirk*, the trial judge found adverse action under items 1 and 7 at once (Module 30).

## Process steps: investigations, suspensions and directions

Most disciplinary processes involve several steps before any dismissal. Each may be a separate action, and an employee can allege that each was adverse. A recent summary lists "Actions that have been found to amount to adverse action" as including suspension, undertaking an investigation into an employee's conduct, and issuing a show cause letter <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[100]" phrase="Actions that have been found to amount to adverse action" />. Assess each step separately by its objective effect, duration, manner and surrounding circumstances; don't turn the list into an automatic legal conclusion.

**Investigations.** The employer in *Avard* argued that starting a misconduct investigation wasn't adverse, because no finding had been made and the employee might be cleared. The judge rejected that argument on the facts and reasoning of that case. Being investigated may be stressful, costly and damaging to reputation, and "Repeated investigations can be an effective tool of harassment" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[136]" phrase="Repeated investigations can be an effective tool of harassment" />. A good reason to investigate goes to why the action was taken; "it is not relevant at the anterior stage of considering whether the action was" adverse <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[136]" phrase="it is not relevant at the anterior stage of considering whether the action was" />. The investigation must still be assessed by its objective effects, including its duration, manner and consequences.

**Suspensions.** In *Heal v Sydney Flames*, the employer accepted that suspending a head coach was adverse action, and the judge so found <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[175]" phrase="the act of suspending Mr Heal was" />. An email naming the external investigator wasn't a further adverse action; it was a continuation of the suspension <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[176]" phrase="not any additional or discrete adverse action undertaken by Sydney Flames" />.

*Avard* shows the other end. A direction not to attend work, on full pay and with the role preserved, lasting about two months, was held not to be adverse on the evidence in that case. It could have been "if it had persisted for long enough to be professionally disadvantageous" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[133]" phrase="if it had persisted for long enough to be professionally disadvantageous" />. That finding turned on what the employee proved about the effect on her. It isn't a rule that paid suspensions are safe.

**Medical assessments.** For a senior clinician, being directed to an independent medical examination "is not, without more, injurious or prejudicial" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[118]" phrase="is not, without more, injurious or prejudicial" />. Again, this was a finding on the evidence, not a general rule.

**What to assume.** Treat every formal step — investigation, suspension, show cause letter, warning, transfer, removal of duties — as a potential adverse-action issue requiring separate assessment. Don't assume that a paid suspension, an investigation or a show cause letter is legally harmless; equally, don't state that every such step is adverse action without analysing its objective effect. The employer in *Heal* conceded the suspension and still won, because each decision-maker proved their reasons, and the general protections claims were dismissed <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[382]" phrase="are otherwise to be dismissed" />. That's where these cases are decided; see [Module 27](/modules/the-reason-for-the-action). The practical consequence is that the reasons for each step, its effects, and who decided it, need to be recorded at each step and not only at the end.

## Threats: s.342(2)

Adverse action includes threatening to take action covered by the table, and organising it. A threat to dismiss is adverse action even if the dismissal never happens.

The Full Court in *AFAP v Regional Express* set out the principles, quoting an earlier judgment. A threat is a communicated intention to inflict harm, and it "must involve an expression of an intimidatory purpose" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 226" at="[149]" phrase="must involve an expression of an intimidatory purpose" />. It can be conditional: "X will occur if Y does not" <Cite id="[2021] FCAFC 226" at="[149]" phrase="a threat to take action may be conditional" />. It doesn't matter whether the person intended to carry it out.

The earlier judgment is *Fair Work Ombudsman v Australian Workers' Union*, which has now been read directly for this guide. Bromberg J drew seven principles from the cases. The central one is that <Cite id="[2017] FCA 528" at="[54]" phrase="the essence of a threat is that it is made for the purpose of intimidating a person" quote />. He then marked the limit that *Totino* later applied: <Cite id="[2017] FCA 528" at="[55]" phrase="There is a distinction to be drawn between the provision of information and a threat" quote />.

In September 2026 the Full Court applied those principles to a show cause letter. In *Totino v Metro Trains*, the letter said the employer was proposing to terminate the employment because of medical incapacity, invited a response by a set date, and said a decision would follow. That wasn't a threat. "Not every notification of a prospective harm, either with or without conditions, amounts to a" threat <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[81]" phrase="Not every notification of a prospective harm, either with or without conditions, amounts to a" />. The dividing line is inducement: "intimidation occurs when the possibility of dismissal is used to induce a person to do something that the person is not already required to do" <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[80]" phrase="intimidation occurs when the possibility of dismissal is used to induce a person to do something that the person is not already required to do" />. The appeal was dismissed <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[110]" phrase="the appeal is dismissed" />.

The Court gave two examples of the other kind: managers telling workers they would be dismissed unless they approved changes to an enterprise agreement, and an employer telling an employee to sign a contractor agreement or resign.

The first example is *CFMEU v De Martin & Gasparini (No 2)*. Employees had voted down a variation to their enterprise agreement that the company needed. Days later the company issued a memorandum about possible redundancies and two managers addressed the workforce. The company said it was only giving information as part of consultation. Wigney J accepted that the memorandum alone might be read that way, but not in context: <Cite id="[2017] FCA 1046" at="[269]" phrase="It comprised far more than the benign communication of information" quote />. The managers' statements showed the purpose: <Cite id="[2017] FCA 1046" at="[264]" phrase="it was intended to inspire or instil fear in the employees" quote />. The company contravened s.340 <Cite id="[2017] FCA 1046" at="[383]" phrase="constituted adverse action because it amounted to a threat" />.

*Messenger v Commonwealth* is on the other side of the line, and is the source of the reasoning in *Totino*. Two staff members received show cause letters and were later dismissed. They pleaded the letters as threats to dismiss. Snaden J held they weren't: <Cite id="[2022] FCA 677" at="[164]" phrase="a communication that alludes only to the possibility, conditional or otherwise, that something might happen is not a communication by which that possibility is threatened" quote />. Even if the outcome was a foregone conclusion, what counted was what the letters said, and <Cite id="[2022] FCA 677" at="[169]" phrase="they made clear that the Senator would consider what, if anything, Mr and Mrs Messenger chose to advance" quote />.

<div class="key">
<h3>Show cause letters: what <em>Totino</em> does and doesn't decide</h3>
<ol class="principles">
<li><strong>It decides</strong> that the particular show cause letter in <em>Totino</em> — which stated reasons, gave a real opportunity to respond and left the decision to follow — wasn't a <em>threat</em> under s.342(2) because it lacked an intimidatory purpose.</li>
<li><strong>It doesn't decide</strong> that a show cause letter can never be adverse action under the "injures" or "prejudice" limbs. Earlier authority, as summarised in <em>Heal</em>, includes issuing a show cause letter among actions found to be adverse. In <em>Messenger</em>, the employees lost on this point only because they had pleaded the letters as threats. Had they pleaded that the letters altered their position to their prejudice, the judge said, <Cite id="[2022] FCA 677" at="[171]" phrase="it seems all but inevitable that the court would have concluded that those letters sufficed to visit adverse action upon them" quote />.</li>
<li><strong>Drafting matters.</strong> A letter that says "unless you withdraw your complaint", or presents an outcome already decided, moves from information to intimidation.</li>
</ol>
</div>

## "The agreement allows it": s.342(3)

Adverse action doesn't include action "authorised by or under" the Fair Work Act, another Commonwealth law, or a prescribed State or Territory law. Employers sometimes argue that because an enterprise agreement permits a step, such as a suspension or a direction to attend a medical assessment, the step is authorised under the Act and can't be adverse action.

*Avard* rejected that argument. It "would significantly narrow the concept of adverse action" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[88]" phrase="would significantly narrow the concept of adverse action" />. The judge drew a line between what an agreement requires and what it permits. Where an agreement only permits a step, "it is neither granting a statutory power to take that action nor exempting that action from consequences" <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[96]" phrase="it is neither granting a statutory power to take that action nor exempting that action from consequences" />. The submission failed <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[97]" phrase="I therefore reject the submission" />.

*De Martin & Gasparini (No 2)*, one of the earlier decisions *Avard* followed, shows the same point from a different angle. The company said its statements to employees were authorised because the enterprise agreement required it to consult about major change, and the Act requires compliance with agreements. Wigney J disagreed: <Cite id="[2017] FCA 1046" at="[283]" phrase="It does not follow, however, that s 50 or any other provision of the Fair Work Act authorises any conduct that is engaged in pursuant to, or purportedly pursuant to, the consultation process" quote />.

So an applicable agreement clause, complied with on its terms, may answer a claim that the suspension breached the agreement. It doesn't by itself answer a general protections claim or establish that the action was authorised by legislation for s.342(3). This is a single judge's decision, following earlier single-judge decisions; treat any s.342(3) argument as one for escalation.

## Job applicants

Under item 2, a prospective employer takes adverse action by refusing to employ a person, or by discriminating against them in the terms offered.

In *ABCC v CoreStaff*, a labour hire company told a 70-year-old applicant for a machine operator's job that he had been unsuccessful because of his age. The company argued there was no real vacancy, because its client wouldn't have taken him. The judge found there was a vacancy and a refusal to employ, and noted that the authorities on who is a prospective employee "support a broad and common sense approach to its meaning" <Cite id="[2020] FCA 893" at="[67]" phrase="support a broad and common sense approach to its meaning" />. The company contravened s.351 <Cite id="[2020] FCA 893" at="[116]" phrase="took adverse action against Mr Selsmark within the meaning of Item 2(a)" />.

A client's preference isn't a defence for a labour hire employer or a recruiter. The person who refuses to employ is the one who takes the adverse action.

## How adverse action arguments fail

<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Directed home on full pay, role intact</h3>
<p>A senior doctor who lodged safety reports was sent for a medical assessment and told not to attend work for about two months. She kept her title and pay, and didn't prove the absence harmed her professionally. <Cite id="[2024] FCA 690" at="[120]" phrase="the IME direction and the direction not to attend work did not constitute" /></p>
<p class="lesson">The employee must prove real prejudice. Later steps in the same case — the investigations and the transfer — were adverse action, and the employer won those on its reasons.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Suspension conceded, reasons proved</h3>
<p>A head coach was suspended after players made allegations about his conduct. The club accepted the suspension was adverse action. Each person involved in the decision gave evidence of their reasons. <Cite id="[2024] FCA 401" at="[196]" phrase="was not a substantial or operative reason for the decision makers taking the Suspension Adverse Action" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Conceding that a step is adverse action costs little if the reasons are sound and provable.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>A show cause letter that informed</h3>
<p>The letter proposed termination for medical incapacity, invited a response and promised a decision afterwards. The employee responded, and the response was considered. <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[85]" phrase="did not contain any intimidatory purpose" /></p>
<p class="lesson">State the reasons, give a real chance to respond, and decide afterwards.</p>
</div>

<span class="tag">Regulator won</span>
<h3>"Unsuccessful because of your age"</h3>
<p>A labour hire company passed on its client's concern about a 70-year-old applicant and told him age was the reason. <Cite id="[2020] FCA 893" at="[116]" phrase="because of his age and therefore contravened s 351(1)" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Refusing to employ is adverse action, and adopting a client's preference doesn't change who took it.</p>

- ***Fair Work Ombudsman v Australian Workers' Union* [2017] FCA 528** (Bromberg J, 17 May 2017) — a decision refusing summary judgment; no finding of contravention. Read for the principles on threats and for item 7 of the table.
- ***CFMEU v De Martin & Gasparini (No 2)* [2017] FCA 1046** (Wigney J, 1 September 2017) — the company contravened s.340 by a threat; the two managers weren't found to be accessories. Read for the threat findings and s.342(3).
- ***Morton v CSIRO (No 2)* [2019] FCA 1754** (Rangiah J, 29 October 2019) — cited here for the meaning of "discriminates between"; see also Module 30.
- ***ABCC v CoreStaff WA* [2020] FCA 893** (Banks-Smith J, 26 June 2020) — the labour hire company contravened s.351; the claim that its client was involved wasn't read for this guide.
- ***Australian Federation of Air Pilots v Regional Express Holdings* [2021] FCAFC 226** (Bromberg, Kerr and Wheelahan JJ, 16 December 2021) — read only for the principles on threats; parts of the claim were sent back for rehearing.
- ***Messenger v Commonwealth of Australia* [2022] FCA 677** (Snaden J, 10 June 2022) — applications dismissed. Read for the show cause letter reasoning.
- ***Robinson v BMF (in liq) (No 2)* [2022] FCA 1191** (Mortimer J, 7 October 2022) — read only for the text of the s.342(1) table set out at [36].
- ***Heal v Sydney Flames Basketball* [2024] FCA 401** (Halley J, 19 April 2024) — general protections claims dismissed; the employee succeeded on contract claims.
- ***Avard v Australian Capital Territory* [2024] FCA 690** (Kennett J, 28 June 2024) — application dismissed.
- ***Azad v Mithila Family Trust* [2025] FCA 1650** (Dowling J, 18 December 2025) — application dismissed; see Module 25. Cited here for the reach of item 3.
- ***Totino v Metro Trains Melbourne* [2026] FCAFC 120** (McEvoy, Dowling and Hill JJ, 8 September 2026) — appeal dismissed.

*Patrick Stevedores v MUA*, *Klein v Metropolitan Fire and Emergency Services Board*, *Sayed v CFMEU* and *CFMEU v Pilbara Iron (No 3)* are relied on only as quoted or summarised in the decisions above and haven't been read in the original for this module. No decision applying items 5 or 6 of the table was read.
