<p class="lead">A general protections claim does not end in the Commission. It is decided by a court, compensation has no cap, penalties are added on top, and the managers who made the decision can be sued personally. This module covers how a claim gets to court, what a court can order, who can be ordered to pay, and what the costs rules mean for settlement.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Tell a dismissal claim from a non-dismissal claim, and say which steps and time limits apply to each.
- Check an application for the points an employer can properly take: no dismissal, out of time, wrong respondent, a second claim about the same conduct.
- Explain to a manager what a court can order, and why the amount is hard to predict.
- Explain who is personally exposed, and what a claimant has to prove against an individual.
- Use the costs rules when advising on an offer of settlement.

## Two routes

The route depends on whether the person says they were dismissed.

| | Dismissal claim | Non-dismissal claim |
|---|---|---|
| First step | Application to the Commission (s.365) | Optional application to the Commission (s.372), or directly to court |
| Time limit | 21 days after the dismissal took effect (s.366) | Six years after the contravention for a court application (s.544) |
| Commission's role | Conference; a certificate if the dispute is not resolved (s.368) | Conference only if both sides agree |
| Court | Within 14 days after the certificate (s.370) | No certificate needed |

For a dismissal, the Commission stage is a gate. The employee cannot go to court without the certificate, with one exception discussed below. For anything short of dismissal, such as a warning, a demotion or a suspension, the employee can go to court directly and has years to do it. An employer who has kept an aggrieved employee should not assume the risk has passed after 21 days.

If a dismissal dispute is not resolved at conference, the parties can agree to have the Commission arbitrate it (s.369). Both must agree. Consent arbitration permits the Commission to determine the dismissal dispute and make remedial orders, but it cannot result in a civil penalty. Sections 727 and 728 restrict a later court application concerning the same dispute after arbitration.

## The Commission stage: points an employer can take

In the ordinary s.368 conference process, the Commission does not determine whether the general protections were breached. It can decide whether it has an application it is allowed to deal with. Consent arbitration under s.369 is different: if both parties agree, the Commission can determine the dismissal dispute and make remedial orders, subject to the statutory consequences for later proceedings.

In <em>Coles Supply Chain v Milford</em>, a casual store worker applied in August 2018 and said he was dismissed in July 2018. Coles said his last shift was in 2014, and later that he had not been dismissed at all. A Full Bench of the Commission held it had to take the date the employee alleged. The Full Court of the Federal Court disagreed. Whether there was a dismissal <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 152" at="[65]" phrase="raises an antecedent issue going to the existence of the FWC's authority" />. The Court summarised the position: <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 152" at="[67]" phrase="it is open to a respondent to assert that there has been no dismissal" />, and it is also open to a respondent to accept there was a dismissal but <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 152" at="[67]" phrase="dispute that the dismissal took effect within 21 days" />. Both are disputes that <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 152" at="[67]" phrase="must be resolved before the powers conferred by s 368 can be exercised at all" />.

Two practical points follow.

- **Take the objection early and in writing.** If the employer says the person resigned, was a contractor whose engagement ended, or was dismissed more than 21 days before applying, say so in the response. The Commission decides that question before it holds a conference.
- **Do not overvalue a win.** A Commission finding that there was no dismissal <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 152" at="[79]" phrase="is not authoritative in the sense of being final" />. The person may still bring a non-dismissal claim about the same events, depending on the facts.

The Full Court quashed the Full Bench decision and sent the matter back <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 152" at="[83]" phrase="It is appropriate to grant relief in terms of orders in the nature of writs of certiorari and mandamus" />.

## Getting to court

### Fourteen days from the certificate

After the certificate issues, the employee has 14 days to file in court, or a longer period if the court allows it. The solicitors for the employer in <em>Blade v Jones Lang LaSalle</em> put the rule this way: the court application must be made <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1183" at="[11]" phrase="within 14 days after the day the Certificate had been issued" />.

Two employees were dismissed on the same day. Their solicitor filed a court proceeding the next day, before either had a certificate, and later added the dismissal claims by amendment. One amendment was filed before that employee's certificate had issued. Halley J held that the dismissal claims had not been validly made. The only way forward was an extension of time.

The starting point for an extension favours the employer: <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1183" at="[37]" phrase="The prima facie position is that the time limit should be complied with unless there is an acceptable explanation for the delay" />. The extension was granted, for two main reasons. <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1183" at="[74]" phrase="The delay was due to errors made by Mr Pike's legal representatives, not to any act or omission of Mr Pike" />. And the employer had known of the dismissal claims from the start, so <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1183" at="[75]" phrase="there is no relevant prejudice to JLL" />. The judge described the result as one reached <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1183" at="[7]" phrase="in the somewhat peculiar circumstances of this case" />.

For an employer's adviser: diarise the certificate date and the fourteenth day. If nothing is filed, the claim is not necessarily dead, but the employee needs the court's leave and an explanation. Keep a record of anything the delay has cost the employer, such as a witness who has left or a document that has been lost, because prejudice counts against an extension.

### Mixed claims: which parts are subject to the 14 days

Court applications often combine a dismissal claim with claims about earlier treatment, such as warnings, demotion or underpayment. Does a late filing put the whole application out of time, or only the dismissal part?

Judges have disagreed. In *AXR54 v Piper Alderman*, an employee filed nine days late an application that mixed dismissal and non-dismissal claims. The employer argued she needed an extension for all of it. Perram J noted that one earlier decision supported the employer and two went the other way; in one of those, <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1509" at="[5]" phrase="Katzmann J concluded that Kelly was plainly wrong and declined to follow it" />. He was not bound by any of them <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1509" at="[6]" phrase="On the state of the authorities, I am not bound to any particular outcome" />, and held that the time limit applies only to the dismissal claims: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1509" at="[7]" phrase="No rational purpose is served by requiring the time bar to apply to non-dismissal claims" />.

The extension for the dismissal claims was granted. The delay was short <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1509" at="[9]" phrase="the nine-day delay is not a large one" />, it was explained, and <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1509" at="[12]" phrase="There is no evidence of any prejudice to the respondents" />. The employer's five arguments that the claim lacked merit were all rejected as matters for trial or for particulars, and the judge added that he would <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1509" at="[31]" phrase="encourage the respondents to be more discerning in the submissions they advance" />.

For an employer's adviser:

- Treat a late mixed application as late only for its dismissal claims. The non-dismissal claims are very likely to go ahead whatever happens on the extension.
- The point is not settled. Three single judges favour that view and one is against it, and no Full Court decision was found. If the answer matters, escalate.
- Opposing a short, explained delay with no prejudice to point to is unlikely to succeed, and merits arguments at that stage need to be strong and few.

### Every respondent must have been part of the Commission dispute

In <em>McGrory v Horizon One Recruitment</em>, a worker placed by a recruitment company with a host organisation named only the recruitment company in her Commission application, describing the host as a "host client". In court she sued both. Collier J dismissed the claim against the host: <Cite id="[2024] FCA 810" at="[20]" phrase="the Court lacks jurisdiction in respect of the claim by Dr McGrory against the second respondent" />. The reason was put simply: <Cite id="[2024] FCA 810" at="[17]" phrase="Litigation is dependent on clear claims, being brought clearly, against specific named respondents" />.

When a court application arrives, compare its respondents with the Commission application and the certificate. This matters most for individuals and related companies added at the court stage. Module 2 has more on the gateway.

### The interim injunction exception

A dismissal claim can go straight to court, with no certificate, if it includes an application for an interim injunction. In <em>Weber v Thomas Foods International (Stawell)</em>, a truck driver filed in court 79 days after his dismissal and asked for interim reinstatement. The employer argued that the 21-day limit for Commission applications must apply. Wheelahan J rejected that. Section 370(a) has a time limit, but <Cite id="[2024] FCA 1506" at="[44]" phrase="paragraph (b) does not provide for any time limit" />.

The judge left open a different case: <Cite id="[2024] FCA 1506" at="[39]" phrase="a colourable claim for an interim injunction might take a general protections court application outside the terms of s 370(b)" />. An injunction claim added only to avoid the Commission may not work.

The driver also got the interim order. He had <Cite id="[2024] FCA 1506" at="[67]" phrase="a reasonably good prima facie case of contravention" />, and he was reinstated until trial, though not with back pay: <Cite id="[2024] FCA 1506" at="[70]" phrase="the minimum equity to do justice between the parties is to order reinstatement from the date of this order" />.

Two lessons. A dismissed employee who has missed the 21 days may still have a route to court. And interim reinstatement is a real possibility where the reasons for dismissal look weak on the documents. An employer served with such an application needs evidence about the reasons within days, not weeks.

## One claim about the same conduct

The Act stops a person running two claims about the same thing. Sections 725 to 732 deal with dismissals: a person who has made one kind of dismissal application, such as unfair dismissal, generally cannot also make another, such as general protections, unless the first was withdrawn or failed for want of jurisdiction. Sections 727 and 728 also matter where a dismissal dispute has been referred to consent arbitration under s.369. Section 734 does the same for non-dismissal conduct and discrimination complaints.

In <em>Deam v Starlight Children's Foundation Australia</em>, an employee complained to the Australian Human Rights Commission about disability discrimination. The complaint was terminated. She then brought a general protections claim in court about the same events. Mortimer J held that a person <Cite id="[2023] FCA 259" at="[86]" phrase="will only be able to seek final resolution of either their claim under anti-discrimination law or their general protections claim" />. Timing is what matters. The bar applies when the second claim is made, and by then <Cite id="[2023] FCA 259" at="[89]" phrase="the first complaint or application must have been withdrawn" />. The wording of the two claims differed, but the judge was satisfied the court claim relied on <Cite id="[2023] FCA 259" at="[99]" phrase="the same or substantially the same conduct alleged in her AHRC complaint" />. Once the bar applies there is no discretion: <Cite id="[2023] FCA 259" at="[114]" phrase="the Court must give effect to the prohibition by dismissing the prohibited proceeding" />.

The employer in that case prepared a table comparing the two claims allegation by allegation. The judge found it helpful. Do the same whenever an employee has an earlier or parallel claim on foot: an unfair dismissal application, a discrimination complaint, or a claim under State law.

## What a court can order

A court that finds a contravention may make any order it considers appropriate (s.545). The orders that matter most are reinstatement, compensation and injunctions. There is no cap, and no equivalent of the 26-week limit in unfair dismissal.

### Reinstatement

Reinstatement is available but depends on there being a job. In <em>Gavin v Svitzer Australia (Relief)</em>, a manager was made redundant in a "snap" decision after exercising a workplace right. Needham J accepted that the facts pointed towards reinstatement but refused it: <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[34]" phrase="I do not consider that it is practicably possible to reinstate Mr Gavin" />. The port had been restructured and <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[34]" phrase="There is no position into which he can be reinstated" />.

### Compensation must be caused by the contravention

Compensation is for loss suffered because of the contravention. That is narrower than loss caused by the job.

<em>Han v St Basil's Homes (No 2)</em> is the remedies decision that followed the liability decision discussed in Module 27. A nurse was dismissed because she had complained and because of her race. She claimed about $2.47 million <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[3]" phrase="a claim for compensation and penalties totalling $2,471,381.65" />. The employer said she should get almost nothing <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[4]" phrase="nothing more than a nominal amount of compensation should be awarded and that no penalties should be imposed" />. Shariff J called both positions ambitious.

Her evidence treated every workplace cause of her psychiatric injury as compensable. The judge said it <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[58]" phrase="did not tether itself to the statutory norm" />. The contravention was the dismissal. So <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[72]" phrase="the relevant question is whether that personal injury was caused by the contraventions" />.

She was awarded $175,000 for past economic loss, about $61,560 for future economic loss, $75,000 in general damages and $10,000 for future expenses <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[5]" phrase="but not to the extent of her claims" />.

For an employer, the lesson has two sides. Separate what the unlawful act caused from what came before it, and require the claimant to prove the link. But do not argue that a proved contravention caused no loss at all unless the evidence supports that.

### Hurt and humiliation

Compensation covers non-economic loss. In <em>Gavin</em>, the judge said <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[58]" phrase="Section 545(2)(b) would encompass the power to award compensation for non-economic loss" />. The amounts turn on the facts. Ms Han received $75,000 because she had <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[157]" phrase="genuinely felt distress, hurt and humiliation that is causally connected to the contraventions" />. Mr Gavin, whose loss arose from one decision and a meeting that was not badly handled, received $15,000 <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[66]" phrase="general damages for Mr Gavin should be paid at $15,000" />. Compensation is not meant to punish. That is the job of penalties.

### Mitigation

What the employee earns elsewhere reduces the loss. Mr Gavin took another job, and the judge accepted that <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[51]" phrase="by taking a position at Thales, Mr Gavin has sought to mitigate his loss" />. Ask for evidence of post-dismissal earnings early. The costs case below shows why.

## Penalties

A penalty is separate from compensation and is ordered in addition to it (s.546). Its purpose is deterrence. The maximum is set in penalty units and changes over time, so check the current figure. In <em>Gavin</em>, <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[67]" phrase="The maximum penalty for each contravention in this case was $82,500" />. The maximum for an individual is one-fifth of the corporate maximum: in an earlier case the figures were <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[54]" phrase="$63,000 for a corporation and $12,600 for an individual" />.

**A civil penalty is discretionary.** The Court assesses the appropriate penalty by reference to deterrence, seriousness, the respondent's conduct and the statutory framework. A penalty is ordinarily payable to the Commonwealth, although the Court may direct payment to another person. The number of contraventions depends on the relevant provisions, the conduct and the statutory course-of-conduct rules.

The cases illustrate the range. In <em>Han (No 2)</em>, the judge imposed $45,000 for the discrimination contravention and $15,000 for the workplace-right contravention, against a maximum of $63,000 each <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[219]" phrase="I do not accept that the present case is one in which no penalty should be imposed" /> <Cite id="[2025] FCA 448" at="[220]" phrase="a penalty of $45,000 should be imposed" />. In <em>Gavin</em>, two contraventions arising from one decision attracted a single penalty <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[81]" phrase="a penalty of $20,000 should be imposed" />; the Court directed payment to the applicant on the facts of that case <Cite id="[2026] FCA 1582" at="[82]" phrase="it is appropriate that it be paid to him" />. In <em>Transport Workers' Union v Qantas Airways (Penalty)</em>, one outsourcing decision generated substantial exposure across many affected workers, but the decision does not establish a universal per-person multiplier <Cite id="[2025] FCA 971" at="[70]" phrase="leading to a maximum penalty available to be imposed of $121,212,000" /> <Cite id="[2025] FCA 971" at="[230]" phrase="this contravention was a single decision" />.

What the employer does after the finding also counts. In <em>Qantas</em>, the judge explained how contrition works: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 971" at="[31]" phrase="this is not a factor that aggravates penalty" />, but genuine remorse can justify a lower penalty because less is needed to deter.

## Personal exposure

A person "involved in" a contravention is treated as having contravened the provision (s.550). Involvement includes aiding, inducing, and being knowingly concerned in the contravention. Directors, managers and HR advisers can be named as respondents, penalised and ordered to pay compensation.

Against a person alleged to be an accessory under s.550, the claimant generally has to prove actual knowledge of the essential matters that constitute the contravention and participation in it. In <em>Haire v WorkCo Australia (No 2)</em>, Horan J put the knowledge requirement this way: <Cite id="[2024] FCA 1266" at="[63]" phrase="the alleged accessory must have actual knowledge of the essential matters that constitute the contravention" />. Both knowledge and participation are needed: <Cite id="[2024] FCA 1266" at="[64]" phrase="neither participation without knowledge nor knowledge without participation will satisfy s 550(2)(c)" />. The s.361 reverse onus generally does not supply the employer's presumed reason against an accessory. If the individual personally took the adverse action, a separate inquiry into that individual's relevant reason may also arise; do not treat proof of a prohibited personal reason as a universal additional element of every s.550 claim.

<div class="key">
<strong>The reverse onus does not usually reach the individual</strong>
<ol class="principles">
<li>Against the employer, the prohibited reason is presumed unless the employer proves otherwise (Module 27).</li>
<li>Against an individual alleged as an accessory, the claimant usually has to prove the s.550 knowledge-and-participation elements; the analysis is not simply the employer's s.361 presumption applied to the individual.</li>
<li>So a claim can succeed against the employer and fail against some or all of the individuals.</li>
</ol>
</div>

<em>SBP Employment Solutions v Smith</em> shows the difference. An employee was dismissed after making complaints about his employment. The trial judge found the employer and all four directors liable. On appeal Rangiah J said <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[181]" phrase="The presumption arising under s 361 of the FWA does not usually apply to an allegation of accessorial liability" />. The employee had complained to two of the directors. Those two <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[188]" phrase="intentionally directed and participated in the contravening conduct" /> and stayed liable. The other two had not received the complaints. They <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[187]" phrase="did not give evidence" />, and still the findings against them were set aside, because the employee had not proved their reasons <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[191]" phrase="must be set aside" />.

Exposure is not limited to people inside the employer. In <em>Haire</em>, the employee was employed by one company but her work was for another organisation. She sued that organisation and one of its officers as accessories to her dismissal, alleging the officer knew the reasons and had enough influence to stop it. They applied to have the claim thrown out early and failed. The judge could not say there was no reasonable prospect of proving <Cite id="[2024] FCA 1266" at="[78]" phrase="there was a practical connection between Ms Klimen and Filecoin and that contravention" />. That was a decision that the claim could go to trial, not a finding of liability. A host or client who asks for a worker to be removed should expect its reasons to be examined too.

What this means for the people an adviser works with:

- The manager who decides, knowing of the complaint, is the person most exposed.
- A person who only carries out a decision, without knowing why it was made, is much less exposed.
- An adviser who knows the real reason and helps carry the decision out is exposed. An adviser who gives advice and leaves the decision to others is in a different position; see the discussion of <em>Totino</em> in Module 27.
- Tell individuals early if they are named. Their interests may differ from the employer's, and they may need separate advice. This is a point to escalate.

## Costs and settlement

Each side normally pays its own costs, win or lose (s.570). A party can be ordered to pay the other's costs only if it started the proceeding vexatiously or without reasonable cause, or its unreasonable act or omission caused the other side to incur costs, or it unreasonably refused to take part in a Commission matter arising from the same facts.

Three consequences.

**Winning does not recover costs.** In <em>McGrory</em>, the host got the claim against it dismissed for want of jurisdiction and still did not get costs <Cite id="[2024] FCA 810" at="[24]" phrase="I am not satisfied that an award of costs against Dr McGrory in favour of the second respondent is justified" />. A defended general protections case will cost the employer its own legal fees whatever the result. That belongs in every settlement assessment.

**Rejecting a reasonable offer can be an unreasonable act, but the bar is high.** In <em>Mount v Dover Castle Metals (Costs)</em>, the employer won at trial after making three settlement offers. Katzmann J accepted the principle that <Cite id="[2025] FCA 402" at="[20]" phrase="a failure to accept a reasonable offer of compromise may constitute an unreasonable act" />, and that the offers were reasonable. The employee had already earned more elsewhere than he would have earned in the job. But when the first two offers were made he still had general protections claims on foot, and if he had won <Cite id="[2025] FCA 402" at="[74]" phrase="he may have recovered civil penalties under s 546 of the FW Act" />. The test is applied as things stood when the offer was made: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 402" at="[77]" phrase="viewed prospectively rather than retrospectively" />. The costs application was dismissed, although the judge described the rejections as <Cite id="[2025] FCA 402" at="[77]" phrase="certainly imprudent, if not foolish" />.

**The Commission conference counts.** Refusing unreasonably to take part in the Commission stage is one of the three grounds for a costs order. Attend, and send someone who can settle.

A written offer, marked without prejudice save as to costs, with reasons and a reasonable time to accept, is still worth making. It may not produce a costs order. It does put the employer's assessment on the record, and it tests whether the claimant is pursuing loss or a penalty.

## Practice scenario: a court application at Brightwell Dental Group

  <Fragment slot="facts">

  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Yes, on these dates.</strong> The Commission application was made 14 days after the dismissal took effect, and the court application 12 days after the certificate. Check both against the documents, but there is no time point.</li>
      <li><strong>There is a point to take, and it needs advice.</strong> Neither individual was named in the Commission. On the reasoning in <em>McGrory</em>, a respondent who was not part of the Commission dispute can ask for the claim against them to be dismissed. Whether that reasoning applies to individuals alleged to be involved in their employer's contravention was not decided in the cases read for this module. Escalate it. On the merits, Dana received the complaint and made the decision, so she is the person most exposed. Lucas's position depends on what he knew. The presumption does not usually apply against either of them, so Priya has to prove their involvement. Both should be told now and the question of separate advice raised.</li>
      <li><strong>Probably not, but compare the two.</strong> The bar depends on the two claims being about the same conduct. The discrimination complaint is about a February roster change. The court claim is about the May dismissal. If the court claim also relies on the roster change, that part needs a closer look.</li>
      <li><strong>The chief executive is wrong about "nothing in it".</strong> Economic loss is small: about two months' pay. General damages for hurt and humiliation are available, and a civil penalty is discretionary; its amount and destination require separate analysis under Module 33. Brightwell will also pay its own costs even if it wins. The reason given, "poor fit", is not documented, and the complaint was two months old. Assess the evidence of Dana's reasons first, as Module 27 describes. Then consider settlement.</li>
