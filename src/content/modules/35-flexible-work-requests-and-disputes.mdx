<p class="lead">Since the Secure Jobs, Better Pay changes, an employer's refusal of a flexible work request can be arbitrated by the Commission. The Commission can order the employer to grant the request. Most employers that lose these cases don't lose because the business case was bad. They lose because they refused before discussing the request, or asserted business grounds they couldn't prove. This module covers the request, the response, and what happens when the Commission is asked to decide.</p>

## What you'll be able to do

- Work out whether a request is a valid request under s.65, at the time it was made.
- Run the four steps s.65A(3) requires before a refusal, in the order the Commission expects.
- Build a reasonable-business-grounds case on evidence rather than assertion.
- Explain to a manager what the Commission can order under s.65C, and why fairness between the parties matters even when the employer is right.

## Is it a valid request?

Section 65 gives a right to **request**, not a right to the change. The right is narrower than many employees, and some managers, assume. A request is only made under s.65 if each of the following is true:

- **A listed circumstance applies to the employee.** The employee is pregnant; is the parent of, or responsible for the care of, a child of school age or younger; is a carer; has a disability; is 55 or older; is experiencing family and domestic violence; or is caring for or supporting an immediate family or household member who is experiencing family and domestic violence (s.65(1A)).
- **The change is because of that circumstance.** The Commission looks for a link <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[3]" phrase="between the request and the circumstance upon which it is based" />.
- **Service.** At least 12 months' continuous service immediately before the request, or, for a casual, regular casual employment over at least 12 months with a reasonable expectation that it will continue (s.65(2)).
- **Form.** The request is in writing and sets out the change sought and the reasons for it (s.65(3)).

**Timing matters.** In *Zammit v Sydney Water Corporation*, the employee asked for a compressed week and more working from home on the basis that he was 55 or older. When he made the request, <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[19]" phrase="he was not yet 55" />. The Commission applied Full Bench authority that <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[20]" phrase="the relevant circumstance must, as a matter of fact, exist" /> when the request is made. His arguments about a continuous process and good faith were <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[21]" phrase="These are all relevant to questions of fairness, but they do not affect whether the Commission has power to deal with the dispute" />. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[25]" phrase="The application is dismissed" />.

A request outside s.65 doesn't oblige the employer to follow s.65A. It may still be worth handling the same way. The employee may make a valid request a month later, an enterprise agreement or policy may give a wider right, and a careless refusal can become evidence in a later general protections or discrimination claim.

## The 21-day response, and what has to happen before a refusal

The employer must give a **written response within 21 days** (s.65A(1)). The response must grant the request, set out an agreed alternative, or refuse it.

An employer may refuse **only if** (s.65A(3)):

1. it has **discussed** the request with the employee, **and** genuinely tried to reach agreement about changes to accommodate the employee's circumstances;
2. no agreement has been reached;
3. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[16]" phrase="the employer has had regard to the consequences of the refusal for the employee" />; and
4. the refusal is on **reasonable business grounds**.

### The order of the steps matters

*Chandler v Westpac Banking Corporation* shows why. The manager <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[18]" phrase="did not discuss the request with the applicant or genuinely try to reach agreement with the applicant" />. Discussions did happen later, after the employee escalated. The Commission held that the first two requirements <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[29]" phrase="should pre-date the refusal" />, and on the facts, <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[29]" phrase="Here the refusal came on 18 March 2025, and discussions occurred afterwards" />.

The Commission also applied Full Bench authority that the consequences step <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[30]" phrase="places a positive obligation on an employer to consider the consequences of a refusal on an employee" />. It should be discussed and recorded in the written reasons. Westpac's refusal letter didn't mention it.

Compare *Duncan v Coal & Allied Mining Services*, where the employer won. The Commission found the employer <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1427" at="[53]" phrase="had regard to the matters about which it was aware and that had a bearing on the likely consequences of its refusal" />.

**The practical sequence:** meet the employee, explore alternatives, write down what the refusal would mean for them, decide, and then write the response. A refusal letter that comes before the conversation can't be fixed afterwards.

### What the written refusal must contain

A refusal must include details of the reasons, <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[8]" phrase="including details of the reasons for refusal, the particular business grounds for refusing the request, and how those grounds apply to the request" />. It must also either set out other changes the employer would be willing to make, or say there are none, and it must explain the dispute and arbitration rights under ss.65B and 65C (s.65A(6)).

A template paragraph saying "operational requirements" is not "how those grounds apply to the request".

## Reasonable business grounds

Section 65A(5) gives a non-exhaustive list of reasonable business grounds:

- the new arrangements would be **too costly**;
- there is **no capacity** to change other employees' working arrangements to accommodate the request;
- it would be **impractical** to change other employees' arrangements, or to recruit new employees, to accommodate it;
- the new arrangements would be likely to cause a **significant loss in efficiency or productivity**; or
- they would be likely to have a **significant negative impact on customer service**.

The Commission assesses the grounds objectively. In *Aoyama v FLSA Holdings*: <Cite id="[2025] FWC 524" at="[42]" phrase="It is not the role of the Commission to stand in the shoes of the employer" />. Instead, the Commission looks at whether the grounds given are reasonable business grounds, and <Cite id="[2025] FWC 524" at="[42]" phrase="That requires an objective assessment of those reasons" />.

### Evidence, not assertion

The employers that lost in the decisions reviewed for this module mostly failed for lack of evidence:

- In *Aoyama*, the employer relied on unwritten "comments" from a customer and a supplier. The Commission found its summary of them <Cite id="[2025] FWC 524" at="[46]" phrase="is so imprecise as to be completely unhelpful" />. On the claimed impact on the business: <Cite id="[2025] FWC 524" at="[48]" phrase="Its case in this regard is nothing more than assertion" />. The employer also <Cite id="[2025] FWC 524" at="[50]" phrase="does not appear to have tailored its submissions to the nature of the change sought" />. The request was for one extra day at home a fortnight.
- In *Chandler*, the claimed benefits of office attendance were real but thin: <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[36]" phrase="the evidence as to the benefits was generalised" />, and <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[36]" phrase="The respondent made no attempt to quantify any supposed losses or negative impact" />. The Commission concluded <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[38]" phrase="the evidence here does not establish that there were reasonable business grounds for the refusal" />.

Detailed evidence is what wins these cases for employers. In *Duncan*, the request concerned how an operator worked a rotating day/night shift roster at a mine. The employer showed specific costs and production effects, and the Commission accepted that:

- <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1427" at="[92]" phrase="Recruiting new employees to accommodate the requests is impractical because the resultant labour need is for work on night shift only" />;
- <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1427" at="[92]" phrase="The potential for capacity constraints to emerge if other employees seek the same flexibility for the same or similar reasons is reasonably foreseeable" />; and
- <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1427" at="[92]" phrase="There are genuine safety concerns arising in connection with the adoption of a night shift only roster pattern" />.

**The adviser's question:** if this went to arbitration tomorrow, what document or witness would prove each ground? If the answer is "the manager's view", the ground is at risk.

## When the employee disputes a refusal: s.65B

A dispute can be referred to the Commission if the employer has refused the request, or if 21 days have passed without a written response (s.65B(1)). The steps are:

1. The parties must first try to resolve it **at the workplace level**, by discussion (s.65B(2)).
2. If that fails, either party may refer it to the Commission (s.65B(3)).
3. The Commission must first deal with it by means other than arbitration, usually conciliation, unless there are exceptional circumstances (s.65B(4)(a)).
4. It may then arbitrate under s.65C (s.65B(4)(b)).

Awards and enterprise agreements also contain dispute-resolution terms, and some agreements give their own flexibility rights. In *Zammit*, the Commission noted that <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[23]" phrase="a separate dispute about the issues raised by Mr Zammit can be brought under an enterprise agreement dispute settlement term" />, once that procedure had been followed. It would not treat a s.65B application as a different kind of application to get around its own limits. See [Module 4](/modules/agreements-and-policies).

## What the Commission can order: s.65C

On arbitration, the Commission can make these orders:

- if no written response was given, that the employer is **taken to have refused** the request;
- if the employer refused, that the grounds should be **taken to have been**, or **not to have been**, reasonable business grounds;
- that the employer take **further steps** if it has not responded, or not responded adequately; and
- that the employer **grant the request**, or make other specified changes to accommodate the employee's circumstances. The Commission can make this order <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[16]" phrase="only if satisfied that there is no reasonable prospect of the dispute being resolved without the making of such an order" />.

Two limits apply to every order:

- **Fairness between the parties** must be taken into account (s.65C(2)). Even when the grounds fail, <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[25]" phrase="There may, for example, be countervailing fairness reasons why another form of permissible order should be made" />. In *Duncan*, where the employer won: <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1427" at="[93]" phrase="Fairness between the parties is a neutral consideration in this case" />.
- **Consistency.** Orders <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[12]" phrase="must also not be inconsistent with the Act or a fair work instrument term" /> that applied to the parties.

Don't treat the consistency limit as a way to use an enterprise agreement against a request. *Chandler* sets out a Full Bench decision holding that an agreement term will exclude or displace the s.65 right <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[53]" status="flagged" phrase="if it would permit an employer to refuse a request for a change to working arrangements other than on reasonable business grounds" />. Under ss.56 and 61, such a term has no effect to that extent.

The orders are real. In *Aoyama*, the Commission found no reasonable business grounds and said <Cite id="[2025] FWC 524" at="[58]" phrase="I make an order that FLSA grant the request contained in Mr Aoyama's email" />. The order was limited to a fixed period. In *Duncan*, the order was that the refusals be <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1427" at="[96]" phrase="to be taken to have been on reasonable business grounds" />.

## Hybrid and return-to-office policies

A general attendance policy is lawful in many cases, and directions under it can be lawful and reasonable. But a policy does not answer an individual s.65 request. Each valid request still needs the s.65A(3) steps, applied to that employee's circumstances and that specific change. The question is whether **this** change, for **this** employee, would cause the identified problem, not whether the policy is a good idea in general.

A request is also often the first sign of a disability, caring or family violence issue. Handle it with the same care as the matters in [Module 29](/modules/illness-injury-and-leave), and avoid any step that could look like a response to the employee asking. See [Module 28](/modules/managing-an-employee-who-has-complained).

## What the Commission looks for

- **A valid request at the date it was made**, including the listed circumstance, service and form.
- **The s.65A(3) steps before the refusal**: discussion, a genuine attempt at agreement, and documented regard to the consequences for the employee.
- **Grounds tied to the actual change requested**, supported by numbers, rosters, customer evidence or safety analysis.
- **A written refusal that explains how the grounds apply**, sets out alternatives, and explains the dispute rights.

## How refusals fail, and when they hold

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail near">
<span class="tag">Point of principle</span>
<h3>Refused first, discussed later</h3>
<p>The refusal letter came before any discussion. The later conversations didn't fix that, and the business grounds weren't established on the evidence. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3115" at="[29]" phrase="should pre-date the refusal" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Hold the meeting, explore alternatives and record the consequences for the employee before anyone signs a refusal.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail">
<span class="tag">Employee won</span>
<h3>Vague customer "comments" for one day a fortnight</h3>
<p>The employer relied on undocumented remarks and general claims about responsiveness. The Commission ordered it to grant the request. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 524" at="[48]" phrase="Its case in this regard is nothing more than assertion" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Size the evidence to the change actually requested, and bring it.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>A roster case proved with numbers</h3>
<p>The employer showed the cost, production, recruitment and safety effects of the requested change, and had regard to the consequences for the employee. <Cite id="[2026] FWC 1427" at="[5]" phrase="I find that C&A MS has met its obligations under the Act in relation to the May and September requests" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Specific evidence of operational effects, set out against the s.65A(5) grounds, is what holds up.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Respondent won</span>
<h3>The circumstance didn't exist yet</h3>
<p>The employee relied on being 55 or older but made the request a month before his 55th birthday. The Commission had no jurisdiction to arbitrate. <Cite id="[2025] FWC 3079" at="[19]" phrase="he was not yet 55" /></p>
<p class="lesson">Check validity at the date of the request. Then consider whether to treat a request outside s.65 the same way anyway.</p>
</div>
</div>

## Practice scenario: the three-day office rule

<Scenario questions={["Is this a valid s.65 request?", "What's wrong with the draft refusal?", "What evidence would you need to support refusing?", "What outcome might you propose instead?"]}>
  <Fragment slot="facts">
    <p>Mei has worked as a payroll officer for four years. The company recently introduced a rule that all head-office staff attend three days a week. Mei's younger child starts school next year. She emails HR asking to attend one day a week and work from home on the other four days, so she can do school drop-offs and pick-ups. She explains that she worked mostly from home for two years without complaint.</p>
    <p>Her manager has drafted a reply, without speaking to her: "Your request is refused due to operational requirements and the company's commitment to in-office collaboration. The three-day attendance policy applies to all staff."</p>
  </Fragment>
  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Very likely valid.</strong> She is a parent of a child of school age or younger, has more than 12 months' service, and has made a written request explaining the change and why. Check that the request was made after the circumstance applied, not in anticipation of it.</li>
      <li><strong>The draft refuses before any discussion,</strong> says nothing about the consequences for Mei, gives a general policy reason rather than explaining how any ground applies to her request, offers no alternative, and doesn't explain the dispute rights. On *Chandler*, a later conversation won't repair that sequence.</li>
      <li><strong>Evidence:</strong> what payroll work actually has to be done on site, and how often; any documented problems during her two years at home; and the cost or service impact of her proposed arrangement compared with the alternatives. "Collaboration" on its own is the kind of generalised benefit that failed in *Chandler*.</li>
      <li><strong>A possible alternative:</strong> meet with her first and explore options. For example, two office days fixed around the school timetable, or different start and finish times on office days. Record her response and the reasons for whatever is decided.</li>
    </ol>
  </Fragment>
</Scenario>
