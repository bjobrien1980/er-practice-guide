<p class="lead">An employee who is sick, injured or on leave is protected by three different general protections at once. Each asks a different question, and passing one does not mean passing the others. This module covers the temporary absence rule, disability discrimination, and leave and compensation claims as workplace rights, and how an employer can lawfully act on long absence or incapacity.</p>

<div class="notice">
<strong>Current-source gate:</strong> the current text of ss.340, 351 and 352 of the Fair Work Act and regulation 3.01 of the Fair Work Regulations was checked on 14 November 2026. State and Territory workers' compensation and anti-discrimination laws have their own protections and are not covered. The Federal Court decisions cited here were read from the judgment files held in the Briefer corpus, not from the Court's website — see the note under Decisions cited. Unfair dismissal for incapacity is in Module 14.
</div>

## What you'll be able to do

- Work out whether an absence is protected by s.352, using the rules in the regulation.
- Explain why the end of s.352 protection is not permission to dismiss.
- Tell a decision made because of incapacity from one made because of disability.
- Decide what medical evidence is needed before acting, and what to do if the employee will not provide it.
- Recognise when taking leave or claiming compensation is a workplace right.

## Three protections, three questions

| Provision | What it protects | The question |
|---|---|---|
| s.352 | Temporary absence because of illness or injury | Was the employee dismissed because of the absence, and was the absence of a prescribed kind? |
| s.351 | Physical or mental disability (among other attributes) | Was action taken because of the disability, and if so, was it because of the inherent requirements of the position? |
| s.340 | Workplace rights, including leave entitlements and compensation claims | Was action taken because the employee had or used the entitlement? |

Section 352 applies only to dismissal. Sections 351 and 340 apply to any adverse action (Module 26). The same reverse onus applies to all three once the employee proves the protected circumstance (Module 27). On s.352 the Full Court has said so directly: <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 204" at="[128]" phrase="Section 360 and the statutory presumption in s 361 apply in relation to conduct in contravention of s 352 in the same way" />.

## Temporary absence: s.352

### What the regulation requires

An employer must not dismiss an employee because the employee is temporarily absent from work because of illness or injury "of a kind prescribed by the regulations". Regulation 3.01 does the prescribing. It is set out in full in <em>Southern Migrant and Refugee Centre v Shum (No 3)</em>.

Under the current reg.3.01, an absence is protected if the employee provides the required medical certificate or statutory declaration within <Cite id="[2022] FCA 481" at="[121]" phrase="24 hours after the commencement of the absence" /> or a longer period that is reasonable, or complies with the notice and evidence terms of a workplace instrument, or has given the evidence required for paid personal leave. Check the current Regulation directly; <em>Shum</em> is authority for the interpretation of the rule, not a substitute for the current text.

An absence is not protected if two things are both true:

1. <Cite id="[2022] FCA 481" at="[121]" phrase="the employee's absence extends for more than 3 months" />, or <Cite id="[2022] FCA 481" at="[121]" phrase="the total absences of the employee, within a 12 month period, have been more than 3 months" />; and
2. <Cite id="[2022] FCA 481" at="[121]" phrase="the employee is not on paid personal/carer's leave" /> for the whole of the absence.

Time on workers' compensation does not count as paid personal leave for this purpose: the regulation says paid leave <Cite id="[2022] FCA 481" at="[121]" phrase="does not include a period when the employee is absent from work while receiving compensation" />.

So, subject to the current regulation's conditions, an employee who remains on paid personal leave for the entire absence may stay protected however long it runs. An employee who has moved to unpaid leave, annual leave or workers' compensation may lose s.352 protection after three months. Losing s.352 protection does not answer the separate s.340, s.351, workers' compensation or unfair-dismissal questions.

### The employee must prove the absence qualifies

In <em>Shum</em>, an employee was dismissed after a long absence. <Cite id="[2022] FCA 481" at="[119]" phrase="She had been absent from work for a period of 105 days" />, partly on unpaid leave. The trial judge found the absence was part of the reason for dismissal and that s.352 was breached. Snaden J set that aside. Before the presumption could help her, <Cite id="[2022] FCA 481" at="[124]" phrase="it was necessary that Ms Shum first establish that she could satisfy the factual circumstance" /> that triggers s.352. She could not, because her absence was over three months and not all on paid personal leave. The result: <Cite id="[2022] FCA 481" at="[126]" phrase="Even assuming that Ms Shum was dismissed because of her absence, dismissal for that reason could not qualify as adverse action effected in breach of s 352" />.

### Two more limits

**The employee must have been absent when the decision was made.** In <em>Leung v Omnia Inclusive Employment Solutions</em>, the employee said she was on certified leave at the time of termination. The claim failed because <Cite id="[2026] FCA 606" at="[108]" phrase="the termination decision was made on 18 August 2022 prior to her absence from work" />. An employer relying on this needs a record that fixes the date of the decision.

**A finding that absence "may have been" a reason is not enough.** In <em>Cummins South Pacific v Keenan</em>, the trial judge's s.352 finding was overturned because <Cite id="[2020] FCAFC 204" at="[134]" phrase="all that his Honour found was that Mr Keenan's absence may have been a reason for his termination" />.

<strong>Three months is not a licence</strong>
<ol class="principles">
<li>When s.352 stops applying, only s.352 stops applying.</li>
<li>If the illness or injury is a disability, s.351 still applies, with no time limit.</li>
<li>If the employee has used a leave entitlement or made a compensation claim, s.340 still applies.</li>
<li>Unfair dismissal still applies (Module 14), and so do State compensation and discrimination laws.</li>
</ol>

## Disability: s.351

Most long illnesses and injuries are, or may be, a physical or mental disability. The hard question is whether action taken because of what the condition does to the person's work is action taken because of the disability.

### The disability and its consequences

Two decisions mark out the ground.

In <em>Shizas v Commissioner of Police</em>, an applicant with a spinal condition was refused a place in police training. The employer argued the refusal was about the applicant's physical limitations, not the diagnosis. Katzmann J rejected the distinction: <Cite id="[2017] FCA 61" at="[122]" phrase="It would be as artificial as saying that a person was refused employment not because he was a paraplegic, but because he had no control over his legs" />. The medical evidence mattered. The applicant had no symptoms. <Cite id="[2017] FCA 61" at="[126]" phrase="All the medical evidence indicated that Mr Shizas was physically fit and asymptomatic" />. The only basis for thinking he could not do the job was the diagnosis itself.

In <em>Western Union Business Solutions v Robinson</em>, an employee had been off work for about seven months with certificates referring to stress and depression. The employer asked him to see its nominated doctor. He queried the request and did not confirm any of the appointments offered. The manager dismissed him for two reasons: failure to cooperate with the medical assessment, and concern about his capacity to return. Her evidence was that she was not sure he was genuinely unwell, and that if he was, <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[12]" phrase="that state of affairs would continue for the indefinite future" />.

The trial judge held that incapacity was a manifestation of the disability and could not be separated from it, so the dismissal was because of disability. The Full Court allowed the employer's appeal. O'Callaghan and Thawley JJ said: <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[137]" phrase="The question is what the disability is, which does not necessarily equate to what the disability causes" />. And: <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[138]" phrase="The present legislative context does not require the conclusion that, if a disability has an effect on capacity for work, that effect must be part of the disability" />.

What decided the case was the manager's actual reasoning, which the trial judge had accepted as honest. She <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[141]" phrase="was not sure one way or the other whether Mr Robinson was genuinely unwell" />. Her concern was that he <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[141]" phrase="could not fulfil any of the requirements of his position for the reasonably foreseeable future" />. So <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[153]" phrase="the underlying causes of that incapacity played no role in Ms Pickles' decision" />.

### Reading the two together

- **In <em>Robinson</em>, the fact acted on was real and present:** seven months of absence, no return date, and no cooperation with an assessment. The cause did not matter to the decision-maker.
- **In <em>Shizas</em>, the fact acted on was assumed from the diagnosis:** the applicant was fit, and the limitation was a prediction about people with his condition.

<em>Totino v Metro Trains Melbourne</em>, discussed in Modules 26 and 27, is on the <em>Robinson</em> side. The employee had been <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[42]" phrase="absent due to medical incapacity for a period of over three months" />. The two decision-makers proved that their only reasons were <Cite id="[2026] FCAFC 120" at="[60]" phrase="Ms Totino's medical incapacity, and the need to fill her position permanently" />.

The practical rule: act on what the person can and cannot do, shown by current medical evidence about that person, and on what the business needs. Do not act on the name of the condition, on assumptions about it, or on how colleagues feel about it. And do not treat <em>Robinson</em> as a formula. It was decided on the evidence of one decision-maker who was believed. A manager whose emails show impatience with an employee's mental illness will not be believed when they say only capacity mattered.

### Inherent requirements

Even if action is taken because of disability, s.351 does not apply to action taken because of the inherent requirements of the particular position. The employer has to prove it. In <em>Shizas</em>, <Cite id="[2017] FCA 61" at="[128]" phrase="the burden is on the employer to prove that adverse action was taken because of the inherent requirements of" /> the position.

The exception failed for the first refusal in <em>Shizas</em>. The court declared that decision a contravention <Cite id="[2017] FCA 61" at="[140]" phrase="the Commissioner contravened s 351(1) of the FW Act by refusing to employ Mr Shizas in March 2013" />, and granted no other relief.

To rely on inherent requirements, an employer needs three things recorded before the decision: what the requirements of this position are, medical evidence about whether this person can meet them, and the decision-maker's own reasoning linking the two. A list of requirements written after the event does not show what the decision-maker was thinking.

## Getting medical evidence

An employer deciding about capacity needs up-to-date medical information. Asking for it is usually reasonable. How it is asked for can itself be challenged.

- In <em>Robinson</em>, the joint judgment described the employer's requests as <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[141]" phrase="reasonable and lawful requests to attend an independent medical assessment" />, and the employee's failure to cooperate was a lawful reason for dismissal.
- In <em>Zabortseva v Commonwealth</em>, a direction to a public servant to attend a medical examination <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1585" at="[199]" phrase="was not an adverse action on the part of the respondent, but a lawful and reasonable direction" />. That direction was made under public service regulations, so the case is of limited help to a private employer.
- In <em>Avard v Australian Capital Territory</em>, discussed in Module 26, a similar direction was held not to be adverse action on its facts.

A private employer's power to direct an employee to an independent examination depends on the contract, any enterprise agreement or policy, and whether the direction is reasonable in the circumstances. Check the source of the power before giving the direction. Then:

1. Say in writing why the information is needed and what decision it will inform.
2. Give the doctor the duties of the position and ask specific questions about capacity and timing.
3. Offer the employee a choice of times and a chance to provide their own specialist's report.
4. If the employee does not cooperate, say so in writing and say what will follow. <em>Robinson</em> turned on a paper trail of three offered appointments.

## Leave as a workplace right

An entitlement to personal or carer's leave under the National Employment Standards or an agreement is a benefit under a workplace law or instrument (Module 25). Taking it, or asking to take it, is the exercise of a workplace right. So is making a workers' compensation claim, where the compensation law is a workplace law; check the definition against the State law concerned.

### Attendance and leave

Can an employer act on a poor attendance record when the absences were all legitimate leave? The Full Court in <em>Robinson</em> summarised an earlier Full Court decision, <em>CFMEU v Endeavour Coal</em>, on that point. An employee was moved to a different roster after 15 absences in under three years, nearly all within his leave entitlements. The manager's evidence was that <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[147]" phrase="the reasons for Mr McDermott's absences did not matter to him, rather it was the fact of those absences that mattered" />. The trial judge accepted that <Cite id="[2019] FCAFC 181" at="[148]" phrase="it was the lack of predictability in Mr McDermott's attendance which was the reason for the decision to move him from the weekend day shift, not the fact that he had previously exercised his rights to personal/carer's leave" />. A majority of the Full Court upheld that result.

This is a narrow win and should be used with care. It depended on a finding about one manager's state of mind. One of the judges quoted in <em>Robinson</em> noted there was material that would have justified a different conclusion. An absence management process that counts protected leave will always invite the claim. If attendance is the concern, record the operational reason (why predictable attendance matters in this role) and apply the same process to every employee with a similar record.

### What the entitlement covers

A claim based on a leave entitlement fails if the leave was not within the entitlement. In <em>Talaugon v Allight</em>, an employee said three refusals of carer's leave breached the National Employment Standards. One absence was to care for a friend after surgery. Feutrill J held that <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1672" at="[49]" phrase="caring for a friend falls outside the scope of s 97" />, which covers immediate family and household members.

<em>Talaugon</em> is also an example of an employer winning on difficult timing. The employee was pregnant and a carer, and had negotiated a flexible working arrangement. <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1672" at="[1]" phrase="Shortly after that agreement was reached, Allight began taking steps to place Ms Talaugon on a performance improvement plan" />, and she was later dismissed. The decision-maker gave direct evidence of his reasons and was believed: <Cite id="[2025] FCA 1672" at="[133]" phrase="Allight has discharged its onus of proof" />.

### Compensation claims

Do not discourage a claim. In <em>SBP Employment Solutions v Smith</em>, the employee was injured in a car accident, started a workers' compensation claim and dropped it, in the judge's words <Cite id="[2021] FCA 601" at="[16]" phrase="because he felt pressured by the Directors not to proceed" />. That history formed part of a case the employer lost (Module 27). State compensation laws commonly restrict dismissal of injured workers for a set period and impose return-to-work duties. Those rules are separate from the Fair Work Act and must be checked for the State concerned.

## How these cases are won and lost

<div class="fails">
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>105 days absent, partly on unpaid leave</h3>

<p class="lesson">Count the days and check what kind of leave the employee was on for each of them.</p>
</div>
<div class="fail won">
<span class="tag">Employer won</span>
<h3>Seven months off, no cooperation with a medical assessment</h3>

    <p>Helen says: "It's been more than three months, so he's not protected any more. And honestly, with his mental health I can't see him ever coping with payroll deadlines again."</p>

  <Fragment slot="answer">
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Probably not, on these dates, but check.</strong> The absence is about four months and Owen has been on unpaid leave since 18 March, so he has not been on paid personal leave for the whole absence. On the regulation as quoted in <em>Shum</em>, that takes the absence outside s.352. Confirm the day count, the leave records and the current text of the regulation. If the compensation claim is accepted and back-paid, that period still does not count as paid personal leave.</li>
      <li><strong>Not on what Helen has said.</strong> Her first sentence treats three months as a licence, which it is not. Her second is the problem. "With his mental health I can't see him ever coping" is a prediction drawn from the diagnosis, which is the <em>Shizas</em> side of the line, and she would be the witness. The firm has no medical evidence about Owen's capacity or prognosis beyond short certificates. A lawful decision would rest on current medical evidence about whether and when Owen can return to this role, on the requirements of the role, and on a documented operational need to fill it permanently, as in <em>Totino</em>.</li>
      <li><strong>It adds two risks.</strong> Lodging the claim is likely to be a workplace right, so dismissal soon after it invites a s.340 claim. And the claim blames Owen's manager. If Helen is that manager, she should not be the decision-maker (Module 28).       <li><strong>Get evidence and change the decision-maker.</strong> Write to Owen explaining that the firm needs current information about his capacity and likely return, and why. Invite a report from his treating specialist addressing the payroll role's duties, or, if the firm has the power, arrange an independent assessment with a choice of dates. Identify a decision-maker who is not the subject of the claim. Record the operational case for a permanent appointment. Only then consider a show-cause letter of the kind described in Module 26.</li>
    </ol>
